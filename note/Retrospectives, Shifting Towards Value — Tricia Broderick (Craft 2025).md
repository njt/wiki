# Retrospectives, Shifting Towards Value — Tricia Broderick (Craft 2025)

Tricia Broderick's day-two essentials talk at Craft 2025 (a ytx gist carrying a structured summary plus full transcript; the venue is inferred from "my keynote yesterday" and a change-pacing callback that matches her Building Resilience keynote the day before). The origin story is a retro that produced "What did we do? Well, communicate. What are we going to do? More communicate." — retrospectives have decayed into ritual, and the talk rebuilds them around the only thing that justifies the meeting: the value the team is trying to deliver. Walking the Larsen/Derby five-stage loop (now V2 with David Horowitz), it becomes a facilitation clinic — focus the topic, declare the decision rule upfront and start with majority, manufacture collaboration before conflict, never skip Generate Insights, keep the insight off people's names, treat anonymous voting as a symptom of team dysfunction, and close on next steps and reinforcement, with depth earned iteratively rather than demanded on day one.

---

## Key quotes

> "What did we do? Well, communicate. What are we going to do? More communicate."

The retro that prompted the talk, and her verdict on it: "It's like we're going through the motions. It's not valuable, it's not helpful to anybody. And it's painful." She traces the decay back to "lessons learned" and — her "favorite wording" — "post mortems. The after death meeting... who can we blame today?", where you reflect only once the project is dead and then "move along."

> "For those that do like Scrum, I actually hate the concept of the entire sprint as the focus. How stupid."

Focus discipline. The whole-sprint retro is her scapegoat for every generic action item ever written; the fix is a narrower topic ("communication with our stakeholders") plus an explicit output budget — "I want one or two highlights... one or two improvements" — because she once watched a retro emit 20 action items for four weeks "and then nobody did anything."

> "Notice how I made it your problem that we're focused on in the retro, not me as the leader coming in going, we're going to do a retro to improve you. That's not motivating."

The motivation mechanic of Set the Stage: gather the team's own words about what hurts, then offer to diminish some of it. The facilitator supplies the frame; the team supplies the pain.

> "The insight is never a person's name."

Her non-negotiable ground rule for Generate Insights: "It could be a behavior, it could be an issue, but the system always contributes." Anything that needs to be about a person happens "outside of your retro."

> "The only way we can make a decision is if we don't talk to each other... You don't have a high performing team. You don't have a resilient learning team."

On anonymous voting: not a technique but a confession — evidence the team can't handle open disagreement, i.e., that stage three never actually happened. She concedes the fallback exists ("are there times that you may still need to do the anonymous approach? Absolutely") but insists "it doesn't promote resilient learning or teamwork in general."

> "If you have to repeat what you did for the day, it stunk."

The Close is not a summary. It is next steps — someone owns taking the action items back — plus positive reinforcement of the collaborate-and-conflict behavior itself: the deposit that buys "the bigger elephant" at the next retro.

> "I know you will not fool me again. I am never speaking in a retro ever again."

A developer whose previous team lead weaponized retro content and reported it to managers. Her response: "Can I buy you a drink? Because that's just horrific." The episode is the talk's strongest argument that safety is contextual and earned, never assumed — and it is why the right to pass exists.

## Key themes

- #concept — The retro as half of a loop whose other half is the product: reflection exists to reinforce value delivery, not calendar hygiene ("You're not doing a retro because it's the end of the month") and not bonding ("trust falls"). Generate Insights is the load-bearing stage: skip it and "who's ever the most senior person in the room says I think we should do this and everybody goes, okay."
- #pattern — Engineered conflict: disagreement is not an accident to be tolerated but the mechanism. Sort by impact, not preference ("Does anybody disagree that that's high impact?") — two people can like the same sessions while one came for the networking, and only the impact argument surfaces the difference. The stage's exit signal is options narrowing from about seven to two or three.
- #pattern — Collaboration before conflict: the 1-2-4-All variation (solo → pairs → fours, one item per level) buys thinking time, gives half-formed ideas safety, forces convergence, and pre-loads collaboration so the hard part isn't "my idea versus yours."
- #tool — The kit: the "which duck are you today?" vibe check; a standing "what should our retro focus on?" board to defuse facilitator bias; a decision-rule declaration (start with majority, not consensus); numbered pre-committed voting against dot-voting's anchoring and bandwagoning; the plush baby elephant for unnamed topics; the right to pass; Hopes and Wishes (Larsen) as the appreciation close; the 30-minute first retro; Definition of Ready as a control-circle move; topic-trigger cadence for non-sprint teams; and treating hybrid as remote.
- #pattern — Depth is earned iteratively: psychological safety is not a first-retro topic ("the elephant will be too big"); topics must be in the team's control; small recurring wins build the tipping point that eventually converts the holdouts — and those who never come around "probably got other problems with them to begin with."
- #person — Tricia Broderick (Ignite Insight + Innovation; co-author, *Lead Without Blame*); Diana Larsen and Esther Derby (the framework's originators; Larsen's Hopes and Wishes); David Horowitz (Agile Retrospectives V2); Jake (the colleague behind the baby elephant); John (the holdout converted by the right to pass).

## Opinionated take

The falsifiable, immediately checkable claims are the talk's real payload: skipping stage three collapses decisions into hierarchy; the whole-sprint focus manufactures "communicate more"; anonymity is a symptom, not a tool; and the decision rule must be declared upfront because participants who discover mid-meeting that they never had a voice don't come back. Each can be tested on a real team within a sprint, which is more than most process talks offer. The subtlest piece is Gather Data as collaboration pre-loading: most retros don't fail at insight, they fail by arriving at insight as opponents.

But the talk shares its keynote's structural asymmetry — systemic diagnosis, individual cure. The 20-action-items anecdote names an organizational failure mode; the cure offered is "narrow it." The weaponized-retro story names a power abuse by a team lead; the remedy is a vague "make it safe in different ways." The gist's own digest is right that "value" — the titular pole of her loop — is never defined, measured, or followed up: nothing in the method asks what became of retro N's actions by retro N+1, which is exactly where most retros die. And "start with majority" is prescribed without addressing the serially overridden minority whose items never win — the precise erosion of participation the talk is trying to build. Consensus gridlock is real, but so is majority fatigue, and she only prices one of them.

There is also no counterargument to retros at all, in a room that self-reported being tired of them: the implicit answer is "do them better," never "when shouldn't you run one," and no evidence that a well-run retro outperforms the alternatives for a given team. Snowden's same-conference warning that Agile "borrowed complexity's language without its practice" cuts both ways here — this talk is the practice, but it still assumes the practice is worth doing.

One wiki-era observation, extrapolated and not hers: this is the human counterpart of the loops this wiki tracks for agents. The "compound" step of a [[Compound Engineering Plugin]]-style loop is a retro for a fleet, and her insistence that reflection exist to reinforce value delivery — never as ceremony — is the same argument the eval-driven-agent crowd makes about harnesses. Her unanswered question, how you would know a retro actually produced value, is the same measurement gap the agent world hasn't closed either.

## Relations

- [[Building Resilience — Tricia Broderick (Craft 2025)]] — the keynote this talk followed by a day. Strengthens: the retro talk operationalizes the keynote's individuals→groups→teams spectrum, gating the biggest topics (psychological safety) on the team "actually collaborating and not just cooperating." Complicates: the keynote diagnosed organizations optimized for group behavior; the day-two answer is a facilitator's toolkit — again a systemic cause met with an individual-level craft.
- [[Crew Resource Management — Joseph Pelrine (Craft 2025)]] — Pelrine inverts the psychological-safety dogma (safety emerges from ground rules that build trust; it is not a prerequisite), and Broderick agrees in practice — safety-first retros fail because the elephant is too big. But where Pelrine prescribes fixed ground rules that let technical skill survive stress, Broderick deliberately engineers conflict inside the meeting: one Craft 2025 talk minimizes avoidable conflict, the other schedules the necessary kind.
- [[Shaped by Demand — The Power of Fluid Teams]] — North demolishes the stable team as the unit of planning; Broderick's whole method presupposes one. Iterated retros, tipping points, and depth earned over months only work for a team that persists long enough to accumulate the learning — fluid self-assembly quietly deletes the reflection loop, or at least makes whoever re-forms the team own it.
- [[Two Languages — Complexity Bounds Systems Thinking]] — Snowden's charge that Agile "borrowed complexity's language without its practice" and his warning about "retrospective coherence" bracket this talk: it is the practice-side counterexample, a designed reflection loop with staged depth — while his concept names the failure her method never guards against, the retro that converges on a comfortable narrative instead of an uncomfortable insight.
