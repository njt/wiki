# Nobody Is Ready for the AI-Agent Flood

John Herrman reports on the arrival of consumer personal agents — Meta's Muse, Instinct, OpenAI's Dots — that call businesses, book tables, and fill in government forms on behalf of their owners, and on the unconsulted recipients: plumbers, karaoke bars, municipal booking platforms, state comptrollers. His framing is the flood: agents are treating every human interface as an exploitable surface, frictions that acted as de facto rate limits are dissolving, and the people bearing the cost never asked for any of it.

---

The column's most vivid material is ethnographic. Muse calls small businesses, discloses that it is transcribing, and asks for quotes. One employee says "Are you AI? I need the real person"; a plumber replies with an obscene counter-question; a restaurateur says flatly: "We don't like AI calling our restaurant. We hate it." Herrman himself becomes the odd one out — a cold-calling human in a world of cold-calling bots.

> "Agentic flooding": what happens "when frictions that were de facto rate limits disappear."

This is the article's analytical core, borrowed from researchers (lead: Chris Schmitz) who studied 84 suspected AI-driven spikes in government services — FOIA requests, public-comment processes, benefit enrollments. In some contexts automation is "massively good" (reaching people the administrative burden excluded); in others it's a "tragedy of the commons." The grim forecast is that friction-rebuilding is cheap, has precedent, and is "very attractive on short notice" — meaning agents may end up fighting defenses designed specifically against them.

> Muse "has all the time and compute in the world. What's a couple more forms to one of the world's largest data centers?"

Herrman's own experiment is the best evidence in the piece: Muse tried to file three unclaimed-property claims, two of them belonging to other people, and was happy to submit all three and let the state sort it out. This is the agent economy in miniature — the cost of a wrong action is borne by someone other than the agent's owner, so the agent errs at machine volume.

> "Once everyone has, as Mark Zuckerberg describes it, an 'exceptionally capable personal agent,' does anyone?"

The piece's thesis in one line. Personal-agent abundance is a positional good that self-destructs: if everyone has a reservation bot, the reservation is scarcer than ever, and the winners are whoever deploys first. Rec's Kadakia proposes agent "passports" and randomized booking lotteries; Spot's Larsen wants to "give everybody one and see what happens" — effectively accelerando as a business model, layering a "dining concierge" over the platforms it snipes.

## Key themes

- #concept — **Agentic flooding**: infinite cheap submissions collapse frictions that were serving as implicit rate limits.
- #pattern — **Externality shifting**: the agent's owner gets convenience; the portal, small business, or public-comment channel absorbs the cost.
- #concept — **Agent identity**: proposals to tie agents to one human identity ("agentic passport") as the precondition for rate-limiting them.
- #tool — The emerging counter-industry: AI-call detection, verification gates, "agent concierges."

## Opinion

Herrman is strongest when he refuses both techno-optimism and reflexive doom: the flood is genuinely good for people shut out by administrative burden, and genuinely destructive for everyone who runs a channel that assumed human-scale traffic. What he underplays is that the "designed barriers" point cuts both ways — a phone-attrition machine at an insurance company is itself friction deployed against humans, so agents attack it from *both* sides: consumers send agents through hold queues while companies deploy agents to answer them. The endgame isn't agent-versus-human at all; it's agents on both ends of every interface, with humans joining only when the two agent stacks fail to interoperate. That makes the "agent passport" question — identity, not intelligence — the actual load-bearing problem, and it's the one nobody in the piece can define yet.

The other weak spot: Kadakia's lottery-and-coordination future is sketched sympathetically, but a randomized three-minute lottery is just a rate limiter with better PR. Coordination between booking agents presupposes exactly the shared identity and protocol infrastructure that no industry body has agreed on.
