# We Need Smart People Who Give a Shit

Violet Pixel's essay argues that complex systems — ACH, M365, air traffic control — are held together by people with intimate knowledge of the system and the drive to make it work, and that generative AI has neither quality. Replacing those people, or hollowing them out into LLM babysitters, is the mechanism by which fragile systems finally break.

---

The essay opens with a working-life lesson: the world is held together by "smart people who give a shit," a truth visible whenever one learns how a complex system barely functions. ACH is the exemplar — old, riddled with flaws, legacy-encumbered — and yet works, because people who care about it fix, patch, bypass, and manually handle every problem. The two ingredients are mutually reinforcing: you must care to learn a system deeply, and knowing it deeply makes you care more.

The pivot is labour economics. Capitalism has always accommodated these people grudgingly, out of necessity. Now leadership believes generative AI can supply the same value from a machine that is cheaper, tireless, and never pushes back. To anyone not in the trenches, "it's all just output" — so the substitution looks free. The author rejects this: the model's intelligence is "an illusion propped up by a massive amount of data strip-mined from the commons," and its apparent care is a design goal of pleasing the user, not of the system working. Smart people notice things, handle edge cases, know when something is about to go off the rails — "more prompting and more output isn't a substitute for this."

The mechanism is the essay's sharpest part: it isn't only that caring people are laid off, it's that the survivors are hollowed out. Re-tasking someone to babysit LLMs does the same job deskillingly — every prompt moves them further from the system they knew, every generated output merged into the system makes them understand less, and the theft is of exactly what made them smart (the struggle) and what made them care (ownership). Recent outages across Microsoft 365, air traffic control, United, the NSA, banks, and Apple are read as early evidence — admittedly without inside information, and the author flags it.

---

## Key Quotes

> "Those two things feed into each other. You have to care about a complex system to dive deep enough to learn how it works, and when you know a lot about how a complex system works you care more about it."

The care-knowledge loop is the essay's actual thesis, and it's a good one: it explains why maintenance expertise is hard to buy and impossible to generate on demand. It also predicts that destroying the loop runs in both directions — lose the care and you lose the learning, and vice versa.

> "It matters because generative AI isn't smart, and it doesn't give a shit. … What seems like care, concern, and attention is just generative AI doing what it's designed to do: telling you what you want to hear."

Bluntly stated, and roughly the position held by several sources in this wiki: models optimise for what looks right to the reader, not for the health of the system. The essay is careful to ground the claim not in model inadequacy alone but in the observer problem — only people who do the work can tell care from slop.

> "Every prompt moves them one step further away from the system they used to know inside and out. Every generated output that becomes part of the system means those people understand less about how the system actually works."

This is the strongest passage, because it names a compounding process rather than a static comparison. It is the same debt the wiki tracks under [[Cognitive Debt]] and comprehension debt — but stated causally, from the employer's side, as a business decision that manufactures the debt.

> "Capitalism was told the stove was hot, but their hand is on it right now. Unfortunately, we're all getting burned."

The closing metaphor is polemic, not argument — the essay offers no evidence that the outages it lists share this cause beyond plausibility and mood. It's an honest enough caveat ("I don't have inside information") but the reader should hold the causal claim loosely while keeping the mechanism.

## Analysis

The essay is a mood piece with a real mechanism inside it. The weak part is the headlines: attributing a week of unrelated outages to an industry-wide skills decline is unfalsifiable armchair diagnosis, and the author knows it. The strong part is the two-ingredient model — intimate knowledge plus the drive to make things work — and the observation that AI adoption as actually practised (layoffs plus babysitting) attacks both simultaneously.

It also lands in a precise spot in this wiki's argument map. The sources that celebrate autonomous software factories mostly assume someone retains deep understanding of the system being produced; this essay is the claim that the labour strategy of the moment is systematically eliminating that someone. It strengthens the sceptical side of the [[Cognitive Debt]] ledger with a concrete social mechanism, and it gives the sharpest statement yet of why [[The Human-in-the-Loop Fallacy (Choudary)]]'s warning about self-liquidating roles cuts both ways: the roles may be self-liquidating, but what liquidates with them is the care the systems need.

Worth noting the countervailing evidence the essay ignores: some field reports in this wiki describe the opposite dynamic — engineers who go deeper on systems *because* agents let them touch more of the system, and verification-first practices that make understanding legible. The truth is likely that AI destroys the care-knowledge loop when used as a labour substitution and reinforces it when used as a leverage; the essay describes the substitution case with unusual clarity, and never considers the leverage case.

## Related

This source strengthens [[Cognitive Debt]] by naming the organisational mechanism — layoff plus LLM babysitting — that manufactures comprehension debt at scale, from the employer's side rather than the practitioner's. It complicates [[The Human-in-the-Loop Fallacy (Choudary)]]: Choudary argues human-in-the-loop roles organise firms around a shrinking residue and self-liquidate; this essay answers that what self-liquidates with them is the system-level care those firms can't buy back. It nuances [[Human-in-the-Loop is Tired]] — Summers describes the exhaustion of the supervising human; this essay describes the organisational decision that creates that exhaustion and then removes the humans anyway. And it echoes [[The Joy and Power of Understanding]]'s claim that struggle is necessary for mastery, extended from the individual to the civilisational scale: a system maintained by people who no longer struggle with it is a system nobody understands.
