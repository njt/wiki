# What the Cheesecake Factory Teaches About Software Factories

Arcolano uses The Cheesecake Factory's 250-item menu as a metaphor for what coding agents did to software: a small set of primitives remixed into combinatorial abundance, where the "kitchen" absorbs the cost of complexity but the customer pays the cost of choosing — and organizations, raised on prix fixe roadmaps, have never practiced choosing from an infinite menu.

---

The essay's structure is a restaurant essay that turns into a strategy essay. Part one explains the 250-item menu as rational business: costs are modest (combinatorics over shared ingredients, plus a kitchen with process discipline), perceived value is huge ("look at all the choices!"), and being the safe default for group dinners is good positioning. The catch is that the cost of choice overload — analysis paralysis, heuristics — lands on the customer, invisibly.

Part two maps this onto software. Coding agents took a small primitive set (text generation, code writing, search, computer use) and remixed it the way restaurants remix pasta, bread, and cheese. A software factory is the kitchen: agents set up with the right environments and process to cook anything off an enormous menu, reliably and at scale. But the rallying cry "be more ambitious" ignores who picks up the tab — the cost of choosing lands on your organization.

The strongest section is the diagnosis of *why* organizations cope badly. They've spent their lives on a prix fixe menu curated by constraints — customers, TAM, tech stack, budget, hiring, and "this is what we do here." That habituation is what makes focus possible (and what disruptors exploit). Now the constraints come off and the one-pager is swapped for the Cheesecake Factory tome, and companies cope exactly like diners: shrink the menu (AI for what we already do), choose a default (same roadmap, faster), follow the crowd (another in-app chatbot).

> "The real skill is choosing ambitiously *and* decisively. Ambitious means reading the whole menu instead of defaulting to the usual. Decisive means committing once you have. The new failure mode isn't just ordering something boring, it's also ordering one of everything and never finishing anything."

That last clause is the essay's best move: it names the failure mode that the ambition-evangelists don't. Everything-shiny paralysis is as much a response to abundance as timidity is.

> "Unlike at The Factory, nobody has actually handed you a menu. The space of things your company could build now exists, but it hasn't been written down anywhere... that work has to be somebody's job. I'm willing to bet that at your organization, it isn't anyone's."

This is the actionable core: menu enumeration as a named role. The concrete suggestion — re-price what you took off the menu in your 2026 planning at today's costs — is a genuinely usable ritual, not just a metaphor.

## Opinionated take

The metaphor earns its length. Most "be more ambitious" essays stop at the exhortation; this one explains the mechanism blocking it — a *skill* deficit in organizational choice, not a motivation deficit — which is more useful and more damning. It also quietly reframes the software-factory literature: every factory essay (including several in this wiki) optimizes the kitchen, and this one points out the kitchen is about to be table stakes. The weak spot is that it never addresses *how* to decide well once you've read the menu — the essay gestures at decisiveness but offers no mechanism beyond the re-pricing exercise, which is backward-looking. Pairing it with a forward-looking decision framework is where the argument is incomplete. There's also an unstated tension: for restaurants the stakes are low ("it's just dinner"), which is precisely why heuristics are fine there — so the analogy cuts both ways, and the essay leans on it harder than it should when arguing companies can't use the same coping moves.

## Relationship to the wiki

- Strengthens [[We Should Be More Ambitious (Dan Luu)]]: Luu argues ambition is now cheap and safe where verification exists; Arcolano supplies the organizational counterpoint — ambition is cheap for the kitchen, but choosing what to be ambitious *about* is the new expensive part. Luu is the supply-side argument, this is the demand-side one.
- Nuances [[The AI Productivity Paradox]]: Cagan says AI speeds output but not outcomes because teams keep a broken project model; Arcolano explains the same symptom via choice overload and prix fixe habituation — a decision-theoretic complement to Cagan's process critique.
- Complicates [[Software Factories, Light and Dark]] and the whole factory thread: those sources ask how to build the kitchen; this asks who decides what the kitchen cooks, and warns the deciding is unbudgeted work at most organizations.
- Connects to [[Optimizing for Decision Points]]: both locate the scarce human contribution at judgment moments rather than generation moments; Arcolano scales that from the individual developer's workflow to the company roadmap.
