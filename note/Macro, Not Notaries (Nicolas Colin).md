# Macro, Not Notaries (Nicolas Colin)

Nicolas Colin's Twitter synthesis of why global capital doesn't flow into the German tech ecosystem: the friction isn't notaries, it's macroeconomics. German export surpluses and Hartz-era wage repression park national savings on corporate and state balance sheets that reinvest in manufacturing competitiveness rather than startups, while the US "overflow mechanism" — trade deficit floods safe assets with foreign capital, crowding domestic institutions up the risk curve — makes venture capital a by-product of dollar inflows. The euro removes exchange-rate rebalancing, and by balance-of-payments identity, a US reshoring push would starve the very capital flows that feed Silicon Valley.

---

## The Occasion

Patrick Collison's ritual quarterly complaint about German notaries — the legal ceremony required for company formation — triggered the thread. Colin's opening move is to refuse the frame: notaries are a rounding error of friction, and the debate should be about why risk capital of any kind doesn't show up. "It's definitely not because Germany has notaries reading company bylaws aloud."

## Key Quotes

> "If corporations hold the keys to the national savings, then they will invest in becoming ever more competitive — which, in Germany, means to double down on exporting. They will spend on capex, on R&D for existing product lines, and on supply chain optimization. They will simply not invest in startups."

The Christensen point, and the thread's load-bearing insight. Germany's famously strong corporate balance sheets — routinely cited as a European strength — are exactly what starves its startup ecosystem: the savings exist, but their controllers' incentives point away from risk. The China parallel is sharp: SOEs and large corporates behave identically, reinvesting in manufacturing competitiveness into involution. China compensates with walled-garden Big Tech and a party-state acting as a nation-scale VC fund; Germany has neither.

> "A fully functioning venture capital industry exists at scale ONLY in the US, as a by-product of the country acting as a magnet for foreign capital (that, plus a few tax incentives and favorable regulations, ERISA etc.)."

The core claim, in its most contestable form. The US trade deficit floods the country with foreign capital seeking safe assets — Treasuries, agency debt, blue-chip equities — which crowds domestic endowments and pension funds out of those assets and up the risk curve, where a "discretionary pocket" of VC allocation waits. Note what this implies: VC is not a clever invention that other countries can copy, but a hydraulic consequence of being the world's reserve-currency sponge. Note also the concession buried in the parenthesis — "plus ERISA etc." — which is where the counterargument lives.

> "You can't have rebalancing and sustained capital inflows at the same time. This follows directly from balance-of-payments accounting: if you reduce the current account deficit, you mechanically reduce the capital account surplus. There is no way around this."

The Trump-era payoff of the framework. Reshoring manufacturing means fewer dollar outflows, less recycling back into US assets, and capital allocated to lower-return activities — so a tariff-driven rebalancing would, mechanically, cool the financial ecosystem that venture capital rides on. Colin reads Trump's world tour soliciting investment commitments from Korea, Japan, the Gulf, and the EU as (possibly instinctive) attempts to replace the structural mechanism with ad hoc bilateral deals. "Whether this reflects conscious understanding or instinctive dealmaking dressed up as strategy is unknowable, but the pattern fits."

> "The plumbing exists, but nothing is flooding it."

Colin's reply to David Rosskamp, and the best line in the thread. Europe has spent two decades importing the US institutional playbook — prudent-person rules (Tibi, Mansion House), fund-of-funds, EIF billions, recycled founders, deepening M&A — and the gap has widened anyway. His narrowing move: institutional plumbing explains the *genesis* of US VC (Rosskamp's list: ERISA, the Swensen model, Nasdaq, option culture, realized returns), but macro explains its *scale*. He proposes three tests: track US/EU VC fundraising ratios against current-account divergence; compare LP alternative allocations both sides of the Atlantic; and look for VC upticks in capital-inflow episodes (post-reunification Germany, pre-crisis periphery, current rearmament).

> "Sweden is an interesting mix: German style exporters and current account surpluses and a national debt at half the German level, but also the strongest venture capital cluster in all of Europe. Also unlike Germans but like Americans, Swedes put their savings into equities." — Arvid Hallén

The anomaly the framework must eventually absorb. Colin's mechanism predicts surplus countries shouldn't build VC clusters; Sweden runs German-style macro with American-style household allocation and got Europe's strongest venture cluster. Colin's answer is only "I'm very intrigued by Sweden" — which is honest, and also the sound of a theory meeting its exception.

## Key Themes

- **#concept** — *The overflow mechanism*: trade deficit → foreign capital into safe assets → domestic institutional capital crowded out → up the risk curve → VC funds. Colin's term for the hydraulic precondition of US venture capital.
- **#concept** — *The corporate savings trap*: when savings accumulate on corporate balance sheets (Christensen), they fund competitiveness, not innovation. Germany and China share it; only China built compensating institutions.
- **#pattern** — *Eurozone amplification*: a surplus country inside a currency union loses the exchange-rate adjustment that would rebalance it. The euro is structurally weaker than a hypothetical Deutsche Mark, so German surpluses — and the savings pattern — persist indefinitely.
- **#pattern** — *Balance-of-payments identity as policy constraint*: industrial policy (reshoring) and financial primacy are the same number with opposite signs. You cannot optimize both.
- **#concept** — *Genesis vs. scale*: Colin's concession to Rosskamp — institutions built US VC, macro scaled it. A distinction worth keeping when judging any "why Europe lags" argument.
- **#person** — Nicolas Colin (The Family co-founder, driftsignal.com), arguing against Patrick Collison's notary frame; David Rosskamp supplying the strongest institutionalist rebuttal; Arvid Hallén supplying the Swedish anomaly; Michael Pettis's savings-glut economics under the whole argument.

## Critical Analysis

This is macro finance wearing a startup-policy costume, and it's better argued than the genre usually is. Three strengths stand out. First, the mechanism is causally specific: it doesn't just say "Germany lacks venture capital" — it traces where the savings land, who controls them, and what those actors' incentives do with them. Second, the balance-of-payments point is unforgiving arithmetic, not opinion: capital-account surplus *is* current-account deficit, mirrored. Any US administration that wants reshoring must accept some starvation of the inflows that pump its asset markets. Third, unlike most Twitter macro threads, Colin offers falsifiable tests and immediately engages the best counterargument in his replies.

That counterargument — Rosskamp's institutional list — deserves its weight. ERISA's prudent-man reforms, Nasdaq's exit path, option culture, bankruptcy norms, and four decades of *realized returns* persuading LPs are not plumbing you can dismiss; they are the reason US VC existed before the modern capital-inflow regime. Colin's split (institutions = genesis, macro = scale) is defensible but conveniently partitions the evidence. And Sweden is a genuine problem: a surplus economy with a deep VC cluster suggests household allocation and equity culture might matter more than trade direction.

For the AI industry specifically, the thread reads as a weather report: Europe's frontier-AI ambitions (and its open-model efforts) are attempts to build inside precisely the capital structure Colin says cannot fund them — which cuts against explanations of the AI gap that stop at "regulation" or "risk aversion." Symmetrically, it implies the current US tariff-and-reshoring turn is, slowly and mechanically, a tax on the funding environment that built its AI labs. Both predictions are checkable within a decade; that's more than most essays offer.
