# Building Resilience — Tricia Broderick (Craft 2025)

Tricia Broderick's day-one keynote on resilience, preserved as a ytx gist with a structured summary plus full transcript (venue inferred as Craft 2025 from the same-day Kent Beck keynote). Her claim: resilience has no recipe — only four "pieces of the puzzle" (compassion, confidence, courage, complexity) — and the talk's real payload is a set of diagnostic instruments for fake teams, indispensable leaders, and unpriced silence, demonstrated end-to-end by an 18-month handstand journey that reached its goal exactly once and never again.

---

## Key quotes

> "I would love to tell you this magical checklist... and then you should fire me for anything you hire me for, because I'm full of it."

Preemptively killing the recipe-book expectation. The same allergy to step-by-step formulas Dave Snowden showed at the same conference: resilience is complex, so the deliverable is patterns, not steps. The puzzle framing is even autobiographical — her family hides one piece from every puzzle she does.

> "Labeling something a team doesn't make it so."

The thesis of the group/team distinction. Groups cooperate — her relay-race image, where "if that second person falls down, no one blames the anchor" and "I could get a bonus if I hit a PR even if we lost the race." Teams collaborate — "let's build this together" — which is where she and Diana Larsen locate co-intelligence.

> "So many of our organizations are not only organized for groups, but they're optimized for group behavior."

The indictment the relay-race test operationalizes, and it lands hardest right where knowledge work lives: the moment work requires discovering what value even is, cooperation stops being enough.

> "I manipulated the crap out of you. I'm so sorry."

Delivered immediately after running the Queen of Hearts card trick on an audience volunteer — a live demonstration that a leader with a power dynamic can always steer you to the answer in their head. The follow-through: "Manipulation is not leadership and it's not trust." The self-audit she derives is sharp: if you could have arrived at your answer no matter what the other person said, you were manipulating, not leading.

> "You will stay the expert if you continue to act like you're the only expert."

The cost of hoarding knowledge, and the hinge of her hub audit: "What is it that I know that's making everybody dependent on me for that question?" Her worked example is a learning program she built as a director, where handing courses to volunteers produced something "so much better than what I would have built" and confidence in both directions.

> "What is my silence saying when I choose not to speak up?"

Her replacement for "have courage," which works exactly as well as "calm down" — never. Priced silence rather than summoned bravery, and explicitly banked on standing: "as a leader, as somebody in a position of privilege and power." See the take below for why that caveat matters.

> "Six weeks to a handstand. They lie."

On YouTube promises and the real timeline of learning. The supporting confession: "I'm an agilist and I say dumb things all the time, like 'I love to learn.' I lied, people, I lied. Because learning is hard" — and "we treat learning like it's this hidden off to the side secret thing that you might do once a quarter, when in reality every day to do your work is learning."

> "Well, you won't if you stop."

A community member's answer to her "what if I never get it?" panic — "It hit me right in the core." She hit the 10-second handstand at exactly 18 months to the day, and never again. Hence the close: "There is no done," "it was never about the check mark," and "How you build resilience is by being resilient."

## Key themes

- #concept — Resilience as patterns, not recipe; the individuals → groups → teams spectrum; cooperation vs collaboration; co-intelligence (with Diana Larsen, from *Lead Without Blame*)
- #pattern — The diagnostic toolkit: the relay-race test (would your leg earn a bonus in a lost race? then you have a group), the hub audit (what knowledge makes you the single point of failure — and does your job description say "drive"?), the Queen of Hearts self-check, the silence check, exploratory steps whose only purpose is unlocking unknowns, change pacing ("conference Tricia" pulling back from daily deploys to CI), recording daily practice
- #person — Tricia Broderick (leadership advisor, Ignite Insight + Innovation); Diana Larsen (co-author); Kent Beck (keynoted the same morning; thanked in person 25+ years after his book — "not because of XP, but because of the journey that it took me on"); Adam Grant's Mount Stupid and Dunning-Kruger name-checked

## Opinionated take

The best material is falsifiable and immediately usable. The relay-race test is a genuinely good one-question audit — most "teams" fail it, and Broderick is right that bonus structures manufacture the failure. The Queen of Hearts demo is the talk's masterstroke: she manipulates a volunteer live, confesses it on stage, and converts the trick into both a leader-side self-check and an audience-side tell ("just tell me what you want me to say"). The hub audit is the piece this wiki's agent-era material circles without naming: leaders taught strategy and delegation but never facilitation or mentoring, panicking when the hub role dissolves — "Uh oh, I have no job."

The gist's own digest prosecutes the talk, and the charges stick. Systemic causes, individual cures: she names orgs "optimized for group behavior" and bonus structures that reward relay-race legs, then prescribes only personal mindset shifts — not one word on changing incentive design. The courage reframe assumes her privilege; someone without her tenure, platform, and book contract faces real retaliation, and "what courage looks like when silence is survival" goes unaddressed. There is no boundary between resilience and burnout — "you won't if you stop" is excellent advice for a handstand and harmful advice inside a toxic job. The COVID story is the most concrete failure narrative and has no ending: her high-performing teams "reverted really quickly back to group," and her confession ("there's actually something that I should have been doing more as a leader") names the miss without naming the fix. The whole thing is n=1 — one career, one handstand, no data — notable from someone name-checking Dunning-Kruger.

A 2026 reading adds a wrinkle the talk predates: the relay race is becoming the default shape of agentic software delivery — work split across humans and agents, throughput bonused per leg. If Broderick is right that cooperation fails exactly when value must be discovered, orgs deploying agents as relay legs are rebuilding the structure she says cannot do discovery. That extrapolation is mine, not hers — but her framework prices it precisely.

## Relations

- [[Reducing Risk in Projects, Increasing Resilience]] — Dave Snowden's Craft 2025 talk shares the title word, the complexity framing, and the anti-checklist stance, and its own digest is similarly self-critical; but they approach trust from opposite ends — Snowden's "I do not trust anybody who tells me to trust them" builds trust from auditability, Broderick from intentional collaboration. Read together they bracket the trust problem in distributed teams.
- [[Shaped by Demand — The Power of Fluid Teams]] — Dan North dissolves the stable team as the unit of planning; Broderick complicates that: her COVID evidence shows people under chaos revert to "just give me my part" — the group end of her spectrum — so fluid self-assembly leans on exactly the team-forming capacity she says is fragile and never taught.
- [[Building World-Class Engineering Teams in the Age of AI]] — Rajan and Dohmke tell leaders "don't be a manager"; Broderick supplies what's missing between the summit stage and the org floor — the leader was never taught what to be instead of the hub, and the transition produces panic before it produces self-organization.
- [[Smart But Scattered — Peg Dawson on Executive Skills]] — Dawson's parents as "surrogate frontal lobes who gradually hand over the controls" is the developmental twin of Broderick's intentional teaching situations; both argue competence transfers through scaffolded handoffs rather than declarations of trust, and both name staying-the-expert as the failure mode.
