# Six Big Facts of Macrohistory (Halperin)

Basil Halperin's essay compresses macroeconomics into six chart-backed facts — from "everyone used to be poor" through the Malthusian trap, the Industrial Revolution, the shockingly steady 2% frontier growth rate, global income divergence, to the million-year acceleration of world GDP doubling times — and lands on the question of which prior to anchor predictions of the future on.

---

## The Six Facts

0. GDP per person is the measure of how well we're doing — and it correlates with happiness, longevity, infant survival, shorter working hours, and social trust.
1. Everyone used to be poor. Before ~1750, any wage gain was eaten by population growth. "We lived in a Malthusian hellscape."
2. The Industrial Revolution was the single most important event in human history: growth went from 0.01%/year to 0.1% to 1% to today's 2%.
3. Growth at the technological frontier has been a shockingly steady 2% per year for 150 years. Running water, electricity, cars, computers, the web — all one straight line.
4. Incomes vary enormously across countries; "those of us born in the United States are so, so lucky."
5. Growth miracles exist (Japan, South Korea caught up from sub-Saharan-Africa-level poverty), as do growth disasters (Argentina) and countries that never grew (Nigeria).
6. Over a million years, world GDP's doubling time has fallen from ~100,000 years to ~35 years. Aggregate growth is speeding up.

## Key Quotes

> "We are ∼10 times richer than our great-great-great grandparents, who themselves in turn were ∼2 times as rich as Jesus of Nazareth. Everyone used to be poor. This is the most important fact about macro history."

The core of the essay, and the thing Halperin says he wants students to feel "in their bones" a decade later — not the Solow capital-accumulation equation. It's a defensible claim about what education is for: retention of a world-view, not of derivations.

> "Growth at the technological frontier – measured here as GDP per person in the United States – has been a shockingly steady 2% per year for the last 150 years."

The eerie fact that makes extrapolation plausible — and, note, the prior that most AI-acceleration arguments treat as the conservative baseline.

> "Do you want to anchor to the last 150 years or so, with our constant 2% growth and doubling every 35 years? Or do you want to anchor to the *long* sweep of macrohistory, where growth has been speeding up?"

The payoff. Every forecast is a choice of prior, and the two most natural priors point in different directions.

> "Walking around outside, I think we should really feel bombarded with this fact, every day... Compared to the grand sweep of macrohistory, we live in a weird time."

Defamiliarisation as the essay's real product: the buildings and the stuff around you are the anomaly, not the norm.

## Key Themes

#concept #pattern

- **The Malthusian trap as humanity's default state.** Most of history is stasis; growth is the anomaly that needs explaining.
- **Priors determine forecasts.** Fact 3 (steady 2%) and fact 6 (accelerating doubling times) are both true and imply different futures.
- **Pedagogy as compression.** A catechism of six facts as the minimum viable mental model of macroeconomics for a civilian.

## Analysis

This is a teaching artefact dressed as an essay, and that's its strength: it commits to the unfashionable move of saying *these are the facts worth remembering*. The choice of GDP per person as the yardstick is argued rather than assumed, with the charts on happiness, lifespan, infant mortality and trust doing the defensive work — though the honest concession that it's all correlation leaves the causal story unstated (the CO2 chart is the single nod at costs).

The essay is quietly provocative for anyone thinking about AI. The standard debate — will AI raise the growth rate? — sits exactly on the fault line Halperin names. Most discussions anchor to fact 3: 2% is the trend, AI would be a step-change from it. But fact 6 says the trend itself has been accelerating over the long sweep, so "growth speeding up" is not unprecedented — it's the historical pattern. Halperin doesn't make this connection explicitly; he just hands you the two priors and declines to pick. That refusal is the honest part.

The weaknesses are the ones he flags himself: single-country frontier measurement (the US), correlation throughout, and fact 6's million-year GDP estimates that he admits are soft ("Measuring GDP in 1 million BC is hard"). But softness doesn't matter at the resolution the essay operates at — the doubling-time collapse from 100,000 years to 35 is too large to be a measurement artefact.

This source relates to the wiki's AI-era debates by supplying the long-run context they usually lack: [[The Road Runner Economy]] predicts a phase transition in software economics on essentially this-year timescales, and Halperin's fact 6 is the macro-historical precedent for believing acceleration is possible at all. [[Building When It Feels Like There's Nothing Left to Build]] asks what building is for when anyone can build anything — a question only askable because of facts 1–3, i.e. because abundance is new. And [[The Flat Curve Society]]'s plateau thesis is, in Halperin's terms, an argument about which prior to anchor to — the 150-year trend line against the claim that this time the acceleration is different.
