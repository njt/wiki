# Ontologies Come of Age (McGuinness 2003)

A Google Scholar citation record for Deborah L. McGuinness's chapter in *Spinning the Semantic Web* (MIT Press, 2003). The excerpted description makes the era's central claim: the Web succeeded because tools made publishing easy, but its pages remain written for human eyes — and that is exactly why search still needs a human to read the results. The fix proposed was machine-readable semantics: ontologies as the shared, formal vocabulary that would let programs, not just people, find and combine information.

---

## Key quotes

> "Arguably, the generation of tools for machine generation and management of Web pages allowed the Web to really take off. Tool platforms allowed nontechnical people to generate and publish Web pages quickly and easily."

The Web's inflection point wasn't a better protocol but better *authoring tools* — an argument that recurs in every wave of abstraction, including today's coding agents: adoption follows tooling, not capability.

> "Web pages still preserve much of their early character of being aimed at human consumption. Thus, applications such as search still require humans to review results pages in order to find the right answer to their queries."

Written in 2003 and still recognisably true in 2026 — the observation underneath every retrieval and agent pipeline: the bottleneck is that meaning lives in documents formatted for people.

> "Finding the exact information one is seeking on the Web today is not as easy as one would hope."

McGuinness concedes even Google-era search hasn't solved retrieval; the chapter's wager is that formal semantics will. History's verdict is mixed — the semantic web largely lost to statistical methods, then the statistical methods got good enough that the wager is being re-litigated.

## Analysis

This is a citation record, not the chapter, so treat it as a signpost rather than a full source. Even so, its framing excerpt is worth keeping because it states the 2003 position in its cleanest form: the next evolution of online environments is content that "programs or automatic readers" can consume. That is the same bet as [[Ontology Development 101 (Noy & McGuinness 2001)]] — Gruber's "explicit formal specifications" shared so electronic agents can read them — two years on, aimed at the Web as a whole rather than at knowledge-engineering practice.

The honest reading is that McGuinness was right about the problem and wrong-ish about the solution. Machine-readable shared semantics did not tame the Web; instead, models learned to read human-consumption documents directly, and the "exact information one is seeking" problem is now attacked by LLMs over unstructured text. But the chapter's diagnosis resurfaces everywhere in this wiki: agents still choke on meaning that is implicit for humans and invisible to programs, which is why formal and semi-formal artifacts keep being rediscovered — see [[The Coming Need for Formal Specification]] and the SEP entry [[Ontology and Information Systems (SEP)]], which closes the loop by noting LLMs are "a model of language but not of the world."
