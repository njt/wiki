# How Technology Empowers—and Imperils—Dictators

Schneier and Gunitsky argue that AI promises autocrats escape from the oldest dilemma of dictatorship — empowering subordinates to carry out orders without empowering them to turn against the ruler — but that the promise is a trap: AI is never autonomous, so dependence on unreliable humans is merely transferred to a small, opaque, irreplaceable clique of engineers, a new digital Praetorian Guard with concentrated leverage over the ruler.

---

## Précis

The essay's opening images are both Russian: Margarita Simonyan's admission that RT's AI-generated anchors "don't need a salary or insurance… won't get arrested" — "wonderful and terrifying at the same time" — and the historical framing that autocrats have always managed the agent problem by staffing key posts with mediocrities, per Arendt's "the best guarantee of their loyalty." AI appears to offer two exits: real-time monitoring that disciplines officials without informants (China's Zero Trust system, which flagged 8,700 officials before being rolled back under bureaucratic pressure), and outright replacement of unreliable intermediaries with "faithful algorithms that cannot be bribed" (Russia's Oculus censorship AI, the Meliorator troll-bot farm, the UAE's AI-drafted legislation and its mandate that autonomous agents run half the federal government within two years).

The rebuttal is structural: someone builds, trains, and maintains these systems, and that work is too technical for the palace to evaluate. The engineers decide what the ruler sees — which threats get flagged, which content gets suppressed — invisibly, from inside the apparatus. And the new guard is more dangerous than the old because it is smaller and indispensable: a dictator can replace a general without breaking the army, but not an engineer without breaking the machine. The 1991 Soviet coup attempt, where a handful of Relcom programmers kept Yeltsin's decrees flowing past the plotters, is the historical proof of concept.

## Key Quotes

> These anchors don't need a salary or insurance… They won't get arrested, and the police won't search their homes. It's all wonderful and terrifying at the same time.

Simonyan's own words contain the essay's thesis. The "terrifying" is doing quiet work: the propagandist senses that the automation that frees her colleagues from arrest also removes the regime's need for her.

> As a result, one of the oldest dilemmas facing autocrats is how to empower agents to carry out orders without also enabling them to turn against their leader.

The framing that makes the piece interesting beyond the security press: the dictator's problem is formally the principal–agent problem, and AI is being marketed as its solution. The essay's answer — automation relocates, not removes, the trust problem — echoes the "judgment relocates, not removed" pattern familiar from software-factory debates.

> Autocrats who turn to AI to escape a dependence on unreliable subordinates have only transferred their vulnerabilities onto a new group of officials.

The hinge sentence. Note what makes the new dependence worse: opacity (the ruler cannot evaluate the work), concentration (a handful of engineers are a single point of failure), and indispensability (skills that can't be conscripted at gunpoint or replaced quickly).

> The Praetorian Guard did not need to kill Emperor Nero to replace him. It simply had to abandon him for a rival.

The sharpest threat model in the piece: future coups need no guns at the palace, only engineers who delay the data, degrade the feeds, and let alerts arrive a few hours late. The ruler then "would be operating blind" — a quiet, deniable coup executed in the dashboard layer.

## Opinion

This is Schneier's best register: not a security advisory but a structural argument that refuses both hype directions. The tempting take is "AI supercharges dictators"; the equally tempting counter is "AI will never work well enough to matter." The essay's actual claim is more unsettling — AI will *work* in autocracies, and precisely by working it will create a new elite whose loyalty no surveillance system can monitor, because the surveillance system is the thing they run.

What the essay doesn't fully press: the same asymmetry runs in the other direction. If a handful of engineers can blind the ruler, they can also *un*-blind the public — Relcom in 1991, and Ovsyannikova's sign in 2022, are both instances of technical workers choosing sides. The regime's dependency is also the opposition's opportunity. And the essay is quiet on the workers' own incentives: engineers with leverage can extract privileges (as Russia's conscription waivers show) without ever plotting — the corruption of the praetorian class may matter more than its conspiracy.

The China anecdote is the tell the authors under-use: Zero Trust worked a little too well and was rolled back *under pressure from the bureaucrats it monitored*. That's the real mechanism — not dramatic coups but the monitored class quietly strangling its own oversight. Algorithmic surveillance of officials is subject to the same organizational immune response as any accountability system.
