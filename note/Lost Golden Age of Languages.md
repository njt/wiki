# Lost Golden Age of Languages

A 2026 *Science* paper upends the standard story of language extinction: the mass die-off of languages began not with colonialism 500 years ago, but with the rise of states and empires 1,000–3,000 years ago. The languages we speak today are survivors of a political bottleneck, not a representative sample of human linguistic creativity.

---

## The finding

Between 1,000 and 3,000 years ago, tens of thousands of languages were spoken worldwide — far more than the ~7,600 alive today. Linguistic diversity grew alongside human population for millennia, then collapsed as large states and multinational empires expanded. The languages of expanding powers survived; those of absorbed or displaced populations disappeared.

The methodology is ingenious because the data doesn't exist: no written records before ~6,000 years ago. Blasi, Bowern, Gray, and Hamilton combined ethnographic data from 171 hunter-gatherer and fisher societies, prehistoric population estimates, and statistical modeling to reconstruct the Holocene's linguistic landscape. Their models estimate only 4,500–6,200 languages 12,000 years ago — *fewer* than today. The peak came later, then the crash.

## Key quotes

> "The languages we see today are the survivors of a massive and highly selective historical bottleneck."

— Russell Gray. This is the paper's thesis in one sentence. The bottleneck wasn't colonial — it was imperial, and it was already ancient by the time Columbus sailed.

> "We show that language isn't isolated from political currents and that this dynamic has been a facet of human history for much longer than previously thought."

— Claire Bowern. The paper's political payload: language death isn't a modern tragedy; it's a structural feature of state formation.

> "It's not inevitable that endangered languages become extinct."

— Bowern again, pushing back against the fatalism that often accompanies language extinction narratives. Political currents can reverse.

> "A linguistic feature may be common not because it is inherently efficient or better for communication, but because it happened to be carried by populations that expanded."

— Gray. This is the killer implication for linguistics itself: the features we study as "universal" might just be the features of winners.

## Key themes

- **#pattern** — The bottleneck as a universal dynamic: political consolidation → diversity collapse. This pattern appears in languages, species, business models, and software architectures.
- **#concept** — Historical selectivity bias: the surviving sample is not representative. Gray's insight that linguistic "universals" might be victors' artifacts, not cognitive constraints, should rattle the field.
- **#tool** — Statistical and social-computational modeling as a way to reconstruct prehistory where direct evidence is impossible. The methodology matters as much as the finding.
- **#concept** — Language diversity as a demographic proxy. Bowern's framing — "a spoken sentence disappears into sound waves" yet we can reconstruct millennia of demographic events from patterns in language — is elegantly counterintuitive.

## Critical analysis

**What's genuinely new.** Pushing the mass-extinction timeline back from 500 to 3,000 years ago isn't a minor correction — it rewrites the moral framing. Colonialism becomes an accelerant of a pre-existing process, not its origin. This doesn't excuse colonialism's linguistic destruction, but it complicates the simple villain narrative. The structural driver is state formation itself, not any particular empire's bad behavior.

**The Gray Gambit.** Gray's claim that linguistic features may be common because their speakers won, not because the features are efficient, is the paper's most provocative idea and the one most likely to be ignored. It's a brutal application of the survivorship bias to a field that has spent centuries cataloguing "universals." If he's right, comparative linguistics has been studying the features of imperial languages and calling them human nature.

**What the modeling can't capture.** The paper reconstructs *quantity* — how many languages existed — but not *quality*. Were the languages of the golden age more structurally diverse than today's? Did the bottleneck filter for certain grammatical features? The model can't say, but Gray's quote suggests the authors suspect the answer is yes. The 7,600 survivors might be not just fewer but narrower.

**The Bowern optimism.** Her insistence that "political currents don't have to move in one direction" is empirically true but historically thin. Language revival movements (Hebrew, Māori, Welsh) exist, but they're state-supported exceptions that prove the rule: only political power reverses political extinction. The structural forces that killed languages for 3,000 years haven't gone anywhere.

**Why this matters beyond linguistics.** This paper is a case study in how curiosity-driven research — [[The Usefulness of Useless Knowledge]] — produces insights that reframe entire fields. No one funded this to solve a practical problem. But understanding that the linguistic landscape is a political fossil record changes how we think about cultural loss, state power, and what "normal" diversity looks like.
