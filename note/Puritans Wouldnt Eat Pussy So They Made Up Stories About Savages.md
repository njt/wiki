# Puritans Wouldn't Eat Pussy, So They Made Up Stories About Savages

Renee's essay argues that the colonial women who ran away to Native communities and refused to come back were a living referendum on Puritan society: the captivity-narrative genre was the propaganda built to explain away their choice. The piece moves from sexual autonomy as the diagnostic of women's standing, through case studies (Eunice Williams, Mary Jemison, Frances Slocum, the forced returns of 1764), to the captivity narrative as the founding myth of American culture and its three-century afterlife in the damsel-in-distress template.

---

## What it argues

A culture that treats a woman's pleasure as irrelevant extends that logic everywhere — land, labor, children, marriage. Colonial America ran a natural experiment: Puritan society on one side, where sex was procreative duty and coverture erased a married woman's legal existence; Native societies on the other, with matrilineal property, clan-mother councils, divorce on a woman's own terms, and (for the Cherokee, Huron, Wendat) sexuality unbound from sin. Colonial women could see both systems, and a significant number chose Native life, repeatedly, across decades.

The strongest evidence comes from the men who noticed. Franklin wrote in 1753 that whites went to Indian life "without compulsion" and could not be reclaimed. The colonial response was not self-examination but a literary industry: the captivity narrative, edited by ministers like Increase Mather, recast women's lived experience as "danger, temptation, and redemption through belief in God." Scholars quote it plainly: anti-Indian propaganda became a major motivation for writing and publishing captivities, and ministers promoted women's narratives specifically to counter stories of women converting and not coming back.

## Key quotes

> "You do not construct an entire literary genre, with ministers as editors and publishers, deployed at scale and with deliberate political timing, to solve a small problem. You build that infrastructure because the problem is existential to your social order."

The essay's central inference, and its best argument: genres are not built for minor inconveniences. The infrastructure of the captivity narrative is itself the evidence that women leaving was widespread and threatening.

> "Whatever Seaver chose to emphasize, soften, omit, or rephrase became, permanently and irreversibly, 'her' story. She never saw a draft."

On Mary Jemison's 1824 narrative, dictated at eighty to a male ghostwriter she couldn't have corrected. The passage is a sharp meditation on mediated voice — the same dynamic Mather worked on Rowlandson, applied to the one captive text that came closest to a woman's own account.

> "The real woman refused to be saved. The myth saved her anyway, because the myth was never actually about her."

On Cynthia Ann Parker's rewrite into *The Searchers*. This is the essay's payoff line about what propaganda is *for*: not describing women, but manufacturing what men need to believe about themselves — Slotkin's self-absolution thesis.

> "Native men, inside communities built out of grief and adoption and active warfare, treated their captives better than Christian men treated their own wives."

The essay handles the obvious objection — Native captive-taking was real — by contextualizing it as the mourning-war response to demographic catastrophe (50–90% population loss to European disease). The comparison it lands on is deliberately uncomfortable.

## Themes

#concept #history #propaganda #culture

- **Pleasure as diagnostic**: female pleasure — sexual, proprietary, political — as the honest measure of how much a society values women. The essay's frame is provocative; the underlying evidence (coverture, divorce rights, clan mothers) is standard history.
- **Propaganda as infrastructure**: a whole literary genre as the response to women voting with their feet, complete with editors who "won every time because they held the printing press."
- **The mediated woman's voice**: Rowlandson edited by Mather, Jemison ghostwritten by Seaver — the record of what women chose survives almost entirely through men's retellings of it.
- **Myth as self-absolution**: Slotkin and Faludi's argument that the rescue narrative lets a culture reframe its own violence as suffering, still running from dime novels to Jessica Lynch.

## Critical take

The provocation in the title is doing rhetorical work, but the essay earns its keep with the boring parts: Franklin's and Colden's admissions, the 40–60% return rates with women and children lowest, Bouquet's 1764 captives physically bound to stop them running back. The weakest seam is the one the author honestly flags — nobody was writing down what happened in colonial-Native marriages, so the sexual-autonomy link is inference from structure, not testimony. The queer and two-spirit material is offered as documented context rather than named case studies, which is the correct epistemic posture even if it leaves that strand suggestive rather than proven.

What elevates it is the media-history move: tracing a propaganda template from Increase Mather's press through dime novels and Buffalo Bill to Hollywood and the 2001 rescue myth. The captivity narrative as "the foundation text of American culture" (Derounian-Stodola, via Slotkin) is a strong claim, but the persistence of the damsel-in-distress as an inciting incident for male violence is verifiable in any theater. The essay's real subject is not the seventeenth century; it is what a society does when women's free choices embarrass its founding story.

## Relations

This essay is a case study in how a dominant group builds a narrative genre to manage an inconvenient truth — an older, bloodier cousin of the myth-maintenance [[Agent of Empires]] traces in how empires narrate themselves, and of the self-serving story Applebaum documents in [[Empire of Grift — MAGA Foreign Policy as Kleptocratic Revolution]]. Its core dynamic — women's lived experience contradicting the official lesson, and the editors winning because they held the press — sharpens [[Four Writing Humans (No Wait, Five)]] from the other direction: that page treats voice as an inheritance freely built from influences, while Jemison's story shows what happens when a woman's voice is assembled by a man who never gave her the draft.
