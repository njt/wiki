# DRI'ing Your Career — Cate Huston

A conference talk preserved as a ytx gist (structured digest + full machine transcript, c. 2025) by the DuckDuckGo client-engineering lead, coach, and author of *The Engineering Leader* — the transcript's moderator garbles her name into "Kate Houston," but the bio is unmistakably Cate Huston's. Thesis: no one will ever care more about your career than you, so run it like a project you are the DRI of — proximate objectives you actually control, opportunities you plant yourself, a support "raccoon squad" instead of one indispensable manager, coachability practiced on purpose, and quitting from power rather than confusion. The market context is one line that carries everything: "we live in Toronto now."

---

## The frame

The talk opens by celebrating good managers, then punctures the reliance on them: they are busy, distracted, sometimes unskilled, and always temporary. The raccoon metaphor does real work — Instagram's pampered raccoons (Melanie on a trike, Piper the painter, Tamo with his own crypto) are tech workers in the fat years; Toronto's trash-bin-defeating survivors are tech workers now. The point is not despair but that Toronto raccoons thrive by being "more strategic, more determined and more resilient."

From there it is a toolkit, delivered at raccoon-photo intervals: optimize the career arc, not the job; set proximate objectives that pass a control test; plant the flowers you intend to pick later and triage the ones offered (roses/tulips/dandelions); write the plan down (what, why, resources, supporters, opportunities); build the squad; become coachable by design; and review quitting annually, watching five signals.

## Quotes worth keeping

> "We live in Toronto now."

The pivot. One sentence does the entire market analysis; the rest of the talk is the individual's response to it.

> "Expect more from your career and less from your current job."

The arc-over-job inversion that organizes everything else: jobs you don't enjoy can still develop you, and jobs you love may not develop you at all — making that trade-off explicit is the decision-making move.

> "Promotions are a level of capitalism and capitalism is a con. I am not anti promotion. Promotions are great. But let's get real."

The control test applied to the default career goal: if somebody else has to give you the goal, "what is your work in that?" The Q&A then walks straight into the tension — titles bring money — and gets deflation ("go and get the max that you can") rather than resolution.

> "Your job title is a couple of words and the work is 40 plus hours a week."

Her saddest hiring-manager cases are people hung up on a title whose stated preferences don't match the work; on her teams, staff engineer means "you're going to fix a terrible problem whilst also shipping something." She is careful not to invalidate people who fought hard for titles — just: don't hold one "at the expense of the work you actually do and the growth you get."

> "Sometimes mentoring is like teaching somebody to swim in a swimming pool and their day to day reality is they're drowning in the ocean."

Mentoring must match the person's actual level; the growth sweet spot is "out of your comfort zone, with help," and strengths come first — only "controlling weaknesses" get fixed (via Buckingham's *First, Break All the Rules*).

> "You know how they say feedback is a gift? Sometimes it is a gift that you need to return, donate or put in the trash."

Feedback triage in one line: information to improve with, information about the giver, or the bin. Bad feedback ignores broader context, negates your input, or undermines rather than helps you succeed. The Netflix standard — direct feedback that helps the person succeed — is the usable kernel. Her own voice-and-accent example shows both the bin ("I do not take unsolicited criticism from men on the Internet just for my own mental health") and the slow mining ("eventually I learned what I actually needed to take from it, which is that my pacing is really fast").

> "If you think other people deserve better, then it's possible that you do too."

Moral conflict about hiring as a quit signal — the sharpest line in the talk, and it doubles as the assert-your-own-worth move. Its sibling: "When we feel ourselves getting smaller rather than bigger, this can be really corrosive… this is how abusive environments keep people."

## An opinionated read

The distinctive thing about this source is that its own digest audits it harder than most reviews would. The "What the Talk Leaves Open" list is largely correct, and worth taking seriously rather than treating as false modesty.

The strongest material is the coachability model and the feedback triage. Treating feedback as data with provenance — some to improve with, some that only tells you about the giver, some for the bin — is the most transferable framework here, and the two-axis coachability model (receptiveness × actionability, with different quadrants appropriate for different trust levels) resists the usual one-size preachiness. The DRI move itself is a clean transfer of Google's accountability culture from deliverables to the self, and it is the right correction to outsourcing career decisions to whoever happens to be your manager this year.

The weaknesses are real. First, the titular framework is thin — "DRI" gets one sentence of definition; this is a bundle of career-advice modules under a catchy brand, and "this is not advice, this is things to think about" is a hedge that sidesteps accountability precisely where advice must fit someone's situation. Second, every remedy is individual strategy for structural weather: the metaphor names a hard market, but layoffs, ageism, visa dependency, and — glaringly for a careers talk aimed at developers in 2025 — AI's reshaping of software work go unmentioned. Third, "planting flowers" assumes spare Saturdays; her own account (a year of writing the book with none) is the tell, and caregivers, visa holders, and engineers living paycheck to paycheck get no version of this advice. Fourth, the hardest part of feedback triage — separating sexist noise from the true kernel — is asserted, not taught; how she decided the pacing criticism was real is exactly the skill the audience needs. Fifth, genuinely bad managers and abusive environments get one sentence and no exit strategy.

The transcript itself is machine-generated and rough — names, numbers, and one-liners are garbled ("it's one years of experience five times over") — so quote it with care and correction where the meaning is clear.

## How this connects

- [[Building Resilience — Tricia Broderick (Craft 2025)]] strengthens this: both talks locate a sustainable career in confidence and deliberate practice rather than output, and Huston's "collect wins" confidence-repair routine and shrinking-confidence quit signal are Broderick's confidence puzzle piece turned inward on yourself.
- [[In Praise of Normal Engineers]] nuances it from the other direction: Charity Majors deflates the 10x-engineer glamour at the org level while Huston deflates title-chasing at the personal level — together they complicate the staff-engineer-as-destination script from both sides.
- [[Nicole Forsgren on AI and Developer Productivity]] complicates it: Forsgren's point that agents which always agree with you can't replace honest human feedback sharpens the coachability argument just as AI erodes the honest-feedback relationships — and the AI-shaped labour market Huston's talk never names is exactly the force Forsgren's bottleneck-shift data describes.
- [[Two Languages — Complexity Bounds Systems Thinking]] shares the genre — another ytx digest honest enough to list what its talk left open — and its complexity-versus-complication distinction quietly challenges Huston's implied model that a career is a plannable system you can set objectives for.
