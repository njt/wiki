# Engagement Is Not Wellbeing — Mosseri's Defense of the Instagram Algorithm

Adam Mosseri's defence of Instagram's recommendation system, and the counter-argument that its engagement metrics are the very harm Australia's Digital Duty of Care bill is written to reduce.

---

This is an argument about which metric gets to count as "good." Mosseri presents a 50% engagement drop and falling time-on-platform as the harm of letting users opt out of ranking. The author's reply is that Australia's bill exists because legislators think engagement and time-on-platform *are* the harm — so the warning and the goal are the same number read in opposite directions.

> "If you go to a chronological feed, it ends up getting overwhelmed with content from companies, brands, and publishers."

This is the strongest point on Mosseri's side, and the article concedes it is real. But it is also a problem Instagram manufactured: the ranked feed rewarded high-volume professional posting, and removing the ranking does not remove the behaviour it trained. The causation runs the other way from how Mosseri tells it.

> "A 50% engagement drop tells you the product changes. It does not tell you whether the person is worse off, and that is the only question the legislation is asking."

The sharpest line in the piece. Mosseri gave engagement, time spent, and satisfaction — but never wellbeing, regret, or how people feel afterwards, the metrics Meta has repeatedly been asked about and its own internal research has covered. The missing number is the one that would settle the argument, and its absence is not an oversight.

> "The lesson of that period is the one the Australian bill is built on: a right nobody surfaces is not a right anyone exercises."

Instagram already brought back chronological feeds in 2022, as an option users had to re-select each session rather than a persistent setting. The bill's insistence on *prompting* rather than mere *availability* is the direct lesson of that episode — which is why Mosseri's claim that he wants algorithm customisation to be "core to the app" is not actually a disagreement with the bill, only with who initiates the conversation and how visible the off-switch is.

**Key themes**

- #concept — engagement as a proxy metric that has drifted from, and now stands against, the wellbeing it was once meant to serve.
- #concept — the difference between a right being *available* and a right being *exercised*, and what it takes to surface one.
- #person — Adam Mosseri, head of Instagram, whose framing choices under regulatory and litigation pressure do real argumentative work.

**Why this matters to this wiki**

This is a story about a metric becoming the product's goal and then being defended as its justification — the exact failure mode this wiki keeps encountering under different names. It **strengthens** [[Dopamine Fracking]] by giving a named, dated, on-the-record example of a platform executive defending engagement extraction as user benefit, and of a regulator treating that same metric as the harm. It **nuances** [[Goodhart's Law and AI Benchmarks]]: that note argues ungameable benchmarks don't exist because the metric gets optimised; here the metric (engagement) was *designed* to be optimised, and the fight is over whether anyone is allowed to stop optimising it. It **complicates** [[The AI Productivity Paradox]]'s output-versus-outcome distinction — Mosseri's defence is a case study in a company arguing from output (time, engagement, satisfaction scores) precisely because the outcome (wellbeing) is the one measure it will not produce.

The piece is also a clean example of a regulatory-design principle: the 2022 chronological-feed episode and Europe's modest DSA Article 38 adoption both show that an option nobody surfaces is an option nobody exercises, and that low adoption is not evidence of disinterest but of concealment.
