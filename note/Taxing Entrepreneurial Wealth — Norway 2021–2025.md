# Taxing Entrepreneurial Wealth — Evidence from Norway, 2021–2025

Marius A. K. Ring's NBER working paper (October 2026) uses Norway's 2021–2024 wealth and dividend tax reforms as a natural experiment to test the central claim of the anti-wealth-tax argument: that taxing business owners' wealth forces them to strip their firms of capital and flee the country. His answer, from unusually rich Norwegian administrative data, is that the "billionaire exodus" was real but tiny (~74–98 people), temporary, loophole-driven, and revenue-irrelevant — and that there is no detectable effect on firm investment, dividends, or bankruptcy.

---

## What the paper finds

- **Migration is real but tiny.** Excess outmigration was concentrated in the top 0.1% of taxable wealth (about 1.78% of that group, 74–98 individuals total), mostly in 2022–2023, and timed precisely around the November 2022 closure of the five-year capital-gains exit loophole. After loopholes closed, migration returned to trend. High-profile departures to Switzerland dominated the press; the data say the exodus was a loophole story, not a wealth-tax story.
- **Revenue barely notices.** Outmigration cost ~0.65 BNOK, about 2% of counterfactual wealth tax revenue. Revenues still rose ~71.5% from 2021 to 2024 net of flight.
- **Firms don't suffer.** Comparing Norwegian-owned firms with firms owned by exempt foreigners (foreign residents, foreign corporations), Ring finds no increase in dividends, no reduction in investment (he can rule out drops larger than 1.2 percentage points), and no excess bankruptcies. He repeats the analysis on foreign-domiciled firms with Norwegian vs. other owners as a robustness check.
- **The theory is ambiguous anyway.** In a life-cycle model with financial frictions, wealth taxes can *lower* the user cost of capital when owners have low elasticity of intertemporal substitution — they may save more inside the firm to offset future tax bills. The reforms reduce investment only if the EIS exceeds ~0.66, above existing Norwegian estimates.
- **Innovators didn't leave.** No excess outflow or reduced inflow of startup founders, tech founders, or patenting inventors versus other Nordic countries; startup investors actually migrated *less* after loopholes closed.

## Key quotes

> "These findings indicate that the short-run economic costs of wealth taxation, in terms of migration and firm investment, are more limited than the public debate implies."

The abstract's closing sentence, and the paper's thesis: the empirical ground truth is far quieter than the Financial Times headlines about billionaire flight.

> "Directly before the reform, each $1 of book equity in private firms entered taxable wealth at a discounted value of $0.55. After the reform, $1 of book equity increased taxable wealth by $0.80. Hence, the reform almost doubled the top marginal tax rate on business wealth from 0.47% to 0.88%."

The mechanics matter: this was not a headline rate change but an assessment-discount change — the kind of quiet base-broadening that hit closely-held business owners hardest and is almost impossible to study in most countries.

> "The slow-down of outmigration coincides with the closure of tax-related migration loopholes, which suggests that these loopholes were an important driver of the response."

The timing evidence is the paper's most persuasive move: the exodus was a race to beat a deadline, and once the deadline passed, the race ended.

## Opinionated take

This is a paper that does the unglamorous work of replacing a moral panic with a number. The public narrative was "Norway's billionaires fled and the tax backfired"; the data says ~100 people left, they left *before* a loophole closed rather than because of the tax level per se, the revenue loss was 2%, and their firms kept investing without them. The loophole-timing result deserves to be the headline: what looked like a response to wealth taxation was mostly a response to *escapability*. Tax policy debates about "capitalist flight" are routinely conducted at the intensity of the most dramatic anecdote; this paper is a template for answering them with population-level registers instead. Two caveats worth holding onto: the paper is explicitly about *short-run* effects (2021–2025), and the strip-and-return strategy it documents — emigrate, park shares in an EEA holding company, strip dividends tax-free, return within 12 years — shows how much avoidance design space remained open even after the reforms. The battle over the exit tax is clearly not over.
