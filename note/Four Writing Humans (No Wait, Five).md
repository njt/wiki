# Four Writing Humans (No Wait, Five)

Rands (Michael Lopp) names the five writers who built his voice — Fulghum, Parker, Cringely, Coupland, and blogger Michael Sippey — in the order he met them, from a Waldenbooks in 1988 to the early-blog era of 1995, and credits each with one ingredient of how he writes today: hope, dialogue, storytelling, legend, and the discipline of stating the obvious.

---

This is a short, warm piece of autobiography rather than an argument, but it does real analytical work: it decomposes a writer's voice into learnable, attributable parts. Each influence gets a section — a quotation, the context of when Lopp read it, and a one-line verdict ("Fulghum is hopeful. Parker was conversational. Cringely was entertaining. Coupland is hilarious. Sippey states the obvious."). The closing paragraph then reassembles the parts into the Rands style readers of Rands in Repose know.

---

## Key quotes

> His characters are talking all the time, and it's delicious. They are clever, funny, moral, and respectful. You sniff out the villains because they can't keep up the banter with Parker's main recurring characters.

A theory of character via dialogue: moral quality is detectable in conversational performance. It also quietly explains why Rands' essays read like a smart person talking rather than a document — he learned prose from a dialogue-first novelist.

> Cringely explained this by claiming he was not a historian, but an explainer.

A useful distinction for anyone writing about technology. The entertaining stories in *Accidental Empires* were partly unsubstantiated, and Lopp files that not as a scandal but as a different job description. It complicates the wiki's usual insistence that every claim trace to a source: legend-building is a legitimate craft, even when it makes bad history.

> There is no place in the Pointcast world for anything resembling a point of view. And that is what you should be surfing for.

Sippey writing in 1995 about push-media screensavers — and the point lands harder in 2026, when the "pre-digested information spewed to you in real time" problem has returned at model scale. The advice ("surf for someone that actually has something to say") reads as an early statement of why human voice still matters.

> You know you can write, but you haven't really gotten around to that first piece, and then you read something so good… that you realize… yeah, I would never be able to write like that. Fortunately for me, I ignored that instinct.

The most practical passage in the piece: intimidation by a better writer is the default response, and the whole career happens because Lopp ignored it. His first blog was, by his own account, "horrible, horrible writing" that he purged — Sippey simply never stopped.

## Key themes

#concept #person #pattern

- **Voice as assemblage**: nobody invents a style; they metabolise five people. The essay's structure — one ingredient per influence — is itself a repeatable exercise.
- **Stating the obvious as discipline**: "It is. Are you already following that advice?" Obviousness is not a defect in writing; acting on it is the scarce part.
- **Pre-Internet memetics**: Fulghum's church-bulletin essay "passed around (pre-Internet)" and became a bestseller — the same folk-distribution dynamics later attributed to blogs.

## Opinion

The essay is slight, and knows it — a gratuity at the end of a long-running blog. But it earns its place as a craft document. Its most quietly subversive claim is the Cringely one: that being "not a historian, but an explainer" is an honest self-description, and that a writer can build legend out of unverified stories and still be worth reading. That is a stance worth arguing with. The other durable idea is the Sippey lesson, which doubles as Rands' whole editorial formula: say the obvious thing, then check whether the reader is actually doing it. Most technical writing fails on exactly that axis.

It also functions as a period piece for a specific reading life — mall bookstores, InfoWorld's back page, a UCSC detective-fiction class, blogs before we called them blogs — that the current agent-era wiki rarely captures.
