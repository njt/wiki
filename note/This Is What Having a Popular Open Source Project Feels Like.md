# This Is What Having a Popular Open Source Project Feels Like

Matt Pocock's one-line video tweet from 10 October 2026: "This is what having a popular open source project feels like ~50% of the time." A 40-second field report on the emotional reality of popular-project maintainership — that the steady state is not building but absorbing: issues, demands, drive-by criticism, and the quiet entitlement of users who treat free labour as a service desk. The "~50%" is the claim's teeth: this isn't an exceptional bad day, it's the median experience.

The precision of the fraction is what makes it land. Not "sometimes" or "when it goes viral" — half the time, i.e. the baseline. Pocock is one of the most visible TypeScript/AI educators on the platform, and his projects attract exactly the traffic that turns a repo into an unpaid support queue. The tweet is less a complaint than a public service announcement for people who think popularity is the reward.

## Analysis

This is a two-sentence source, and it earns its place in the wiki precisely because it's the compression of a much larger literature. Pocock's "~50% of the time" is the felt, first-person version of what the wiki's other open-source sources argue structurally. [[Zero-Cost Fallacy of Open Source]] diagnoses the economics — permissive licensing confused with a license to exploit, corporations extracting without reciprocating. [[I Don't Want Your PRs Anymore]] describes the operational inversion — when LLMs flood maintainers with plausible-but-unreviewable contributions. Pocock's tweet is what all of that *feels like* at 8:52pm on a Friday: the human telemetry underneath the structural analysis.

It also complicates the rosier open-source takes in the wiki. [[The Golden Age of Open Source Applications]] celebrates the flourishing of small open apps; [[Why Open Source Matters for AI]] argues the commons is strategically essential. Both are true, and both quietly depend on someone standing in the blast radius of popularity. A commons whose maintenance experience is "dread half the time" is not free — the cost is simply displaced onto the person whose name is on the README. With AI now multiplying inbound volume (slop PRs, auto-filed issues, drive-by "fix this" demands), the ~50% is more likely to creep upward than down. The tweet is anecdote, not data — but it's the anecdote the structural arguments predict.
