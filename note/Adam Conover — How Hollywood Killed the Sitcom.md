# Adam Conover — How Hollywood Killed the Sitcom

Adam Conover's video essay takes a question that is usually answered with culture-war mush — "why did TV comedies get bad?" — and answers it with industrial economics. The claim is falsifiable and specific: the sitcom drought is not cancel culture or taste, but three deliberate cost-cutting changes to how television is produced, which together dismantled the "comedy factory" that made Cheers and Parks and Rec funny.

---

## The argument in one paragraph

Sitcoms are not being made because the streaming business model made them impossible to make, even though demand for them is strong and measurable — sitcoms are 1.7% of new shows this decade, yet a show that ended in 2019 is still top-10 streamed, and they punch far above their catalog weight in subscriber retention. Three structural changes did the damage: (1) short season orders of 8–10 episodes force serialized dramatic arcs because there is no room to just spend time with characters, and time is the essential ingredient in comedy; (2) separating writers from production destroyed the apprenticeship pipeline that produced creators with thousands of episodes of accumulated craft; (3) outsourcing shoots overseas severed the live feedback loop between writers and audience, without which jokes cannot be tested and punched up. If Conover is wrong, then the drought should be explicable by audience taste or prestige economics alone, and a streamer that restored the old production process should be able to manufacture a new Friends on demand. His implicit bet is that they cannot — the process, not the talent, was the product.

## Key quotes

> "Much like a Jenga tournament on the San Andreas fault, the problem isn't cultural at all. It is structural."

The thesis in one image, and the move that makes the essay worth reading: he relocates the explanation from the content of the shows to the incentives of the industry, the same move a good systems analyst makes about any "people got worse" story.

> "The process is the product. These shows were funny because of how they were made, because of the factory that produced them."

The strongest and most transferable claim in the piece. It denies that quality is a property of individual genius and asserts it is an emergent property of a production system — which is why the streamers can hire the same caliber of writer and still not get the same shows.

> "comedy is not just a monologue. It's a conversation between the writer and the audience"

This is the deepest of his three mechanisms. The live studio audience was not set dressing; it was an evaluation harness. Laughter is a "visceral reaction" you cannot fake from another continent, so removing the audience removed the only reliable test of whether a joke works.

> "And as anyone who's been on a bender with me knows, binging is no way to make friends."

The joke carries a real argument: the weekly broadcast schedule was not just a distribution constraint, it was the mechanism by which parasocial bonds formed. Compressing a years-long friendship into a weekend changes what the audience can feel about characters, which changes what the writing can do.

> "like medieval nobles trading Roman statues that no artist alive can sculpt"

The best line in the essay, and an accurate description of the current streaming catalog economy: streamers bid up rights to sitcoms whose production process no longer exists anywhere in the industry, trading artifacts of a lost manufacturing capability.

## Critical analysis

What is genuinely non-obvious here is the framing of the studio audience as infrastructure. Most nostalgia about sitcoms is about the shows; Conover's argument is about the *system* — audience-as-test-suite, writers'-room-adjacent-to-soundstage as tight iteration loop, 20-episode orders as the training regime that turned writers into craftspeople. Read this way, the essay is quietly about something the software world keeps rediscovering: you cannot separate the quality of an artifact from the process that produced it, and destroying the process while keeping the artifacts gets you a museum, not a factory. His point that first seasons are awkward *because the writers don't know the characters yet* is a real insight into why short-order shows feel cramped — they are all first season, forever.

The weaknesses are the ones the précis itself flags. The economics are asserted, never quantified: he never shows that the old factory model was profitable under streaming economics, or that short orders and overseas shoots actually save meaningful money after you account for the retention data he himself cites. This leaves a genuine hole in his own argument — if sitcoms retain subscribers at 13% from 7% of catalog, the incentive-mismatch puzzle (why haven't streamers noticed?) is set up and then abandoned. The likeliest resolution, which he doesn't explore, is that retention value accrues to the platform over years while production savings accrue to the quarterly report — a classic short-termism story he could have told but didn't. He also dismisses the Rainn Wilson cancel-culture argument with a joke rather than an argument, which is satisfying but leaves the strongest version of the opposing case (prestige economics, the collapse of the mid-budget comedy, changed viewing habits) untouched. And the promised "what it would take to get them back" resolves to a vague "eventually this sitcom will rise from the dead" — no prescription, not even a sketch of what a streaming-native comedy factory would look like. The sponsor digression on Ground News eats the time that should have gone there.

Still, as a piece of structural analysis it does the rare thing: it takes a complaint everyone makes ("they don't make 'em like they used to") and converts it into a testable claim about process. Whether the claim survives contact with real unit economics is exactly the kind of question this essay equips you to ask.
