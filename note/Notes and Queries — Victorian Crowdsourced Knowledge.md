# Notes and Queries — Victorian Crowdsourced Knowledge

The 1849 periodical *Notes & Queries* was the Wikipedia of Victorian Britain: a crowdsourced, pseudonymous community of amateur scholars trading historical finds ("notes") and open questions ("queries") across issues, sparking ongoing debates that anticipated both the collaborative model of the *Oxford English Dictionary* and the discursive chaos of Reddit.

---

## Key Quotes

> "founded in 1849 by William Thoms, a clerk in the House of Lords" who gave amateur scholars a venue to "share antiquarian finds and sources ('notes') or raise questions arising from them ('queries')"

The founder was a civil servant, not an academic — a reminder that the infrastructure of knowledge has often been built by outsiders with access to institutional machinery. Thoms also coined the term "folk-lore," naming an entire field in passing.

> "Had [George Eliot's] novel been set later, [Edward Casaubon] might have found the perfect outlet in *Notes & Queries*"

The Casaubon archetype — pedantic, completion-blocked, drowning in notes — is MacFarlane's sharpest observation. *Notes & Queries* gave these people a home. Wikipedia has inherited them: the anonymous editor who knows everything about 14th-century Flemish guild structure but cannot finish a paragraph. The medium doesn't cure the psychology; it routes around it.

> "a crowd-sourced, virtual community of anonymous writers"

Every generation discovers crowdsourcing and thinks it's new. The Victorian version had the same dynamics: pseudonymous contributors, cross-issue debates, fact-checking by counter-claim, reputations built on accuracy of response. The latency was measured in weeks instead of milliseconds, but the social structure was identical.

> "highly representative of the popular approach to history in Britain in the nineteenth century" with "its mix of alleged testimony, uncritical manuscript use, and sometimes dubious claims"

The methodological critique that arrived in the 1900s — that this was amateur hour, not real history — is the same critique leveled at Wikipedia in its first decade and at AI-generated summaries today. The pattern repeats: a new knowledge infrastructure emerges, lowers the barrier to contribution, produces a flood of content of wildly variable quality, and then professional gatekeepers arrive to declare it unscientific. The gatekeepers are sometimes right about the quality and always wrong about the trajectory.

## Key Themes

- **#pattern** — **Crowdsourced knowledge before the internet.** *Notes & Queries* had every feature we associate with online communities: pseudonymity, threaded discussions across issues, reputational economies, fact-checking by contradiction, and the peculiar mix of genuine expertise and crackpottery that defines open-contribution systems.
- **#concept** — **The Casaubon problem.** The pedant who cannot finish is not a failure of the medium but a feature of it. Fragmented knowledge platforms (periodicals then, wikis now) let the Casaubons of the world contribute useful fragments without ever completing the magnum opus they're constitutionally incapable of finishing. The platform captures the valuable byproduct of an otherwise stalled personality.
- **#concept** — **Amateur-to-professional lifecycle.** Every knowledge domain goes through the same arc: amateurs build the raw material, professionals arrive to systematize and dismiss it, then the amateurs find a new frontier. *Notes & Queries* was the amateur phase of British history; by 1900 the professionals had taken it, but the model had already migrated to the *OED*, the *DNB*, and eventually Wikipedia.
- **#tool** — **The periodical as social network.** Before there was a feed, there was the letters column. *Notes & Queries* was a social network running on paper and postage stamps, with the same dynamics of engagement, reputation, and slow-motion flame wars.

## Critical Analysis

MacFarlane's essay is a tightly constructed piece of cultural history that makes its argument through juxtaposition rather than assertion. The Casaubon frame does real work: it names the psychological type that open-contribution platforms attract, and it reframes what looks like failure (the unfinished magnum opus) as misdirected success (thousands of useful fragments). This is genuinely insightful.

But the essay pulls its punch on the most interesting question: **what does the amateur-to-professional lifecycle imply for Wikipedia and AI today?** The amateur phase of *Notes & Queries* lasted about 50 years before professionals declared it obsolete. Wikipedia has already lasted longer. The difference is that Wikipedia's amateurs built institutional defenses — notability guidelines, reliable-source policies, bureaucracies — that *Notes & Queries* never attempted. The amateur project that survives is the one that learns to police itself before the professionals arrive to do it.

The Cromwell material is darkly funny: a pseudonymous correspondent writes in to confirm they've personally seen Cromwell's skull in some gentleman's house in Kent. This is the Victorian equivalent of a Reddit AMA with a guy who swears he has the real story. The form is different; the epistemic quality is not.

The deeper point, which MacFarlane gestures at but doesn't fully develop, is that **the medium shapes what counts as knowledge.** *Notes & Queries* traded in facts — burial locations, executioner identities, skull whereabouts — because facts fit the format. A query fits in a paragraph; so does a note. The periodical form selected for granular, disputed, surprising factoids over systematic understanding. Wikipedia has the same bias, and so does every platform that atomizes knowledge into discrete contributions. The format teaches you what's worth knowing.
