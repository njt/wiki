# Fixing Bad Posture — Crown, Core, and Chin Tucks

A Hacker News commenter in their 40s reports escaping decades of bad posture the moment advice stopped naming an outcome and started specifying a mechanism. "Stop slouching" only ever got them a puffed-out chest they couldn't hold; two cues borrowed from chi running videos — push the crown of your head skyward as if pulled by a string, and engage the core so the pelvis is level — finally made standing tall something the body could execute and sustain. They round it out with a desk-side ergonomics picture as a sitting reference and daily McKenzie chin tucks to wear down decades of forward head posture. A small n=1 case study in the difference between instructions that name a state and cues that specify an action.

---

## Key Quotes

> "I gave up until a few years ago because all I ever heard or read was 'stop slouching'."

The whole failure of posture pedagogy in one line. "Stop slouching" describes an end state without specifying the movements that produce it, so compliance gets improvised — and the improvisation ("puffing my chest out") was both wrong and unsustainable. The commenter didn't lack willpower; the instruction lacked an executable.

> "(1) push the crown of your head to the sky as if being pulled by a string (2) engage your core so that your pelvis is level. The core+pelvis part is key for sustaining good back and neck posture."

Two transferable cues, and the interesting detail is their origin: chi running videos, from a school the commenter "never took up." The string image gives direction (elongate, don't arch); the core engagement gives the foundation that makes the direction hold. The pelvis is the part everyone skips.

> "I realized I never engaged my core when sitting or standing."

The hidden variable, found by noticing its lifelong absence. Once the core holds the pelvis level, "stand up straight" stops being a heroic position and becomes a resting configuration — which is why it finally felt sustainable where the puffed chest never did.

> "Specifically to try to combat the effects of having forward head posture for decades, I do the chin tuck exercise ... from the McKenzie method for neck pain/injury prevention."

The remediation layer, kept separate from the positioning cues. Chin tucks come from Robin McKenzie's mechanical-diagnosis tradition — mainstream physiotherapy, distributed as a cheap book rather than a clinic dependency. Cue for position; exercise for accumulated damage.

## Key Themes

- **#pattern** Mechanism over outcome — an instruction that names a state ("stop slouching") is unexecutable; a cue that names a movement ("string pulling the crown") can be complied with
- **#concept** The pelvis as posture's foundation — core engagement to level the pelvis is the insight the commenter flags as "key," and the one decades of advice never mentioned
- **#pattern** Cross-domain transfer — the fix arrived from a discipline (chi running) the commenter never actually practiced; the cue survived the transfer
- **#concept** Sustainability as the acceptance test — positions get rejected not for being wrong but for being unlivable ("never felt like something I could sustain")

## Critical Analysis

**The strong parts.** The comment is a clean articulation of why some advice fails: it specifies outcomes instead of actions. The two-cue protocol has the shape of well-designed instruction anywhere — concrete, checkable (is the pelvis level?), and cheap to run continuously. And the commenter is honest about the failure mode of the naive alternative, which posture moralizing rarely admits.

**Where it's thin.** This is an n=1 with no reported outcomes — no pain data, no follow-up — and the evidence trail is YouTube links and a truncated image URL: pointers, not studies. The two halves of the protocol also have very different epistemic standing. Core-and-pelvis leveling is standard orthopedic physiotherapy, and chin tucks belong to a method with decades of clinical use; but chi running itself is a commercial technique school whose posture chapter rides on tai chi imagery, not trials. The commenter lucked into sound cues — nothing about the source guarantees it.

**What's missing.** No strength dimension at all. Posture at 40 is partly a deep-neck-flexor and back-strength problem, and a cue cannot substitute for capacity. The sitting half is delegated entirely to a picture pinned at the desk. And "combat the effects of having forward head posture for decades" is optimistic: chin tucks are reasonable maintenance, not a time machine.

**The wiki connection.** This is the anecdote wing of a genre [[20-20-20 Rule — Digital Eye Strain Study]] anchors the evidence wing of: desk-health advice that spreads through the tech community faster than anyone trials it. The eye-strain rule went on to fail its controlled test; this protocol has never faced one — together they bracket how body-maintenance lore actually circulates, and why a wiki should hold both. The comment also strengthens [[Thinking Hard Burns Almost No Calories]]'s thesis that the knowledge worker's body is an engineering substrate rather than a motivational problem: Li's fix is scheduling, this commenter's is a cue plus a feedback prompt (the desk picture), and both refuse the "try harder" frame. And the McKenzie chin tuck extends the pattern of [[Goeckerman Regimen]]: the old, low-tech protocol that survives because it is cheap, specific, and does something, while newer and fancier alternatives orbit around it.

**The bottom line.** Worth the two minutes if you've spent a lifetime being told to stop slouching. The crown-and-pelvis cues are physiologically sensible and free to try at a desk today; treat the chi running packaging as provenance, not authority.
