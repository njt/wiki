# Where Do AI Norms Come From

Charity Majors opens a blog dialogue with research psychologist Dr Cat Hicks on AI-era norms, answering the practical question buried in the framing: how does a company go from friction and internal debate about AI usage to shared, written norms? Her answer is process, not policy — listen first, then codify.

---

## What it says

A reader (Niklas Lochschmidt) asked, after Majors' "Confessions of an Unrepentant Slop Snob" post and Honeycomb's public AI norms/values docs, what guidance she'd give companies that have the friction but haven't arrived at shared norms. Her reply:

> Start by listening. I put a few discussion hours on my calendar, opt-in, open door, each one capped at 10 people, and asked wide-open questions like "What AI-related thing has annoyed you lately?"

The mechanism matters as much as the content: small groups, opt-in, deliberately open-ended prompts. Themes emerged and were followed in later sessions. And the listening had a second function — catharsis as pre-condition for consensus:

> It also felt like an excellent steam valve to release some of the pent-up anger and frustration people were feeling, to the point that when we did eventually release the AI norms and values documents internally, it felt like the heat had simmered down quite a bit.

The rest situates the dialogue: Majors and Hicks were writing books simultaneously (a "sprawling" observability text; Hicks' disciplined *Psychology of Software Teams*) and found they were making the same case from different angles — what feeds greatness in teams, our responsibilities to each other, governance of sociotechnical systems. Now AI is in the mix, with managers expecting 10x while teams deliver 2x:

> The only honest answer is that no one really knows yet.

Hicks' side of the exchange lives at her newsletter (fightforthehuman), making this the opening serve of a two-blog conversation.

## Key themes

- **#pattern — Norms as emergent, not decreed.** The norms document is the *output* of a listening process, not the input. Most "AI usage policy" rollout fails because it runs that pipeline backwards.
- **#concept — Catharsis before consensus.** The anger is real data and real resistance; venting it in a sanctioned venue is part of the adoption work, not a distraction from it.
- **#concept — Radical uncertainty as the honest baseline.** Whether manager expectations or team output should adjust is explicitly undetermined — a refusal to fake certainty that most adoption playbooks avoid.

## Analysis

The strongest thing here is the sequencing insight, and it's the thing most orgs get wrong. The default corporate move is to write the policy document and announce it. Majors inverts that: the document is downstream of org-wide sense-making, and the sense-making must include an outlet for frustration or the document lands on top of unresolved resentment and gets ignored or resented. "Steam valve" is doing real work in her account — the norms landed *because* people had been heard first.

The honest caveat: this is a one-paragraph answer wrapped in a lot of throat-clearing. The post is deliberately light — it's a dialogue opener, not a playbook. There are no details on what Honeycomb's norms actually contain, no numbers, no failure modes. Readers looking for the artifact itself need to go find the norms and values docs; readers looking for a repeatable process get one data point from a company with unusually strong engineering culture to begin with. What transfers is the shape (listen → theme → iterate → publish), not the content.

The 10x-vs-2x passage deserves more than it gets. Majors frames it as "no one really knows yet," which is honest, but it also quietly names the political economy of AI norms: a norms document is partly a negotiation with executives' expectations. That thread goes unpulled here — presumably the Hicks dialogue picks it up.
