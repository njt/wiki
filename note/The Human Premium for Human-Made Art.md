# The Human Premium for Human-Made Art

A BBC InDepth essay argues that AI content is creating a market premium on visibly human-made art — but the author doubts it will hold, because we already accepted algorithmic curation without a fight, and production may follow discovery.

---

The piece runs on a nice personal frame: the author DJs at a night called Rave Machine in Falmouth, and the sweaty, unrepeatable, entirely human experience of it becomes the benchmark against which AI-generated culture is judged. Around that anchor sit interviews with economist Daniel Susskind (King's College London), New Yorker writer Kyle Chayka, philosopher C Thi Nguyen (University of Utah), novelist John Lanchester, and Superstruct Entertainment CEO Alex Mahon.

The empirical spine is three Deezer statistics that pull in different directions: 44% of uploaded tracks are AI-generated, 97% of listeners can't tell, but more than half say they care anyway. Susskind's thesis is that art is special among human tasks because we value the *process*, not just the output — unlike tax accounting. Chess after Deep Blue is his existence proof that a machine winning doesn't kill human interest.

Chayka supplies the mechanism for the premium: AI writing reads as "generic, obsequious and banal," so the market starts rewarding visibly personal, even deliberately messy work; designers go weirder because "the average is too easily accessible." The institutional response — Fairtrade-style "human made" labels — is already appearing on physical media.

---

## Key quotes

> "If I walk into the Sistine Chapel and look at the ceiling, I think not only is that extraordinarily beautiful, but isn't it amazing that a human hand, flesh and blood like my own, came up with that. A mind like mine dreamt that up."

Susskind's account of why the human premium exists: awe at the artifact plus awe at the maker being *one of us*. This is the strongest version of the argument, and notably it's an argument about meaning, not quality — it survives the 97% statistic by design, since you could never detect this property by listening.

> "It wasn't simply the moves on the board that people care about. It's the fact that it's a human being sitting behind them, moving those pieces along that matters."

The chess analogy is doing real work here. Deep Blue losing to Kasparov was supposed to be the death of chess; instead the game boomed. For anyone who thinks AI-generated code kills human programming, this is the optimistic precedent — spectators and practitioners may care more, not less, when machines can do it.

> "I think readers are coming to value very obviously personal writing, and maybe even writing that's messier, less structurally sound or composed or perfected, than they maybe once would've desired."

Chayka's subtle point: the premium doesn't go to perfection, it goes to imperfection-as-signal. That's fragile — once messiness is what sells, messiness becomes a producible aesthetic, and AI will generate convincing mess.

> "If you commodify one kind of thing, the things you can't commodify start to seem more important."

Lanchester's economic framing, and the essay's best single sentence. It's also the source of the author's scepticism: commodification is dynamic, so "what can't be commodified" is a moving target, not a permanent moat.

## Analysis

The essay is honest enough to undercut its own title. The "human premium" is well-evidenced as a *sentiment* and as a *pricing signal* (AI-free labels, weird design, live-experience demand). The author's real argument is about the second-order effect: we already outsourced cultural *discovery* to recommendation algorithms with barely a murmur, and that acceptance is the precedent for accepting outsourced *production*. Chayka's *Filterworld* logic — the algorithm flattens taste, creators chase the algorithm, taste flattens further — is a self-reinforcing loop that doesn't care about the Sistine Chapel argument.

What the essay misses, from this wiki's vantage, is that the human premium has an exact analogue in software. The Deezer statistics map cleanly onto the code-review literature: the output is indistinguishable (tests green, prose fluent), but people still demand a human in the loop — and [[The End of Code Review]] shows even that demand eroding under volume pressure. The provenance-labeling movement is [[Code Standards That Survive the Next Agent]] in cultural form: standardise what every artifact must prove, because polish no longer implies care. And the calculator anecdote is the right caution against complacency in *both* domains — teachers lost that protest not because they were wrong but because the tool was simply better at the commodity layer.

My take: Susskind's Sistine Chapel argument is correct but non-actionable, and Lanchester's commodification logic is correct but cold comfort. The interesting question is which art remains un-commodifiable — and the essay's own DJ night suggests the answer is *co-presence*, the thing that can't be delivered as a file. That's a narrower moat than "art" but a real one; [[Programming Isnt Special — Its Art]] makes the adjacent case that the same commodification pressure hits software's creative layer too, and the response there is identical: lean into what the machine can't generate because it can't be present, can't be accountable, can't be *yours*.
