# The Art of Decision-Making

Joshua Rothman's 2019 New Yorker essay uses the decision to become a parent as a lens for examining why our biggest life choices resist rational analysis — and why that might be a feature, not a bug. The essay traces a philosophical arc from Darwin's pro-con list through decision theory's value-maximization framework to three women philosophers (Ullmann-Margalit, Paul, Callard) who argue, in different ways, that transformative choices aren't choices at all in the traditional sense: they reconfigure the values by which we'd evaluate them.

---

## Key Quotes

> "One of the paradoxes of life is that our big decisions are often less calculated than our small ones are. We agonize over what to stream on Netflix, then let TV shows persuade us to move to New York."

Rothman names something immediately recognizable but rarely articulated. The inversion is so consistent it suggests a structural explanation, not a personal failing.

> "The point of such a move isn't to maximize one's values. It's to reconfigure them, rewriting the equations by which one is currently living one's life."

This is Ullmann-Margalit's core insight about "opting" — and it's a direct attack on the premise of decision theory. If the choice changes what you value, you can't evaluate it by pre-transformation values.

> "'Bad' reasons are how she moves herself forward, all the while seeing them as bad, which is to say, as placeholders for the 'real' reason."

Callard's most counterintuitive claim: pretending to value something before you actually do is not hypocrisy — it's the mechanism of self-transformation. The aspirant is a well-meaning phony, and that's the only honest way to become someone new.

> "Perhaps you think that you can know what it's like to have a child, even though you've never had one, because you can read or listen to the testimony of what it was like for others. You are wrong."

L.A. Paul's Vegemite Principle is the essay's epistemological grenade. Some experiences are epistemically closed to outsiders — you literally cannot know what they're like from the outside. This has implications far beyond parenthood.

> "Like pages that turn themselves, the meaningful instants follow one another too soon. It's hard to think of them as stepping stones on the way to anywhere else."

Rothman's closing turn from philosophy back to lived experience. The essay's structure mirrors its argument: systematic analysis yields to the irreducible specificity of the actual.

## Key Themes

### #concept: Transformative Experience

L.A. Paul's framework: some experiences are both epistemically transformative (you learn something you couldn't have known) and personally transformative (you become a different person with different values). Having children is the canonical example. The implication is radical: there is no fully rational way to decide to undergo a transformative experience, because you can't evaluate it using your current value system. This isn't a bug in human reasoning — it's a fact about the structure of certain choices.

### #concept: Opting vs. Deciding

Ullmann-Margalit's distinction is sharp and useful. "Deciding" is value-maximization (Subaru vs. Toyota — your values are stable, you're just optimizing). "Opting" is value-reconfiguration (having children, emigrating, changing careers — the choice changes who you are). The terminology matters because it explains the behavioral paradox: we're casual about big choices because we sense, correctly, that the rational apparatus doesn't apply.

### #concept: Aspiration as Agency

Callard's contribution resolves the tension between Paul (you can't rationally choose transformation) and the experience of actually steering your life. Aspiration is the gradual process of trying on new values — the "near face" (shallow reasons you're embarrassed to state) and "distant face" (the real value you can't yet articulate). It's the best description I've read of how people actually change.

### #pattern: The Limits of Formal Decision Methods

Darwin's pro-con list, Franklin's Prudential Algebra, Johnson's decision science charrettes and scenario planning — Rothman walks through increasingly sophisticated decision frameworks and finds them all wanting for the hardest choices. The pattern: formal methods work for decisions where values are stable. They fail for decisions that change what you value. The essay is a taxonomy of that failure boundary.

### #person: Three Women Philosophers Who Reframed Choice

Rothman's essay serves as an accessible introduction to a philosophical tradition that's been largely ignored outside academic philosophy. Ullmann-Margalit (opting), Paul (transformative experience), and Callard (aspiration) form a loose dialectic: Paul says you can't rationally choose transformation; Callard says you can, just not all at once; Ullmann-Margalit brackets the rationality question entirely and focuses on what kind of act it is. Together they're more interesting than the decision-theory orthodoxy they replace.

## Critical Analysis

Rothman does something structurally clever that most New Yorker essays don't: he lets the philosophers argue with each other rather than using them as decorative citations. The essay builds a genuine dialectic — decision theory → Ullmann-Margalit's critique → Paul's radicalization of the critique → Callard's synthesis — and then tests it against lived experience. The personal narrative isn't window dressing; it's the experimental apparatus.

The essay's limitation is that it treats Callard as the resolution when she's really the beginning of a new question. If aspiration is gradual and requires "bad reasons" as scaffolding, what makes some bad reasons work and others fail? When does pretending to value something become actually valuing it? Callard's framework describes the process but doesn't give you diagnostic tools for when you're on track versus when you're deluding yourself. Rothman seems to sense this — the essay ends not with Callard's resolution but with the irreducible mystery of actual parenthood.

The obliquest critique in the essay is aimed at Steven Johnson's "Farsighted" and the entire decision-science genre. Rothman doesn't say it directly, but the structure makes the argument: Johnson's methods (charrettes, scenario planning, full-spectrum analysis) are tools for decisions where the decider remains the same person before and after. Applying them to transformative choices is a category error. The PowerPoint deck Johnson made to convince his wife to move to California is the essay's quiet punchline — not because it didn't work, but because it treated a transformative choice as an optimization problem.

## Connections

- [[Not-Knowing (Vaughn Tan)]] — Tan's taxonomy of uncertainty types (risk, ambiguity, complexity, genuine unknowns) maps directly onto the essay's argument: decision theory handles risk, but transformative choices involve the "genuine unknown" of who you'll become
- [[Engineering for Bounded Cognition]] — The cognitive constraints that make big decisions hard are exactly the ones bounded rationality describes; the essay extends the argument from cognition to identity
- [[The People Who Will Thrive in the AI Age]] — David Brooks argues volition beats intelligence when thinking is cheap; Rothman's essay is about what volition actually looks like in practice
- [[Decision Framework Skill]] — The 37signals decision framework as the structured alternative to pro-con lists; interesting to read against Rothman's argument that no framework can handle transformative choice
- [[Agent Identity]] — Identity as participation rather than retrieval; Callard's aspirational self is an identity under construction, not one that can be looked up
- [[Advice to Young People (Jason Liu)]] — "Good decisions beat hard work" — but Rothman's essay complicates what "good decision" even means when the choice changes the chooser
- [[The Joy and Power of Understanding]] — The essay is itself an argument for the joy of understanding: Rothman reads philosophy not to decide better but to understand what kind of act deciding is
