# The Engineer Manager Pendulum

Charity Majors' 2017 essay argues that the engineer/manager split into permanent "lanes" is a mistake, and that the strongest technical leaders deliberately swing between the two roles like a pendulum — because each side of the swing is precisely the training the other side needs.

---

## The argument in one paragraph

The claim: career ladders that treat management as the only path to progression systematically damage engineering organisations, because they (a) push people who don't want to manage into management, (b) drain senior engineers of the management experience that would make them better tech leads, and (c) keep managers away from hands-on work so long that their technical judgement rots. If Majors is right, organisations that force a one-way choice between IC and management tracks should show measurably worse tech leadership and more burned-out managers than organisations that support serial switching — and individuals who have done both should be disproportionately represented among the people who can actually get things done at scale. That is falsifiable: you could survey tech leads and managers about their role history and correlate it with org outcomes. If excellent lifelong managers who never coded after year five turn out to be just as effective, the pendulum is a personal preference, not an organisational principle.

## Key quotes

> "Fuck the whole idea that only managers get career progression."

The essay opens with its thesis stated as an obscenity, which is characteristic Majors: the register is deliberately unprofessional because she is attacking a professional norm. The target isn't managers but the ladder structure that makes management the only exit from senior-engineer plateau.

> "The best frontline eng managers in the world are the ones that are never more than 2-3 years removed from hands-on work, full time down in the trenches. The best individual contributors are the ones who have done time in management."

This is the pendulum's empirical core, stated as two symmetrical claims. Note that it is asserted from experience, not evidenced — the essay offers no data, only a pattern Majors has observed hiring and managing. It reads as true to anyone who has worked under a manager who forgot what coding feels like, but "best in the world" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

> "You can only really improve at one of these things at a time: engineering or management."

The mechanism underneath the whole argument: the two roles compete for the same scarce resource, sustained attention. Management is interruptive by design; engineering learning requires blocking interruption. This is why the hybrid manager+tech-lead role is, in her words, "unstable" — you are decaying at one craft no matter which one you feed.

> "Management is not a promotion, management is a change of profession."

The essay's most quotable and most consequential line. It reframes the switch as a restart: you go back to junior in a new skill set, and the status you thought you were gaining was never there to gain. Every downstream pathology she describes — resentful managers, burned-out reports, starved mentor pools — flows from organisations and individuals believing the promotion myth instead.

> "There’s nothing worse than reporting to someone forced into managing. Please don’t be one of the reasons people burn out hard on tech."

The moral stakes, stated directly to the reader. This is the passage that makes the essay more than career advice: bad management is treated as a public health problem in the industry, and the person who takes the job for status is implicated in it.

## Critical analysis

What is non-obvious here is the symmetry. The standard framing of the IC/manager question is about what the *engineer* gains from managing (influence, scope, compensation). Majors inverts it: management experience is primarily valuable to the *engineering* side, because tech leads who have managed know how to get shit done through other people, and ICs who have managed understand why the organisation looks ridiculous from the leaf node. The pendulum isn't career hedging; it's a deliberate curriculum where each role is a prerequisite course for the other.

The weakest joint is the essay's total reliance on assertion. Every claim is a superlative — "the best," "rare," "immense" — backed by one person's career at early-stage infrastructure companies. Majors flags this herself ("It's a good cycle for people who like early stage companies, or have ADD"), but the caveat is easy to miss. At a 2,000-person company with deep specialisation, the pendulum may be much harder to swing, and the org may genuinely need career managers who never return to the trenches. The essay also says nothing about the structural barriers that make pendulum careers rare: compensation systems that pay managers more, interview loops that penalise employment gaps in hands-on work, and the very ladders she curses. Telling individuals to swing doesn't change the machinery that punishes swinging.

What's left out, interestingly, is any mention of the people who try management once and discover they hate it — the essay's advice ("do it as long as it makes you happy, then stop") quietly assumes the exit is socially available, which the promotion myth she attacks is precisely what makes it not.
