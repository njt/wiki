# What I've Been Seeing Lately — In Software

A first-person field report from the self-consciously post-LLM corner of software culture: the author (mei, writing as "thinks") catalogues how people who "care about the art of software" are resisting, organising, and building — and argues the resistance is a social movement, not just a preference.

---

The essay skips the "is AI good?" debate entirely and instead documents a community. Its three threads: an aesthetic/moral position (handmade output, "the master's tools will never dismantle the master's house"), concrete organising (the Evocation Forth system, the Polyphony community's growth from one protocol into a multi-project collective), and a political reading (the powerful will happily consume slop; large commons like nixpkgs feel "inadequate" as vehicles). It closes not with despair but with inventory: small beautiful websites, federated alternatives, governments running Forgejo and NixOS.

---

## Key Quotes

> "the practical merits of a tool cannot outweigh the moral ones; the master's tools will never dismantle the master's house"

The thesis in one line, borrowing Audre Lorde. This is the sharpest available contrast with the rest of the wiki: where [[Own the Outer Loop]] or [[The New Software Lifecycle]] ask *how* to work with agents well, this source asks whether the trade is legitimate at all — and answers with ethics rather than economics.

> "Programming is labor. Any particular project requires a lot of it, continuously. Most software systems are inadequately documented — context lives in developer brains."

Interesting because it's the same observation the agent literature makes — context is the scarce resource — but inverted. Where [[The New Software Lifecycle]] concludes "capture the context for the agent," mei concludes "the labor and tacit knowledge are why we must keep going ourselves." Same fact, opposite program.

> "The whole world is polarizing lately, and the ruling class seems to understand the degree to which software rules the world. So they will take slop for less whiny developers who don't care about ethics or appearances."

A class-analysis frame almost entirely absent from the rest of the wiki, which treats AI adoption as a workflow or economics problem. Whether or not one buys the framing, it explains something the workflow literature can't: why some developers reject LLMs on principle regardless of measured productivity.

> "I want to see post-LLM development grow and get institutional support. Today, the Dutch government uses Forgejo... The French government uses NixOS."

The essay's most concrete claim: institutional non-LLM-adjacent infrastructure exists (Forgejo at code.overheid.nl, Codeberg as a verein, French NixOS adoption). This grounds the movement in procurement and governance, not just aesthetics.

> "I look forward to writing complicated software. The world seems tired of one-more-SaaS."

The closing turn — from critique to appetite. Echoes [[Building When It Feels Like There's Nothing Left to Build]]'s "build for joy" answer, but arrives at it from the opposite direction: Chip Huyen accepts AI and seeks what remains; mei rejects AI and finds the same joy.

## Key Themes

#concept — post-LLM development as a named movement, not a mood
#pattern — small aligned communities (Polyphony, Website League) as the unit of resistance, in place of large commons
#comparison — handmade software as moral stance vs. AI as practical merit

## Analysis

This is the first source in the wiki written from entirely outside the consensus this wiki mostly documents. Every other essay here — even the skeptics like [[LLMs Are Not Tools (Rich Hickey)]] or [[Breaking the Spell of Vibe Coding]] — argues about *how* to use AI or *why* to be careful. mei simply declines the premise, and the essay is valuable precisely as a documented alternative position rather than a strawman.

The weakest part is the argument's own admission: "we are losing, mostly. Or not?" The evidence offered for the movement's health is thin — one friend's Forth project, a community with two projects, a wish for a third. The strongest part is the political-economic observation, which is genuinely underexplored in the wiki: if slop is cheap and the powerful don't need craftsmanship, then artisanal software becomes a class marker, which is exactly how the author frames it. That's a testable prediction about the next few years, and most workflow-optimist sources here implicitly assume it's false.

The community-organising thread complicates [[Vibe Coding and the Maker Movement]] nicely: Sachin argued vibe coding skipped the scenius phase; mei describes a scenius forming *in resistance* to AI rather than enabled by it — Evocation, Polyphony, weird websites, the Website League. Whether that counts as the scenius the Maker comparison lacked, or a mirror-image subculture, is worth arguing about.
