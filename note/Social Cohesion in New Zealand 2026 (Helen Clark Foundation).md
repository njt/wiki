# Social Cohesion in New Zealand 2026 (Helen Clark Foundation)

The Helen Clark Foundation's April 2026 report is the second wave of an annual survey (2,882 respondents, late 2025, modelled on Australia's Scanlon monitor) measuring five dimensions of social cohesion: belonging, sense of worth, social inclusion and justice, participation, and acceptance. Its headline is a paradox: belonging and pride remain high (84%, 81%) while everything tied to whether the *system* treats people fairly — trust in government (39%, a record low), belief elections are fair (51%, down 6), perceived opportunity (45%, down 6) — is slipping. The report's real contribution is structural: a clustering of 5,513 responses into three New Zealands, and regression evidence that subjective financial stress is the dominant predictor of cohesion.

---

## The Three New Zealands

K-means clustering finds three stable populations, and the typology does more explanatory work than any single survey item:

- **The Connected (30%)** — comfortable, settled, high belonging and acceptance, but the *least* likely to participate in community, religion, or politics. Older homeowners and centre-right voters; the most materially secure and the most civically disengaged.
- **The Ambivalent (41%)** — the policy-critical middle. Middling trust, low participation, no hostility — but the group that "feels it the most" when the economy worsens or discourse turns divisive.
- **The Alienated (28%)** — low belonging, low acceptance, high sense of unfairness, but *oppositionally engaged*: protests, boycotts, posting online. Almost half of Māori and Pasifika, nearly half of Green voters, and seven in ten NZ First voters sit here — groups that disagree on everything except their structural position.

> "They are the loudest voices in our public conversation, and the ones most likely to feel the country is not for them."

This is the report's sharpest observation and it generalises well beyond New Zealand: the people who dominate public discourse are not the median but a structurally distinct cluster whose engagement is a symptom of disconnection, not health.

## Key Quotes

> "Financial stress, political alliance, institutional distrust, and social isolation reinforce each other. They produce a population that is frustrated and disconnecting from the conventional institutions we rely on for collective decision-making."

The regression evidence backs this: relative to the prosperous, someone "just getting along" scores 6.5 points lower on the cohesion index, someone "struggling" 9 points, someone "poor" 14.4 — a straight dose-response curve. Subjective financial position outperforms objective measures like housing status. The report's conclusion is blunt: "poverty alleviation is a critical social cohesion policy pillar," and "no amount of community programming can compensate for economic policy that leaves people behind."

> "Even among struggling people, those who had a high sense of belonging were just as likely to participate in civic and social activities as those more financially comfortable."

The hopeful nuance inside the bleak headline. The causal chain runs poverty → eroded belonging → reduced participation, so belonging infrastructure works *alongside* poverty relief, not instead of it. Housing tenure shows the mechanism: outright owners report 14 points higher neighbourhood belonging than renters, plausibly because duration in place anchors belonging — and half of adults now rent.

> "Isolation appears to shift the form of participation rather than reduce it: toward visible, collective, expressive, and oppositional forms of action, and away from institutional channels like voting."

Isolated people attend more protests (19% vs 12%) and post more politics online (32% vs 22%) but vote less (75% vs 85%). The "re-affiliation mechanism" — protest offers community, voting is a solitary act requiring belief in the system — explains why alienation and hyper-engagement coexist. Notably, isolation pushes people in *both* directions politically: Green voters report 61% isolation, NZ First voters 50% financial dissatisfaction and the strongest desire to replace the system.

> "Contact with people from different backgrounds consistently predicts stronger belonging and more accepting attitudes."

An explicit Allport-vs-Putnam framing: each additional ethnically diverse friend raises the probability of strong belonging by about half a percentage point, and 91% of New Zealanders report at least one diverse close friend — yet support for multiculturalism is at its lowest since 2011. The UK companion study supplies the caveat: proximity alone does nothing without "repeated, meaningful interaction around shared practical goals."

> "Most New Zealanders use social media multiple times a day (56%) and most also believe it is bad for society (54%). The overlap between these two groups is substantial: we are heavy users of tools we believe are doing harm."

The new social-media module is honestly inconclusive — no clean causal link survives confounding by age and financial position — but the self-aware-heavy-user finding is the memorable datum. Heavy X and Reddit users stand out for low institutional trust; the disengaged from all media are also disengaged from civic life.

## Critical Take

The report's strongest move is refusing the usual nostalgia frame. Cohesion "tied to who we are" (identity, pride) is holding; cohesion "tied to how the system treats us" is what's eroding. That reframing kills the lazy "immigration broke cohesion" reading before it starts — immigration attitudes are hardening (36% say too high), but the report shows the harderening tracks financial stress, not contact with immigrants, and the pace is far slower than Australia's.

The weakest seam is the policy section. The four recommendations (fund for continuity, evaluate relationships not events, share authority, align economic policy) are sensible but arrive via a UK case study that is essentially a list of ways programmes failed — an argument from absence. And the report is candid about its own limits: cross-sectional data can't establish causal direction, and the social-media module explicitly cannot separate platform effects from who uses platforms. Still, as descriptive infrastructure this is unusually good: the three-cluster typology and the financial-stress dose-response curve are findings other countries' survey programmes should copy.

---

This report gives empirical ballast to [[1000 Players Simulate Civilization]] — that Minecraft experiment dramatised inequality as an engine of social breakdown; this survey measures the same mechanism in a real population, with financial stress as the dominant predictor of whether people feel the country is for them. It nuances [[Why Your City Feels Built For Cars, Not People]]: that piece argues physical geometry is the binding constraint on urban life, and this report's housing-tenure findings (owners out-participate and out-belong renters by wide margins) show the built environment's stake in place operating on social fabric, not just transport. It complicates [[The Power of the Powerless]] — Havel's alienated citizens withdrew into ritualised compliance, while this survey's alienated 28% do the opposite, over-engaging in oppositional channels; both are responses to systems people don't believe in. And it extends [[First Four Ships]] from founding-structure to present-tense: the Canterbury Association baked social structure into a colony's manifest, and this report shows what that structure looks like 175 years on — and which parts are fraying.
