# All 178 Notes I Took on Writing Fiction

Cristina White's two-year MFA notebook, typed out and sorted by category: 178 terse aphorisms on characters, dialogue, endings, craft, plot, revision, sentences, setting, structure and writing psychology, gathered from many teachers (and deliberately including their contradictions). The dominant lessons — characterisation is action, the body is the apparatus of the plot, the art is in the revision, tolerate your own bad writing — read as a compression algorithm for what actually makes prose work.

---

## What the source is

A Substack post that is exactly what the title says: a spreadsheet of every note taken at writing residencies over two years, sorted into eleven categories, released as-is. No narrative, no argument — just distilled maxims, several of them contradictory on purpose ("Simplify the action and complicate the motivation" sits a few rows from other teachers' advice that disagrees with it). The author is candid that these are her interpretations, not verbatim class content, and that she paid $35k for them.

## Key quotes

> "Characterization is action, the choices they make." / "'Joe worried' no, give him something to worry about."

The characters section's core doctrine: interiority that never becomes behaviour is invisible on the page. Abstractions ("she's conservative", "he's traumatized") are called out as unhelpful — get specific or don't write it.

> "The body is the apparatus of the plot. If you're just thinking, you're frozen."

And its failure modes: "Too much action = a dog on an agility course. Too much emotion without action = fetal position, staring through a rainy window." The prescription is a ratio, not a rule.

> "The art is in the revision, it's not punishment." / "First draft is often scaffolding, and future draft is the art. You have to take the scaffolding away at some point."

The revision section is the most practical: pull one thread at a time (place, voice, colour, tension), save a "big scary draft," cut a third and the meaning will stay, and the reader rule — three trusted readers, and if two agree, concede.

> "The most important skill: write badly and tolerate your own bad writing enough to keep going." / "Self-trust is the #1 thing to develop for writing a novel."

The closing wisdom section is about psychology, not technique. Its best joke is also its thesis: after 178 notes, "when you get home, forget all your notes."

> "Journalism makes arguments, fiction stages arguments."

One line that quietly explains the whole craft: fiction doesn't tell the reader what to conclude, it stages the situation and lets them conclude it.

## Key themes

- #concept — action over interiority: characters exist only in what they do; "eyebrow work" and autonomic body functions are not actions
- #concept — resonant vs important moments: important moments are socially constructed; resonant ones are small, specific, and carry more emotional weight ("the day before the wedding")
- #pattern — revision as the real art: drafting produces scaffolding, revising produces the work
- #person — a chorus of unnamed teachers whose advice occasionally contradicts, which the author treats as a feature

## Opinion

This is the rare craft list that survives compression because it was already compressed — each note is a rule you could apply today. Its honesty about contradiction is its best quality: writing advice is a set of lived trade-offs, not laws, and anyone presenting it as a system is selling something. The psychological section is where it's most transferable outside fiction. "Write badly and tolerate your own bad writing enough to keep going" and "the book you can't talk yourself out of is the one you should write" are as true for any long creative or technical project as for a novel. The weakest parts are the genre-formal notes (two timelines, twist stories), which lose context without the lecture they came from. Overall: a $35k education given away, and worth more than most craft books precisely because nothing in it is defended.
