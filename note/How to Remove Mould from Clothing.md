# How to Remove Mould from Clothing

Expert-backed guide to removing mould from clothes and preventing it in wardrobes, drawing on materials science and occupational hygiene — with a bracingly honest assessment of when to give up and throw things out.

---

## Key Quotes

> "Cleaning mould from a hard surface is like wiping mud off a dinner plate, but cleaning it from a porous textile is more like removing ink from a sponge — you can remove it from the surface, but the material deeper in the structure may remain."

Ash Boss-Handley's analogy is the article's conceptual backbone. It explains why some items can't be saved: fungal root structures penetrate porous fabric the way ink wicks into a sponge's interior. Surface cleaning masks the problem. This is the difference between visible mould and structural contamination, and it's why heavy infestations justify disposal rather than heroic cleaning efforts.

> Mould needs the right mix of "moisture, warmth and suitable nutrients" to flourish.

Nisa Salim's triad is dead simple and makes prevention legible. Wardrobes trap warmth and humidity; overcrowding kills airflow; sweat, oils, and food residue on clothes provide the nutrient layer. Every prevention strategy in the article maps back to breaking one leg of this tripod. That's good systems thinking dressed as household advice.

> "If in doubt, contact an expert such as an occupational hygienist or indoor environmental professional (IEP) to assess the environment and provide tailored advice."

Boss-Handley's closing line quietly professionalises the problem. It's not "ask a cleaner" or "try vinegar" — it's "hire an engineer who measures airborne particulates for a living." The article treats mould as an environmental health problem, not a housekeeping embarrassment.

## Key Themes

- **#concept** The porous-surface problem: the fundamental asymmetry between hard-surface cleaning (complete removal possible) and porous-material cleaning (removal may be structurally impossible). This governs whether an item is salvageable.
- **#pattern** Prevention-as-system-design: mould is an emergent property of three interacting conditions (moisture, warmth, nutrients). Prevention means breaking at least one link. Airflow is the cheapest intervention.
- **#tool** Oxygen-based bleach as the key remediation agent for durable fabrics — gentler than chlorine, effective on fungal cells, and stain-removing.
- **#concept** Spore containment: taking garments outside before brushing is a low-tech but critical protocol that most people skip. Spread prevention before removal.

## Critical Analysis

This is a deceptively good article dressed as a lifestyle puff piece. The two-expert structure (materials scientist + occupational hygienist) gives it more rigour than the headline suggests, and it's honest about the limits of remediation in a way domestic advice usually isn't. "You might have to throw it out" is not what people click for, but it's what they need to hear.

The article's real contribution is Boss-Handley's ink-in-a-sponge model. Once you understand that porous materials hold contamination at depth, you stop treating every mouldy garment as salvageable and start making triage decisions. That's a genuinely useful mental model — and one that transfers to other domains where surface-level fixes mask structural problems (technical debt, anyone?).

See also: [[Micasa]] for a home maintenance tracking tool built for exactly this kind of recurring household task.

The weak spot: the prevention strategies are correct but generic. "Improve airflow" and "use a dehumidifier" are true but don't account for rental constraints, building envelope problems, or budget. A follow-up on cheap interventions (silica gel, cross-ventilation setups, wardrobe placement) would make this actionable for people who can't renovate.

The safety advice — P2 respirator, gloves, eye protection — is a nice touch that most domestic mould content omits. Treating mould as an inhalation hazard rather than an aesthetic problem reframes the whole task.
