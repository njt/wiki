# Why Your City Feels Built For Cars, Not People

Laurie Winkless interviews the authors of *Transportation and the Shape of Cities* — Christof Spieler (engineer, now Director of Transportation for Madison, Wisconsin), David Copeland-Loredo (Houston planner and illustrator), and Mandi Chapa (planner-architect, teaches at Rice) — about their argument that physical space, not policy, is the binding constraint on urban transport. The book gives each of five US modes (pedestrian, micromobility, car, train, transit) its own geometry — the footprint it needs to operate safely — rendered in 500+ scale drawings, and shows that most transport arguments fail because nobody draws the wish-list to scale and discovers it doesn't fit.

---

## Key Quotes

> "Over and over again, you end up in the situation where you have the wish-list of what everybody wants, and then you try drawing it out and realize it doesn't fit. That is such a common pattern that it led to the realization that space is the most precious thing we have in the city." — Christof Spieler

The book's origin story is a planner's lived failure: stakeholders list everything they want, and geometry says no. The move is to treat space as the *budget* and every mode's footprint as a *cost* — a framing that turns a values war into an arithmetic problem.

> "I think most people actually underestimate how much space transportation takes. And they also overestimate how much flexibility there is with that. There's always this feeling, if you could just be a little more clever, you can figure this out. But there are some things you just can't figure out, no matter how clever you are." — Christof Spieler

The anti-cleverness principle. Turning radii, lane widths, platform depths: these don't yield to ingenuity. It's a constraint-acceptance argument rarely heard in any design field, where the culture prizes finding a way.

> "Cars are always treated with rigor – the idea that this has a turning radius and a lane width is built into conversations. But pedestrians are also mechanical objects that move in space. They have every bit as much a requirement for space or for slope as other modes." — Christof Spieler

The most quietly radical claim in the interview. Pedestrians get aspirational language ("vibrant", "walkable"); cars get turning radii. Extending engineering rigor to walking — slope requirements, level-of-service, flow rates — is what makes the book's comparisons honest instead of rhetorical.

> "A parked car might get a 9x18 or a 9x20 space in a garage. Meanwhile, in our offices, we might get an 8x8 space, meaning you could fit two of us in the space one of our cars takes up." — David Copeland-Loredo

The statistic that does the most work per word. A parked, *empty* machine is allocated more floor area than the person it serves — and that ratio is invisible because garage geometry is so rigid it dictates the shape of the development around it, not the other way round.

> "People talk about rail not being flexible, but freeways are not flexible either. Once you've built one, you can't really move it. We don't talk about that because we've all gotten used to the idea that if you build a freeway, development follows." — Christof Spieler

Symmetry-restoring observation: the "inflexibility" charge against rail is true of every infrastructure commitment. Infrastructure shapes the city that grows around it, so what looks like responding to urban form at time of construction becomes determining urban form forever after.

> "We're trying to move people, and we have limited space to move people in. What are our strategies? I've found that to be an enormously useful approach, because you realize you're solving a practical problem. You're not actually making an ideological statement." — Christof Spieler

The political judo of the whole project: define the objective as *capacity* (moving people through scarce space) and the allocation question stops being cars-vs-bikes tribalism. Pair it with Copeland-Loredo's safety point — an extra foot of lane width buys faster driving and more severe crashes — and you get a frame that asks, per unit of space, how many people moved, how safely.

## Key Themes

- **#concept transport geometry** — Each mode has a physical footprint it cannot negotiate away: turning radius scales with speed (200-mph train ≈ 13,000 ft; bike ≈ 8 ft), parking garages are rigid enough to dictate urban form, walking thrives only on density.
- **#concept completeness of networks** — The US car network is legally guaranteed complete (developers *must* connect a driveway); transit has no such guarantee. Scarcity of stations, not transit itself, produces gentrification effects: "You wouldn't see gentrification effects if every neighborhood in the city had a subway station."
- **#pattern rigor for the uncounted mode** — Pedestrians, bikes, and buses are analyzed with the same engineering seriousness as cars. Buses "don't get the respect they deserve" — Houston's highest-ridership route in Texas still lacks dedicated space.
- **#concept capacity over ideology** — Reframe street-space fights as an optimization: limited space, goal is moving people, what maximizes throughput and safety?
- **#project Transportation and the Shape of Cities** — Spieler, Copeland-Loredo & Chapa, Princeton University Press, August 2026. Grew from a Rice urban transportation class (2015); 500+ illustrations; DC-vs-Baltimore as the canonical stick-to-the-plan case study.
- **#person Laurie Winkless** — New Zealand physicist and science journalist; the interview format (she reports the book "changed how I view the challenge of moving city dwellers around") keeps it accessible to non-planners.

## Critical Analysis

**The geometry frame is the book's real contribution.** The strongest version of this argument isn't "cars bad" — it's that the physical city is a fixed-sum allocation problem, and the US has never run the accounting. Parking a machine at 9×18 while seating its driver at 8×8 is a policy choice that reads as a fact of nature until someone draws it. The scale drawing is the honest broker: you can argue with a zoning board, not with a turning radius.

**But "space, not policy" undersells how much policy made the geometry.** The authors supply their own counterexample: the car network is "always complete" because *legally* developers can't build without a driveway. That's not geometry — that's policy so old it fossilized into apparent physics. The same goes for parking ratios and street-width mandates: the car's footprint in US cities is the sediment of decades of law. The honest framing is that geometry sets the *budget* and policy sets the *allocations* — past allocations masquerading as physical necessity are exactly what the book needs to expose, and occasionally re-enters as physical necessity itself.

**The DC/Baltimore contrast is doing double duty.** Washington's Metrorail was sketched in 1969, survived three decades of administrations, and was complete by 2001; Baltimore's 1971-mile plan (proposed 1976) shrank under every political cycle. That's not a geometry finding — it's a governance finding wearing geometry's clothes. The lesson the authors actually draw — "it's about the completeness of networks" — is about institutional stamina, which is a different book.

**The capacity frame is a rhetorical move, and it should be used knowingly.** "We're just solving a practical problem" is powerful because it disarms, but choosing *capacity* as the objective (rather than, say, street life, or emissions, or freedom of movement for the young and old) is itself a values decision. Utilitarian framing wins arguments precisely because it hides the value choice inside the metric. The authors are probably right that it's the only frame that sells in a US city council — but a reader should notice what the frame quietly optimizes away.

## Related Pages

This source is a transport-scale companion to [[A Pattern Language (Christopher Alexander)]]: Alexander's claim that "the patterns closest to physics outlast the patterns closest to sociology" is here promoted to a whole book, and his **Network of Paths and Cars** (#52) — pedestrians and cars as complementary but separate networks — becomes a measurable geometry rather than a preference.

It strengthens [[First Four Ships]] with a modern-scale confirmation of the same mechanism: early infrastructure decisions compound into permanent structure. Christchurch's 1850 street plan and LA's freeway network are the same phenomenon at different speeds — the plan, once built, shapes everything after it, and the DC-vs-Baltimore contrast shows the penalty for letting the plan churn.

It nuances [[Life at Low Reynolds Numbers]] from the opposite direction: Purcell shows that at bacterial scale, geometry and physics leave cleverness nowhere to go, and this book makes the same argument at the scale of a city — turning radii and lane widths are the urban Reynolds number, the constraint that no amount of clever design can talk its way past.
