# Piston v0.1 — A Causal Model That Knows When It Cannot Answer

Piston v0.1 is OlynkAI's 12M-parameter open-weight (Apache-2.0) causal model for time series: give it a panel of up to 9 variables and a what-if intervention question, and it returns an effect estimate with a 90% range — plus `p_answerable`, the probability the data can answer the question at all, refusing below 0.5. On a frozen benchmark of 4,592 questions in 998 unseen synthetic worlds, it beats OLS, double machine learning, CCPFN, TimesFM 3, and Chronos-2 by 27.6% on the risk–coverage metric without a graph, and exact identification + Piston wins by 37% with one — while its own limitations section is more honest than most papers' entire discussion.

---

## What it is

A ~4M-parameter axial transformer (3-seed ensemble, 12M total), trained only on synthetic worlds, that does in-context causal effect estimation: no fitting per dataset. Inputs are the last 256 steps of the panel and an intervention question (set `treatment` to x1 instead of x0 at step t; how much does `target` change 1–3 steps later?). Outputs are effect quantiles (5/50/95), `p_answerable`, and reachability — the probability the treatment reaches the target at that lag.

The distinguishing move is refusal. On the benchmark, 32% of questions are simply not identifiable from the panel, and every baseline answers all of them anyway. Piston answers 90% and covers 94% of its 90% ranges.

## Key quotes

> **Refuse when `p_answerable < 0.5`. Baselines answer every question, including the ~32% that are not identifiable from the panel.**

This is the whole thesis in one line. Every forecasting-style baseline is structurally incapable of saying "this data cannot support this question" — they produce a number regardless. Piston makes refusal a first-class output, which is what lets its risk–coverage curve dominate: the worst answers are simply never given.

> **The benchmark families are unseen, but they come from the same generator as the training worlds. That is a home advantage over CCPFN, which never saw this generator. No real-data evaluation yet.**

Remarkable candour. The headline 27.6% improvement is measured on the authors' own benchmark, generated by the same machinery that produced the training data. The numbers are real; the external validity is not established. Most model releases bury this; this one leads with it.

> **Confident-wrong is still 35% without a graph. Our own target was half the best baseline's rate; v0.1 does not reach it.**

A 35% confident-wrong rate means that of the answers Piston actually gives, a third are wrong. Better than baselines' ~40–45%, but nowhere near trustworthy. The refusal mechanism shifts which answers are given, not yet how reliable the kept ones are.

> **Does not transfer to contact physics. On simulated robot pushing (one-sided contact, feedback control), no model we tried, this one included, beat predicting zero.**

A small, unprompted negative result that tells you more about the method's actual reach than any leaderboard row.

## Themes

#concept — calibrated refusal: making "unanswerable" a model output rather than a human judgement
#tool — a 12M-parameter model small enough to run anywhere, with a pip-installable inference API and a frozen, reproducible benchmark
#pattern — training on synthetic worlds from a generator, then evaluating on unseen families from the same generator: cheap labels, home-field scoring
#concept — risk–coverage curves (T1) as the right metric when a model can abstain

## Analysis

The interesting thing here is not the leaderboard gap — it is measured against baselines the authors had to widen by factors of 7 to 56 to get 90% coverage, on their own benchmark, from the same generator as the training data. Take the T1 numbers as directional, not literal.

What survives that discount is the design idea. Causal effect estimation from observational time series is a problem where "I cannot tell" is often the correct answer, and Piston is the first model in this comparison built so that abstaining is a native output rather than a post-hoc threshold. That reframing is what makes the with-graph result (exact identification + Piston, T1 0.112, 7.1% confident-wrong) coherent: the learned refusal is weaker than exact identification, and the authors say so — use the graph when you have it, use the learned refusal only when you don't.

The limitations section is a model for the genre. Synthetic-only training with an admitted home advantage, no real-data evaluation, a failed transfer to contact physics, a refusal that does not detect unfamiliar data (it answers "answerable in worlds like my training worlds", not "answerable in this world"), and a missed self-imposed target, all stated flatly. Against the current norm of model cards that read like press releases, this is worth as much as the numbers.

The unresolved tension: 35% confident-wrong means the model is not yet safe to trust its kept answers, only safer than alternatives that answer everything. The contribution is the architecture of honesty, not yet the honesty itself.
