# Digital and Sexual Citizenship in an Age of Social Media Bans

Conference notes from an ECU Ethical Digital Futures Group event on Australia's under-16 social media ban, covered from the keynote (Tama Leaver) through panels on selfie culture, digital paternalism, parental policy, and sexual shame. The recurring argument: the ban is moral panic and regulatory theatre — politically effective, evidentially weak, and built on shame and surveillance rather than rights and literacy.

---

## What the source argues

**Leaver's keynote** traces the ban's origin to Malinauskas reading Haidt's *The Anxious Generation*, an ad-company-built advocacy campaign (36 Months), and News Corp's commercial grievance against Meta — not to the research literature, which Odgers and others show is muddy. The "exemption framework" that would have rewarded platforms for age-appropriate design was traded away in last-minute ALP–Liberal horse-trading. Implementation leans on stacked age-estimation tools with "some flaws. To say the least." After the ban: 85% of young people still on the platforms, zero fines issued, and the government's response was to double potential fines and quietly redefine "social media" to capture recommender systems and endless feeds.

**The selfie panel** (Dobson, Coffey, Hawker) finds young people as forensic critics of editing culture: "structural edits" (body reshaping) are "breaking the rules", "touch ups" are acceptable — the line being edits achievable "on my best day" versus edits requiring surgery. Editing is private and shame-based, and the technology is shifting from overlay filters to generative tools (EffectHouse, Meta's in-housed filter creation) that apply "beautification" invisibly and without public oversight.

**Sutcliffe** names the governing logic *digital paternalism*: pre-emptive, infrastructural, protectionist. The beach analogy lands — we educate, not ban, for a real danger. A rights-based approach would ask: is the restriction proportionate? Was participation interfered with more than necessary? Were young people genuinely consulted? (They weren't — only one young person, Leonardo Puglisi, had media voice.)

**Green** reframes the harm question: youth suicide numbers are *falling* (110 in 2020 → 70 in 2024), and the most at-risk groups are Indigenous, rural, and queer kids — not the white middle-class families Haidt centred. The ban's budget might have disabled harmful algorithms and funded those communities instead.

**McKee** on shame: the ban's goals morph depending on who you ask — "and that can be seen as being by design". His pornography research found no conclusive evidence of harms like misogyny, but plenty of shame — and shame is what stops young people telling adults when something goes wrong online. The ban manufactures exactly that.

## Key quotes

> "the Australian government approach to youth social media bans fits the definition of a moral panic, and can be seen as a (spectacularly effective) act of 'regulatory theatre' which primarily acts to convince parents that action is being taken"

The most quotable line in the piece, and the frame for everything else: the ban's primary output is parental reassurance, not child safety. It's policy optimised for being seen to act.

> "we can't stop you doing this, but we can make you feel bad about it"

McKee's compression of shame-based discourse around porn, applied to social media. The insight that shame forecloses adult conversation is the piece's most practical point: protection rhetoric actively destroys the safety channel it claims to protect.

> "we don't ban people from the beach, we educate them to try to make it safer"

Sutcliffe's analogy is devastating precisely because the beach is a genuine, physically mortal hazard — and Australia still chose education. Social media is less dangerous than the surf, and got a ban.

> "critical but caught"

Gill's phrase for young people who can articulate the problem with heavy editing and do it anyway. It generalises far beyond selfies — it's the condition of anyone embedded in an attention economy they can see through.

## Opinionated analysis

What makes these notes valuable is that they're *post-implementation*. Most ban debate was a priori; this is a field report from after the rollout, and the results are what sceptics predicted: near-universal evasion, no fines, a goalpost move via redefinition. The government doubling fines for platforms it has never fined is not enforcement — it's escalation of the theatre.

The strongest structural observation is the traded-away exemption framework. That's the moment the policy chose punishment over design: instead of platforms building age-appropriate versions (the carrot), we got only the stick. Paired with Leaver's warning against a Digital Duty of Care built on parental surveillance dashboards, the picture is of a policy apparatus whose only available instrument is control.

The selfie panel deserves more attention than it will get. The generative-filter shift — from overlay (which "preserved privacy" by leaving the face under the filter) to generative replacement (which creates "something 'new'") — plus Meta's killing of third-party filter creation with no public code of conduct, is a platform-governance story hiding inside a body-image story.

One weakness: as conference notes, it's compressed. Claims like the waterfall approach's flaws or the evasion figures are asserted without the underlying studies named. It's a map of an argument, not the argument itself — which is fine for what it is, but the citations (Odgers, Hall, the "Panic First, Evidence Later?" piece, the youth perspectives report) are the real payload for anyone following up.
