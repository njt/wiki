# Software Is Just Content

A designer's essay (with a co-authored second half by Claude) arguing that generative software built in minutes behaves like content, not software: it is made to be seen, forked, memed, and discarded, and it travels through feeds and shared files the way music did in the 2000s. Using Byung-Chul Han's *Shanzhai*, Virgil Abloh's 3% rule, and Nintendo's "multiplicative play," it builds a method for this world — reference, don't copy; change the right 3% — and then follows the economics to an uncomfortable place: a Spotify-of-software where value concentrates in headline brands while everyone else earns streaming cents, and an unsolved question of who gets paid for the echo.

---

## Key Quotes

> Because that is how I design now: by referencing other software. Notion, InDesign, Photoshop, Premiere, Final Cut, Roblox, Figma, Paper. To a language model, each of those names is a latent conceptual space... I am not copying those tools. I am naming them, distilling what makes them work, and evolving it. The reference does the heavy lifting of shared understanding, so that all of my effort can go into the difference.

This is the essay's most useful operational insight: a product name has become a compressed prompt. "Spellcasting with references" only works because decades of interface decisions are already in the weights — which means the real skill is knowing *which* reference and *which* 3% to change. Finding the three percent, the author insists, *is* the work, not a shortcut.

> One branch rises into entertainment: galleries, trending pages, forks, likes and remix buttons, a TikTok architecture built for things that happen to execute... The other branch sinks into infrastructure: headless services that agents call on your behalf... And the application in the middle, the thing with a name, an icon and a slot on your home screen, empties out.

The fork diagram is the essay's central claim, and it's more precise than the title suggests. Persistence dies on both branches: content is seen and forgotten, infrastructure is spun up and dissolved. The shipped, versioned app — the defining object of the last software era — is the casualty.

> The files are apps, and the viruses are called prompt injections: instructions hidden in a page, waiting for the agent that reads it.

The file-sharing analogy (burned CDs, torrents, viruses) is genuinely apt, and the security observation is the essay's most concrete: the installer is now "a paragraph of English," and nobody has shipped the antivirus.

> Software may look the same. If Adobe or Figma becomes, in essence, a prompt, the price of that prompt falls to a couple of cents... For most smaller makers, that economy hardly matters; they will never sit at the head of that curve.

> Most of the culture will happen in the second world. Most of the money will flow through the first.

## Analysis

This is a field report from the same moment [[The Cathedral, the Bazaar, and the Winchester Mystery House]] names from the developer's side: when implementation is nearly free, the binding constraint moves to attention, and idiosyncratic personal tools proliferate. The Winchester essay predicts sprawling one-off houses; this essay predicts them going *viral* — the same object seen as a post rather than a home. Together they suggest the Winchester houses don't stay private: they get memed, forked, and dated within weeks, exactly as the isometric-drawing trend did.

It is also a useful counterweight to [[Breaking the Spell of Vibe Coding]]. Where Thomas sees dark flow and slot-machine psychology in minutes-per-prototype building, this essay sees compounding taste and a library of references — "my taste and knowledge is exploding." Both can be true: the same short loop that lets taste compound lets judgment atrophy, and the essay's own confession ("you are probably not ambitious enough, I tell myself this every day") reads suspiciously like the affirmations Thomas warns about.

The economics section is the weakest and the bravest. The author concedes they can't see a sustainable business model for most software — a designer gleefully describing the liquidation of their own industry's pricing power — and the "Spotify of software" argument lands because it's the only precedent we have. But it quietly assumes the fork is total. Durable, boring, boring-to-trend software still exists (databases, ERP, everything that must work next year); the essay's own supernormal section admits this. The more likely world is not that all software becomes content, but that the *edges* of software — prototypes, internal tools, marketing surfaces, toys — migrate to the content layer while the persistent core stays exactly where it was.

The credit problem deserves more weight than the essay gives it. Six tools from one base in an evening, with attribution as "a polite note in a README" — that is the remix economy of music without even the royalty system music eventually built. Whether value can travel back up a chain that short is an open institutional design problem, not just a missing feature.
