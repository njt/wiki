# Five Kinds of Organizational Debt (Bond)

Suzan Bond's Constellary framework argues that the technical-debt metaphor belongs as much to organisations as to codebases: workarounds and unresolved gaps compound during rapid change until they slow everything down. It splits the lump category into five diagnosable types — leadership, relational, structural, systems, and reality debt — each with a checklist of "org smells," then analyses the reinforcing cycles and predictable combinations (scaling startups, rapid growth, post-merger) before prescribing leadership-first intervention.

---

## The framework

The move from one undifferentiated "org debt" to five types is the point: "If we're trying to fix everything at once, we'll probably fix nothing."

- **Leadership debt** — capabilities, infrastructure and layers that haven't kept pace; avoided conversations, bottlenecks, leaders thinking functionally rather than systemically.
- **Relational debt** — eroded trust and psychological safety; information hoarding, whisper networks, "us vs them." "A quiet assassin."
- **Structural debt** — org charts designed for yesterday's priorities; what worked at 20 people becomes dysfunctional at 200.
- **Systems debt** — duct-tape tooling, manual reconciliation, disconnected databases.
- **Reality debt** — plans built on best-case assumptions; "particularly dangerous because it compounds all other types of debt."

The "org smell" device is borrowed directly from code smells — surface indications pointing to specific dysfunction underneath. It's a diagnostic taxonomy, not just a metaphor.

## Key quotes

> "If tech debt is why our software is slow and buggy, org debt is why our teams can't collaborate, decision making is hard, and the best people are leaving."

The sharpest sentence in the piece: it prices the difference between the two debts. Tech debt degrades one output; org debt degrades the organisation's ability to produce anything.

> "When we're in transition we often have this weird double vision — we know we need to change but we're still making decisions based on who we used to be rather than who we're becoming."

Reality debt's mechanism, and the article's most quotable diagnosis. It recurs across scaling startups, rapid growth, and mergers.

> "Organizational changes without leadership changes simply create new structures for old behaviors. We end up with the same dysfunction, just with different reporting lines."

The core prescriptive claim. Tangible fixes (org charts, decision rights) feel safer because they're checkable; Bond calls that "the trap."

## Opinionated take

The taxonomy is genuinely useful and the smell lists are actionable checklists a leadership team could score itself against in an afternoon — that's more than most org essays deliver. The weaknesses are the usual consulting-framework ones: no evidence beyond assertion, no measurement ("watch how fast it accumulates" — how?), and a self-serving prescription that whatever the problem, the answer is leadership coaching, which is what the author sells. The compounding-cycles analysis is the strongest part; it explains why piecemeal reorgs fail, and it's falsifiable in a way the rest isn't.

For this wiki the debt framing is the hook. Bond's org debt is the human-side twin of the agent-era debt taxonomy in [[Agents and Acquiring Debt]] — where bl00cyb adds comprehension debt to technical debt, Bond adds leadership/relational/reality debt to the ledger, and both agree the highest-interest entries are the ones nobody wrote down. It also strengthens [[Shaped by Demand — The Power of Fluid Teams]]: North's demand-led fluid teams are one answer to structural debt, teams organised around yesterday's priorities — though North would say the fix is re-formation around demand, not a redesigned chart. And it nuances [[Startup Anti-Patterns]]: Bond's phase-predictable debt combinations (scaling → reality+structural+leadership) are exactly the kind of named, repeatable failure pattern that series argues for, generalised from startup dysfunction to organisational dysfunction at every stage.
