# The Usefulness of Useless Knowledge (Flexner, 1939)

Abraham Flexner's 1939 Harper's essay argues that curiosity, not utility, produces the discoveries that later prove most useful — and that institutions must be built to protect useless inquiry from the criterion of usefulness. Written as Europe fell to fascism, it is also the founding manifesto of the Institute for Advanced Study.

---

## What it argues

Flexner opens in a world "steeped in irrational hatreds" and asks why anyone still pursues beauty and knowledge. His answer: the useless satisfactions are precisely where undreamed-of utility comes from. He then runs the case studies:

- **Radio.** George Eastman names Marconi the most useful scientist. Flexner corrects him: Marconi contributed "practically negligible" — the last technical detail (a soon-obsolete coherer). The real credit is Maxwell's abstruse 1865–1873 equations and Hertz's 1887–1888 demonstration, neither of whom "had any concern about the utility of their work."
- **Electricity.** Faraday, a bookbinder's apprentice, "was absorbed in disentangling the riddles of the universe… As far as he cared, the question of utility was never raised."
- **Mathematics.** Gauss dared not publish non-Euclidean geometry for 25 years — yet relativity "would have been utterly impossible" without it. Group theory was abstract puttering until it became the basis of quantum spectroscopy. Probability theory was invented to rationalise gambling and instead underwrote insurance and physics.
- **Medicine.** Young Ehrlich ignored dissection to play with dyes — "Ich probiere" ("I am just fooling") — and his fooling became the staining techniques in daily use in every hospital.
- **Industry.** Dry spinning, the basis of modern rayon, was discovered by accident when the coagulation water was switched off.

His conclusion is deliberately stronger than the examples require: he pleads "for the abolition of the word 'use'." The waste of free inquiry "looks prodigious. It is not really so" — and even where waste is real, freedom is justified without any utility argument at all.

## Key quotes

> "Throughout the whole history of science most of the really great discoveries which had ultimately proved to be beneficial to mankind had been made by men and women who were driven not by the desire to be useful but merely the desire to satisfy their curiosity."

The thesis in one line. Note the careful framing: the *beneficial* outcomes are retrospective; the *driving* motive was curiosity. Flexner never lets the utility argument backwards into the lab.

> "I am not for a moment suggesting that everything that goes on in laboratories will ultimately turn to some unexpected practical use or that an ultimate practical use is its actual justification."

This is the escape hatch most bowdlerised versions of the essay lose. Flexner is not making a delayed-gratification investment argument ("useless today, useful in 30 years"). He is saying usefulness is the *wrong category* for judging inquiry — a poem, a symphony, a mathematical truth "bear in themselves all the justification" they need.

> "The real enemy of the human race is not the fearless and irresponsible thinker, be he right or wrong. The real enemy is the man who tries to mold the human spirit."

Written in 1939 with Germany and Italy clamping down on universities. The essay's politics and its epistemology are one argument: directed research and directed thought are the same error at different scales.

> "You have no duties — only opportunities."

Flexner's reply to a Harvard professor asking what his IAS duties would be. The Institute's design — no committees, no faculty meetings, no required outputs — is the argument made architecture.

## Analysis

The essay has aged strangely well and strangely badly at once. Well: every specific example still holds, and the deeper point — that you cannot steer discovery by usefulness because utility is unknowable at the frontier — has been repeatedly rediscovered (Bush's *Science, the Endless Frontier*, the mouse/GR literature, the transistor's roots in quantum mechanics). Badly: the modern misreading of Flexner as an ROI argument ("basic research pays off!") has been used to justify exactly the metrics-and-accountability machinery he wanted abolished. His actual claim is more radical and less fundable: some waste is the price of freedom, and the freedom is justified even when nothing comes of it.

There is also an honest tension he half-acknowledges. The piece was written to defend a newly endowed paradise for scholars, staffed by refugees from Hitler. Flexner notes the tables can turn — practical problems can stimulate theory (Pasteur, salvarsan, insulin) — but he never seriously confronts the allocation question: if all inquiry is justified by curiosity alone, who funds what, and on what basis? The IAS answer — an endowment, smallness, and a founder with strong opinions — is a solution, not an answer.
