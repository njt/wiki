# Tom Barraclough — Sovereign AI Policy

A 2026 Memia podcast interview where Tom Barraclough (Brainbox Institute, Syncopate/DocRef) systematically dismantles the notion that AI sovereignty means "the government builds a foundation model," replacing it with a multidimensional framework spanning compute, data, training, literacy, and individual agency — and arguing that the real infrastructure play is turning law from static PDFs into structured, versioned, referenceable datasets.

---

## The Core Argument

Barraclough's thesis has two layers. The surface argument is that sovereign AI for small nations is a multidimensional problem, not a procurement decision. The deeper argument — the one that makes this conversation worth attention — is that the substrate beneath sovereignty is **digital regulatory infrastructure**: law published as open, versioned, linkable datasets rather than layered PDFs. Without that substrate, neither governments nor citizens can reason systematically about what AI is doing to them.

This is infrastructure thinking applied to governance itself. It's the same pattern as [[Specifications as the Product]] applied to law: the durable artifact is the structured dataset, not the document that renders it.

## The Sovereignty Framework

Barraclough developed a multidimensional sovereignty prototype (live at sovereign-ai.tombcgh.tech) that rejects the single-model government approach. His layers:

- **Compute sovereignty**: who controls the hardware
- **Data sovereignty**: whose data trains what, under what terms
- **Training sovereignty**: the capability to train or fine-tune, not just consume
- **Content moderation sovereignty**: whose values govern what's permissible
- **Literacy sovereignty**: population-wide competence, not elite expertise
- **Individual agency sovereignty**: what Ben Reid calls "the control plane I'm most interested in"

The framework's insight is that **these layers don't all need to be owned by the same actor**. Te Hiku Media's community-owned Māori language AI demonstrates that distributed sovereignty works — and that values-driven development at the community scale is a genuine alternative to both state monopoly and market surrender.

> "There's all these different interesting layers of sovereignty that don't just require it being a government."

## The One-in-a-Million Shot

Barraclough's sharpest strategic observation: even China can't nail every layer simultaneously. Getting all sovereignty dimensions right requires a perfect alignment of political will, economic incentive, legal frameworks, and technical capability — a "one in a million perfect shot." For small nations with thin resources, the top-down approach is structurally doomed.

This is a genuinely useful heuristic. It reframes sovereignty from a binary (have it / don't) to a portfolio allocation problem: which layers do you control, which do you influence, which do you accept dependence on, and what's your hedge?

## Law as Culture, Not Runtime

> "Any large organization that requires any kind of institution or risk management process or policies is just basically made up of PDFs."

Barraclough's legal philosophy is the foundation everything else rests on. Law is an interpretive culture — courts apply judgment shaped by shared context, not mechanical rule-following. This means:

1. **Formalizing law as computable rules is a category error**, not a hard engineering problem. The PDF isn't the bug; the assumption that law could be deterministic is.
2. **But structured, referenceable, versioned law-as-data is still essential infrastructure** — not for automated enforcement, but for systematic reasoning, annotation, and interoperability.
3. **Jack Clark's "paper plumbing"** names the actual problem: the document architecture itself is broken. Organizations can't network their own rules.

This is a more sophisticated position than either "AI will eat law" or "law is irreducibly human." It's: law is cultural, but the infrastructure it runs on is garbage, and fixing that infrastructure enables everything else — including the parts that should stay human.

## DocRef: Law as Structured Dataset

Barraclough's startup Syncopate Labs builds DocRef, which turns regulation into granularly referenceable, versioned datasets. The live demo — DocRef + MCP + Claude drafting a new API standard from regulatory corpus in an afternoon, work that traditionally takes nine months — makes the case concretely.

The tension Barraclough acknowledges is real: speed is a public good, but so is regulatory stability. When lawmaking accelerates from 9 months to an afternoon, the process that produced democratic legitimacy in the slower world — consultation, deliberation, objection periods — becomes the bottleneck. This is the same dynamic as [[Writing Code vs. Shipping Code]] applied to governance: the tool accelerates production, but the human governance layer doesn't accelerate with it.

## Confidential Computing as Personal Sovereignty

The conversation connects the macro (national sovereignty) to the micro (personal sovereignty) through confidential computing. Apple's Private Cloud Compute and the startup Tinfoil offer hardware-verifiable privacy through cryptographic attestation — you don't have to trust the provider's contract; you can verify the silicon.

This is sovereignty for individuals, not states. It's the layer where [[Security and Sandboxing]] meets governance: trust enforced by hardware, not promises.

## Critical Analysis

**What holds up:** The multidimensional framework is genuinely useful. It replaces the sterile "build our own GPT" debate with an actual decision surface. The law-as-structured-data thesis is correct and underappreciated — the fact that regulation ships as PDFs is a civilization-scale infrastructure failure that nobody talks about because it's boring.

**What's underspecified:** The jump from "structured law enables systematic reasoning" to "this produces better democratic outcomes" is assumed, not argued. Making law machine-readable also makes it machine-gameable. Barraclough is aware of this — he cites Bruce Schneier on AI finding "bugs in law" — but the defensive dimension (how structured law resists adversarial AI) gets less airtime than the enabling dimension.

**What's missing:** The conversation is strong on infrastructure and weak on power. Who controls the canonical version of the law-as-dataset? What stops a future administration from retracting or rewriting the structured versions? The PDF problem is technical; the version-of-record problem is political. Barraclough would probably say the Cambridge paper addresses this, but the podcast doesn't surface that answer.

**The AOC quote is doing real work.** "We didn't have a rules-based order. We had a pre-rules based order" reframes the entire conversation. The international order wasn't broken by recent events — it never finished being built. This is the infrastructure thesis at geopolitical scale, and it's more honest than most foreign policy commentary.

**Te Hiku Media is the most important example in the conversation and gets the least time.** Community-owned, values-driven AI for an Indigenous language is the existence proof that distributed sovereignty works. It demonstrates that the "one-in-a-million shot" framing might be too pessimistic — you don't need every layer; you need the layers that matter for your context, done well. The Te Hiku model deserves its own deep treatment in this wiki.
