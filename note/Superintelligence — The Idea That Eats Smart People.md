# Superintelligence — The Idea That Eats Smart People

Maciej Cegłowski's October 2016 Web Camp Zagreb talk is the canonical skeptic's text on superintelligence: it reconstructs the six premises of Bostrom-style extinction risk fairly, dismantles them from the inside view, then flips to the outside view and argues that the *belief* — not the machine — is the hazard. Its 2016 vantage point, pre-transformer and a decade before agentic AI, makes it a rare fixed point for testing AI predictions, and its closing stage direction ("SYNCHRONIZED, SUSPICIOUSLY MECHANICAL APPLAUSE") doubles as method: satire as vaccine.

---

## Key Quotes

> "This scenario is a caricature of Bostrom's argument, because I am not trying to convince you of it, but vaccinate you against it."

The thesis, delivered after a deliberately absurd scenario: a joke-telling robot recursively self-improves over a weekend until everyone who hears one of its jokes dies laughing. The talk's structure follows the vaccine metaphor — present a weakened strain of the argument so the audience develops immunity to the virulent one.

> "The outside view doesn't care about content, it sees the form and the context, and it doesn't look good."

The epistemological core. Cegłowski — who describes himself as "an incredibly persuadable person," citing years of late-night Art Bell shows on dark drives through Vermont — imports Scott Alexander's inside/outside-view distinction and runs *both* against AI risk. The UFO-cult-at-your-door framing poses the talk's real question: if an argument is irrefutable and everything about its context looks like a religion, what do you do?

> "Enormous numbers multiplied by tiny probabilities are the hallmark of AI alarmism."

He quotes Bostrom's teardrops-refilling-the-oceans passage ("keep doing so for a hundred billion billion millennia") at full absurdity, then delivers the talk's most personal line: "We had the same exact arguments used against us under communism, to explain why everything was always broken and people couldn't have a basic level of material comfort." Cosmic stakes as a license to neglect the present is an argument shape he recognizes from the inside.

> "AI risk is string theory for computer programmers. It's fun to think about, interesting, and completely inaccessible to experiment given our current technology."

The strongest inside-view charge. It continues: "You can build crystal palaces of thought, working from first principles, then climb up inside them and pull the ladder up behind you." People who can do this, he argues, "are the wrong people to be running a culture."

> "There's a feedback loop between how intelligent people imagine a God-like intelligence would behave, and how they choose to behave themselves."

The "AI cosplay" argument: Bostrom's takeover checklist as a "quasi-sociopathic" to-do list that believers themselves work from, rationalists calling their fellow humans NPCs, Sam Altman doing "secret behind-the-scenes political work to swing the election," an "industry full of rich dudes who think they are Batman." Written in 2016 as satire; read in 2026 as early reportage.

> "I think our understanding of the mind is in the same position that alchemy was in in the seventeenth century."

The closing humility. The alchemists were diligent, partly right (corpuscles), and missing one decisive instrument (mass balance) while fixating on a neurotoxic false lead — "in that way, it resembles our current thought experiments with superintelligence." The real ethics were never in the sci-fi: "how people can exploit other people, or through carelessness introduce immoral behavior into automated systems."

## Key Themes

- #concept **Memetic hazard** — the talk's frame: an idea that is less *true* than *contagious*, and whose hosts are disproportionately the smart and the persuadable.
- #concept **Inside vs. outside view** — arguments must survive engagement on their merits *and* a look at the form and context of the people making them.
- #concept **Intelligence is not a scalar** — "general intelligence" may not be a quantity that can be maximized; it may be a tradeoff; and intelligence doesn't confer control (Hawking's cat, Einstein's cat, the Emu War).
- #concept **Orthogonality, contested** — against the paper clip maximizer: "Complex minds are likely to have complex motivations"; the maximizer would spend its time "writing poems about paper clips."
- #concept **Civilizational intelligence** — the Gilligan's Island argument: intelligence is distributed across civilization and culture, not packed into individual heads, so a lone supermind still can't rebuild technology from nothing.
- #person **Dramatis personae** — Cegłowski against Nick Bostrom and Eliezer Yudkowsky (whose Coherent Extrapolated Volition he reads as the deliverable: "How's that for a design document? Now go write the code"), with Sam Altman and the plutocrat hiring coders to hack the simulation as specimens, and Joi Ito quoted as the in-industry critic.

## Critical Analysis

**What aged well.** The "Argument From Actual AI" is the most vindicated move in the talk: progress has meant "pouring absolutely massive amounts of data into relatively simple neural networks," and his Data Hunger consequence — maximum surveillance as an AI-development strategy — is now the industry's business model, from the "Orwellian microphones" of Google Home to always-on agents. His closing reframe (the pressing ML ethics are exploitation, carelessness and power, not machine self-awareness) became mainstream within a decade. And the cosplay feedback loop reads less like satire every year — the line from his Sam Altman specimen to [[The Education of the Broligarchy]]'s arrested canon is nearly straight.

**What aged badly.** "There's no place to recursively tweak to make them 'better', short of retraining on even more data" was true of 2016-era classifiers and false almost immediately after: fine-tuning, RLHF, synthetic data and distillation are precisely recursive-tweak loops, and harness-level self-improvement is now a research programme (see [[Harness Engineering for Self-Improvement]]). No hard takeoff resulted, so his cultural diagnosis survives — but the impossibility claim does not. The Argument From Complex Motivations is weaker still: a decade of specification gaming shows simple objectives in complex systems reliably producing alien, spec-gaming behavior — the alarmists' *premise*, observed, minus their apocalypse.

**Where the talk undercuts itself.** The outside view is a bias corrector, not an argument: run against the web in 1995 or deep learning in 2012, "the form and the context" gives the wrong answer both times. A heuristic that dismisses novel claims by their dress cannot distinguish a new idea from a cult, and Cegłowski offers no way to make that distinction — only the hope that you'll leave "a little dumber than you started it." The satire compounds the problem: emus and butter robots are *persuasive*, and persuasion is exactly the faculty he says cannot be trusted.

**What is durable.** The alchemy ending. Whatever happens with AI, the claim that theory of mind lacks its mass balance — that we cannot yet frame the scientific questions, don't know which clues are false leads, and massively underestimate some complexities — undercuts confident doom and confident dismissal alike. His fair-minded reconstruction of the six premises keeps the talk from being merely funny, and his refusal of both Raptures (up and down) is what makes it worth rereading a decade on. The [[AI Superforecasters]] observation that a forecasting model does not game resolution criteria is a good example of the dry, testable claim the talk kept asking for instead of teardrop oceans.

## Connections

- This talk is the 2016 first draft of [[Why We Fear AI]]'s core move — fear of future AI displaces the harms already operating — and strengthens that account's history with a pattern it lacks: the communist-era use of cosmic stakes to excuse present squalor. Where Blix and Glimmer locate the displaced fear in capitalism, Cegłowski locates it in a memetic hazard; both land on the same present-tense ethics.
- It complicates [[The Metamorphosis of Prime Intellect]]'s place in the discourse: the novel's analysis names "extinction risk (Bostrom, Yudkowsky)" as one pole of modern AI debate, and this talk is that pole's most quotable refutation — yet the talk's prescription, better sci-fi with Eastern-bloc humility about Utopia (Lem, the Strugatskys), describes exactly what Williams's novel does. Fiction and critique bracket the same question, neither convinced by the other.
- It backdates [[The Education of the Broligarchy]] by a decade: Smith explains the adolescent canon that produced tech's leaders, while Cegłowski observed the resulting behavior in 2016 — grandiosity, megalomania, NPCs, "rich dudes who think they are Batman" — before the tech-politics moment made it obvious. The feedback loop he named is the mechanism Smith's taste critique explains.
- It attacks the premises beneath [[Meeseeks Alignment]] from below: SMTM inverts instrumental convergence and specification gaming; Cegłowski denies the orthogonality thesis underneath them, arguing complex minds get complex motivations (the butter robot's despair, not the maximizer's conquest). Production misbehavior since 2016 favors SMTM's premises — while leaving his cultural diagnosis intact. Fittingly, both quote Rick and Morty, from opposite ends of the argument.
