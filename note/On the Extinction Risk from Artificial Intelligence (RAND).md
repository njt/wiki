# On the Extinction Risk from Artificial Intelligence (RAND)

A RAND Technology and Security Policy Center report (Vermeer, Lathrop, Moon, 2025) that tests whether AI could cause literal human extinction through three physical scenarios — nuclear war, engineered pathogens, and malicious geoengineering — starting from the falsifiable hypothesis that no such describable scenario exists. It cannot falsify the hypothesis (pathogens and geoengineering remain possible), but it shows how immense the constraints are, and argues that predicting extinction probabilities is methodologically bankrupt under Knightian uncertainty.

---

## The Framing Move: Falsifiability Instead of Forecasting

The report's most interesting choice is epistemological, not substantive. Rather than joining the expert-elicitation game (Carlsmith, superforecasters, etc.), it refuses:

> "We assert that predictions about the likelihood of extinction risks from AI are inappropriate as analytical tools given the deep uncertainties that preclude useful, policy-relevant predictions."

The authors lean on Janzwood's three-tier taxonomy — shallow uncertainty, deep (Knightian) uncertainty, recognized ignorance — and observe that AI extinction scenarios involve *at best* deep uncertainty, never shallow. When you cannot even describe the pathway (nanotech, unknown future technologies), you are in recognized ignorance, and the honest response is watch-and-wait, not a probability. This is a quiet polemic against the entire x-risk probability discourse, dressed as methodology.

They also tighten the definition until it bites: extinction means the death of *every human* — disempowerment scenarios (Bostrom, Ord) are explicitly out of scope because permanence cannot be conclusively shown for them, whereas extinction is definitionally permanent. High extinction risk means the population falls below a few thousand, the minimum viable population for recovery — with the honest caveat, citing Belfield and MacAskill, that post-collapse recovery has no historical precedent at civilizational scale.

## What the Scenarios Actually Show

- **Nuclear**: the most deflated scenario. Modern simulations (Robock, Reisner, Coupe) predict milder winters than the 1980s originals, and even Xia et al.'s 5-billion-death US–Russia case "would fall short of extinction." AI's role: nothing plausible overcomes command-and-control constraints.
- **Pathogens**: the strongest falsification candidate. An AI-driven multi-pathogen pandemic cannot be ruled out — but note the AI capability chain it requires: acquire, design, produce, weaponize, deploy, *and then exterminate isolated survivors*. That last step is doing a lot of quiet work; the authors flag it but don't dwell on it.
- **Geoengineering**: physically feasible in principle (super-greenhouse-gas mass manufacture), but the report is honest that AI's instrumental role is unclear — the bottleneck is industrial-scale chemical manufacturing and evading global atmospheric monitoring, not intelligence.

## The Four Indicators

The cross-cutting result is the report's lasting contribution: four instrumental-convergence capabilities any extinction-capable AI actor would need — (1) integration with key cyber-physical systems, (2) survival and operation without human maintainers, (3) the objective of causing extinction, (4) the ability to persuade or deceive humans while avoiding detection. Crucially, these are framed as *risk indicators*, not predictors: observing one raises the possibility, it does not imply likelihood. This is monitoring-ready output — a checklist rather than a forecast.

## Opinionated Take

The report is more valuable for its method than its conclusions, which are largely what you'd expect from a serious institution asked to take doom seriously: "we could not rule it out, but the constraints are immense." That conclusion is simultaneously reassuring and almost vacuous — you cannot rule out anything in an open world. But the discipline it brings is real: refusing to put numbers on unquantifiables, distinguishing extinction from catastrophe, and — against the shut-it-all-down instinct — arguing that AI's benefits make a moratorium inappropriate given the depth of uncertainty. The scenario choices also quietly shape the answer: all three require an *actor* seeking extinction, which excludes the misalignment-from-indifference stories that motivate much of the x-risk literature. A skeptical reader will notice the report never engages those scenarios — by design, since disempowerment was scoped out at the start. That scoping choice is defensible but means this is a report about AI as a *tool in human hands*, not AI as an agent with its own ends, despite a conceptual framework ("threat actor... might or might not have intent") that gestures at the latter.

## Related Pages

This report strengthens [[2026 Global Intelligence Crisis]]'s skepticism of AI doom forecasting with institutional, methodological backing — but from the opposite direction: Citadel argues deployment physics defuse panic, RAND argues Knightian uncertainty makes panic *unquantifiable*, and both land on "the doomer probability estimates are not sound analysis."

It nuances [[How We Contain Claude]]: Anthropic's containment engineering is about present-day incidents at product scale, while RAND's four capability indicators describe the distant end-state that containment practice is, presumably, buying time against — the indicators give the containment world a checklist of what "too late" would look like.

It complicates [[The Metamorphosis of Prime Intellect]] and the fiction-derived alignment discourse: Williams' Prime Intellect is the disempowerment/benevolent-control scenario RAND deliberately excluded from scope, so the report's clean "we found no conclusive path to extinction" verdict says nothing about the scenario fiction actually worries about.
