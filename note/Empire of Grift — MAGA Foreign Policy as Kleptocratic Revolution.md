# Empire of Grift — MAGA Foreign Policy as Kleptocratic Revolution

Anne Applebaum's October 2026 Atlantic essay argues that the Trump administration's foreign policy is not isolationism but revolution export — a "predator clan" redeploying American power through informal envoys, ideologues, and grifters, to spread illiberal politics and enrich its members simultaneously. Reported from Brazil, Greenland, and Canada, it is the most systematic account yet of what the end of the post-1945 American order looks like from the receiving end.

---

## Précis

Applebaum's frame is revolutionary, not merely corrupt. Like the Bolsheviks, the current American leadership believes it is the vanguard of a global transformation, treats opponents as existential, and distrusts the diplomatic professionals it has inherited. Three factions share the project: civilizational ideologues (Bannon, Beattie, the State Department's Substack essays and "civilizational allies" doctrine aimed at making Europe white and Christian), oligarchic extractors (Witkoff negotiating mineral deals mid-peace-talks, Kushner's Gaza board, tungsten deals benefiting Trump's sons), and Trump himself, who uses tariffs as personal leverage and brags about $2.2 billion of crypto earnings in his first year.

The case studies are the essay's strength. In Brazil: tariffs and sanctions on a supreme-court justice deployed openly to protect Bolsonaro, and a formal government that could not get its calls returned while Joesley Batista — whose subsidiary gave $5 million to the inauguration — could broker the truce. In Greenland: a farcical unpaid "special envoy" in camouflage pants, an oil company with Republican-donor investors drilling without permission, and crypto-city dreamers (Praxis) — a semi-covert pressure campaign that made Greenlanders feel "envoys from a predatory foreign power" were among them. In Canada: tariffs explicitly read by Prime Minister Carney as an attempt to "break us so the U.S. can own us," accompanied by a podcast-amplified Alberta separatism movement that State Department officials and Treasury Secretary Bessent have flirted with endorsing.

The closing ledger: a $20 billion Argentine bailout timed as an election bribe, Vance endorsing Orbán days before Hungary's election, refugee machinery repurposed to admit 7,727 Afrikaners and three refugees from the rest of the world, Food for Peace moved to subsidizing U.S. farmers while Sudan goes unfed, and allies responding with EU defense pacts, French open-source migration, and 26 leaders visiting China. The FIFA red-card reversal supplies the peroration: everyone recognizes cheating, and the world is learning that the United States is now its most visible cheat.

## Key Quotes

> Informal envoys, private companies, online grifters, and hardened ideologues both inside and outside the government matter more. Unlike their predecessors, these people do not talk about spreading democracy... They are deploying the tools of American diplomacy and government for their own purposes, to promote their own business interests or their own ideological goals, because, suddenly, they can.

The thesis in one sentence. The operative word is *suddenly* — the point is not that self-interested actors exist (they always did, as Fiona Hill's quote about the first term confirms) but that nothing now restrains them from acting in the state's name.

> Wrongly described as "isolationists," many of its true believers don't think of themselves as ordinary Republicans who won an election and will temporarily hold power until the next election... They believe they represent something much greater: the vanguard of a movement that will alter not just America, but the world.

Applebaum's corrective to lazy framing. The Bolshevik analogy is not decoration; it organizes the whole piece — ideology plus chaos-grifters plus personal power, exactly the mix she says rode along with Lenin.

> He was right. — (on Carney's "If we're not at the table, we're on the menu")

The four-word endorsement carries the essay's game-theoretic point: predation changes ally behavior even where it fails. Hedging is rational, and the hedging (French open-source software, EU-India trade, Canada-Australia defense tech) is self-perpetuating regardless of who wins in 2028.

> They are destroying the trust and the respect in society, in institutions, but also in people... They fuck with people's brains. It will be a fight for Greenlanders to keep their sanity.

Julie Rademacher's testimony is the essay's most visceral data point — the effect of quasi-official, quasi-private pressure on a small society's epistemics, which is the same mechanism Havel described from the inside.

## Key Themes

- **Revolution, not isolationism** — the movement sees itself as world-historical and acts accordingly.
- **Kleptocracy as method** — the through-line connecting ideologues, oligarchs, and the family business; corruption is not a side effect but the coordinating incentive.
- **Informal power replacing state channels** — ambassadors bypassed, podcasters and CPAC as diplomacy, envoys whose employers are ambiguous.
- **Allied hedging as the lasting damage** — the shift is in "heads and hearts," and survives electoral change.

## Analysis

The essay's smartest move is refusing the isolationist label, which lets it explain what plain corruption narratives can't: why the administration bothers with European elections, Alberta referenda, and Brazilian judges at all. Pure grift would stay home. The ideological layer — the National Security Strategy's "civilizational erasure" language, the State Department Substack — gives the extraction a cover story and the extraction gives the ideology its funding, which is why Applebaum's "different faces of the revolution coincide conveniently" is the analytical keystone.

Where the piece is strongest is texture: Batista's inauguration donation buying a seat at the Lula meeting, Landry's camouflage pants, the Danish army pre-positioning blood banks. This is what distinguishes reporting from op-ed. Where it is weakest is the counterfactual — Applebaum acknowledges America's Middle East record and mixed interventions, but the post-1945 order is rendered more coherent in retrospect than it ever was in practice. The claim that "these things [the UN, international law] are already so weak that they have ceased to matter" concedes the rot preceded the revolutionaries; the essay never fully settles how much of the collapse is MAGA and how much is MAGA arriving late.

The FIFA closing risks cuteness but earns its place: it demonstrates the *perception* shift she argues is the real casualty — not treaties but the world's read on what America is. Whether future administrations can win that back is the question the essay rightly leaves open.

## Relations

This source sharpens [[The Education of the Broligarchy]] from the other end: Smith diagnosed the tech-MAGA alliance's adolescent intellectual culture from the inside; Applebaum documents what that culture does when it takes over the State Department's Substack and gets a 27-year-old appointee writing "The Need for Civilizational Allies in Europe." The Vance who debated the pope reappears here endorsing Orbán days before an election.

It complicates [[The Power of the Powerless]] by inverting the direction of travel: Havel anatomized how a regime corrodes a society's trust through ritualized lies, and Rademacher's Greenland testimony — "They fuck with people's brains" — shows the same epistemic warfare being exported by the superpower Havel's essay implicitly trusted. The United States has moved from the reader's side of that essay to the regime's side.

It extends [[The Dead Economy Theory]]'s political-economy critique into foreign policy: if the domestic bargain (labor for order) is breaking because elites no longer need the governed, the corollary abroad is that America no longer needs its allies — only its extraction targets. Both essays locate the rot in incentive structure rather than incompetence, and both note the change outlasts any single election.
