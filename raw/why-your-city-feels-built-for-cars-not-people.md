And it’s not an exaggeration to say that it has changed how I view the challenge of moving city dwellers around. The book argues that space – rather than policy or politics – is the most significant limitation for transport in urban landscapes. It focuses on the five major modes of transportation in US cities – pedestrian, micromobility (including bikes), car, train, and transit – and shows that each mode has its own geometry; a physical footprint that it needs in order to operate safely. The book visualizes these geometries through hundreds of scale drawings and illustrations, backed up by thorough research and real-world examples of how transport networks fit into urban design.Transportation infrastructure will always take up space. The faster a vehicle travels, the wider its turning circle needs to beDavid Copeland Loredo / Princeton University PressThe authors – Christof Spieler, David Copeland-Loredo, and Mandi Chapa – have spent years working together on planning projects across the US. Spieler is an engineer and now Director of Transportation for Madison, Wisconsin, after a long stint in Houston; Copeland-Loredo is a Houston-based planner and illustrator; Chapa is a planner trained in architecture who teaches urban transportation at Rice University. Transportation and the Shape of Cities is predominantly written for planners, engineers, architects, and other professionals in urban design and city management. But it is packed with information that would appeal to an average city dweller who’s curious about their surroundings… which is exactly how I view my readers. So, last week, I hopped on a call with the three authors, to discuss their work, the book, and the challenges of building cities that offer transport options.Where did the focus on the geometry of various transport networks come from?"We''ve worked together on a bunch of different planning projects in very different contexts,” Spieler said. “Over and over again, you end up in the situation where you have the wish-list of what everybody wants, and then you try drawing it out and realize it doesn''t fit. That is such a common pattern that it led to the realization that space is the most precious thing we have in the city." Chapa pointed to her and Copeland-Loredo''s shared background in architecture – "I think the fact that in our work we take up space, we build has a lot to do with our view" – while Spieler, an engineer, said his instinct was “to look for constraints."That framing extends to what Spieler thinks we get wrong most often: "I think most people actually underestimate how much space transportation takes. And they also overestimate how much flexibility there is with that. There''s always this feeling, if you could just be a little more clever, you can figure this out. Copeland-Loredo, originally brought in to illustrate class handouts, ended up producing all of the book''s visual material – "over 500 illustrations or photos," by his estimate. That visual-first approach was a deliberate choice; a way to bring complex topics to life, to show “everything as physical objects that take up space”, and to support the written text.For Spieler, one of their priorities was to ensure that all of the transport modes were discussed equally, “to treat all of these modes with the same rigor,” including the pedestrian, all-too-often left out of the technical language reserved for cars. " Cars are always treated with rigor – the idea that this has a turning radius and a lane width is built into conversations," Spieler said. "But pedestrians are also mechanical objects that move in space. They have every bit as much a requirement for space or for slope as other modes.” That rigor produced surprising findings, even for the authors. Spieler learned from Chapa how rigid parking garage geometry is, meaning garages end up dictating the shape of developments around them. Copeland-Loredo offered a comparison that stuck with me: "A parked car might get a 9x18 or a 9x20 space in a garage. Meanwhile, in our offices, we might get an 8x8 space, meaning you could fit two of us in the space one of our cars takes up."
