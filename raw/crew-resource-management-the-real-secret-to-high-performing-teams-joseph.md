The real secret to high-performing teams — Joseph Pelrine, Craft 2025

1. Key points — High performance is an environment problem, not a talent problem

Talent ≠ team. Stack up skilled individuals and "they act like a bunch of idiots" (his example: the Swiss national football team). Like functional vs. non-functional requirements, teams need technical and non-technical skills — and it's the non-technical ones that make the team perform. HR keeps hiring for technical skill because it can't test for team fit, so Pelrine's answer is to build the environment instead.

Three failure modes, from psychological research: human factors (cognitive overload), fixation errors, and communication errors. Error rates: one mistake every ~30 minutes on routine unstressed tasks, every 5 minutes on complex unstressed ones, every 30 seconds under stress. He demoed overload live with a Stroop test (color-word conflict, plus language-switching): "95% of people make mistakes. The other 5% are liars."

Overload → tunneling → fixation. Under cognitive load you narrow focus and "keep doing more of the same." He cited Google research that developers debug the wrong thing 50% of the time.

CRM is the fix. Crew Resource Management — developed by NASA, proven in aviation and medicine — is a set of ground rules for action and interaction that lets technical skill survive stress. He's applied it in helicopter rescue, with Lufthansa, Formula 1 (Ferrari), and Swiss champion football.

Invert the psychological-safety dogma. Everyone recites psychological safety as the prerequisite; CRM treats it as the result: ground rules → trust (an interpersonal construct) → psychological safety emerges in the culture. Grounded in Lewin's B = f(P,E): change the environment, not the person. Culture is "a derivative property" — you can't change it directly.

XP is already CRM. Extreme Programming is a set of interaction ground rules, not a technical-skills spec. His best XP team, under stress, turned the practices "up to 11 or 12" instead of letting them slip — because trust meant nobody had to check anybody.

Systems beat blame. The person-centered view of error produces fear and punishment; the systems approach treats "human error as a consequence, not a cause." Via James Reason's Swiss cheese model, high-performing teams ask not "how do we get better" but "how can we make it systematically more difficult for these errors to become an incident?" Blaming is easier — which is why most organizations choose it.

Match countermeasures to failure mode: fixation → 10-for-10 + FOR-DEC; communication → closed-loop protocols (SBAR, challenge-response); human factors → systemic barriers (his Swiss pharmacy example adds ~1 minute and closes the error path).

The stakes: "70% of production problems come from human factors, not tech errors." CRM isn't just for crises — it's for performance.

Adoption: don't memorize 15 rules; pick one habit, review it in retrospectives, and reframe the retro itself — not "who's to blame" but "how did our system defenses mess up?"

3. Tools, practices, and methodologies

Crew Resource Management (CRM) — NASA-originated ground rules for non-technical team interaction, proven in aviation and medicine. Adopt as team-level behavioral ground rules so technical work survives stress; start with one practice, not all fifteen.

The 15 CRM practices (his downloadable checklist, via QR code) — know your environment; watch what you're focusing on; accept roles and a leader (official or not); communicate clearly and precisely; speak up; support others; use all available information; cross-check and challenge; never assume; keep re-evaluating; anticipate and plan ahead; call for help early (the ego problem in software); distribute workload; use checklists and memory aids; set priorities dynamically. Review adoption in retrospectives.

Stroop test — classic cognitive-overload demo (say the color a word is printed in while the word names a different color; he added language-switching). Use it live so a team feels why stress produces errors.

James Reason's Swiss cheese model — errors become incidents only when the holes in successive systemic defenses line up. Use it to design multiple, moving barriers rather than one fix.

Person-centered vs. systems approach to error — diagnostic frame: "bad people" thinking yields fear and punishment; the systems approach traces error to causes (fatigue, hunger, workplace design, team problems) and fixes those.

10-for-10 principle — anti-fixation: stop for 10 seconds, breathe, then discuss with the team what you'll do for the next 10 minutes or until something changes.

SBAR — medical handoff protocol: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation. For structured, closed-loop status communication.

Challenge-response (aviation) / order-repeat (kitchens) — closed-loop verification: one person states, the other reflects it back, the first confirms. Installable in standups, handoffs, deploys.

McGrath's four team functions (he credits McGrath and Larson) — teams must (1) surface and get new ideas accepted, (2) build shared situation awareness, (3) decide between alternatives, (4) divide and execute work. Every CRM practice maps to one of these, with verbal and nonverbal communication underneath.

Lewin's B = f(P,E), extended to teams — to change team behavior, change the environment, not the people.

Pair-switching after 30 minutes of debugging — his software-specific anti-tunneling tactic.

Sunset-date experiments (his Kent Beck pattern) — "let's try this for a sprint," with an explicit end date, then review and let the team decide. "You can't force people to do things. But you can hold them to their commitment."

Lufthansa crew mixing — never fly with the same crew twice: levels the playing field and forces the team to function with strangers; a structural alternative to team-building.

Recurrent simulation training — airline crews drill 1–2 weeks a year; ground rules decay without rehearsal.

4. Unanswered questions and omissions — Holes in the Swiss cheese

How to hire for non-technical skills. He diagnoses HR's failure ("they don't know how to test for people who work well in a team") but never offers an assessment method. The hiring half of the problem is untouched.

Evidence. The "70% of production problems come from human factors" and Google's "50% of debugging on the wrong thing" are asserted without sources. Every success story is from a life-critical domain; no data on CRM's effect in software teams specifically.

The transfer problem. He concedes the 15 rules are "set up for medicine" and aviation's differ — but never shows a software-adapted set. What does "trust each other with your life" mean when the stakes are a sprint?

The psychological-safety inversion is asserted, not demonstrated. No mechanism or longitudinal case showing ground rules producing psychological safety in a software organization; "culture is the first derivative" does the work an argument should do.

Leadership legitimacy. CRM requires accepting a leader — but asked what to do when the leader isn't recognized by the team, he punted: "issues that will have to be dealt with outside of that." His objection to military metaphors ("we are not in the military... you cannot be shot for insubordination") is fair, but he offers no replacement model for contested authority. Aviation has a captain whose life is on the line; most software teams don't.

SAFe, explicitly dodged. Asked whether CRM applies beyond XP to SAFe, he declined to answer. The scaled-framework question went unanswered.

Remote and distributed teams. Completely absent. The practices — 15-minute briefings, closed-loop verbal protocols, reading nonverbal cues — assume co-location and high-bandwidth interaction.

Cost and time to adopt. Lufthansa invests "a week or two every year" of simulation per crew. Nothing on what adoption realistically costs a software team, or how long before habits stick.

Failure modes of systemic interventions. His own reindeer example partially failed — drivers mistook reflective antlers for pedestrians — a perfect lesson in unintended consequences that went unextracted.

Measurement. No metrics, no leading indicators for whether the ground rules are actually working.

The individual. A psychologist's talk with no psychology of individuals: nothing on personality, selection, or when to remove someone (the "get him out of there" joke gestures at it and moves on).

Existing software-industry practice. No engagement with Google's Project Aristotle, SRE blameless postmortems, or incident-analysis culture — work that already occupies this ground. And the promised story about his high-stress XP team ("I don't have the time to talk about it") never gets told.
