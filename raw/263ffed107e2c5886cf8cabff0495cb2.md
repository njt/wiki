Mind Your P's and Queues — Daniel Vacanti, Craft 2025

1. Key Points: Priority Sets the Start Line; Queues Set the Finish Line

Prioritization optimizes the wrong moment. The Scrum Guide demands an ordered backlog to "maximize value" (he stresses: maximize, not optimize). But value is only realized when something is delivered to the customer. So if you care about maximizing value, what you actually care about is the order in which items finish — yet prioritization only determines the order in which items start. "Doesn't that seem backward?"

Agile frameworks rest on two unverified assumptions: that items which start sooner finish sooner, and that items roughly finish in the order they started. Vacanti has never seen an organization check this — they just assume the prioritized list is doing something.

Queues — not priority — dictate finish order. The one thing "no Agile framework talks about at all": how many things you're working on at any given time. That, not the backlog order, influences when work gets done.

Queues are everywhere and invisible. Every board column ("up next," "analyzing," "creating," "validating"), every level of the work breakdown structure — portfolio, initiative, feature, team boards — is a queue.

WIP explodes across levels without anyone noticing. A team pulling one story each from features A through E looks disciplined at the story level but has actually pulled five features into progress. Multiply by "10, 20, 50 teams doing exactly the same thing" and you get "an explosion of work in progress across your organization."

The Monte Carlo proof. The customer's fundamental question is "When will it be done?" Monte Carlo simulation can attach completion probabilities to features against a release date — but only under two assumptions that "never happen": one thing at a time, and strict priority order. Model WIP=2 and priority 9 already loses to priority 10. At WIP=9 (of 18 features), priority #1 has a lower completion chance than priorities 2 through 15. With everything in progress, "stories not assigned to a feature" beats the #1 priority — nine features beat #1.

Therefore: prioritization is waste. Once WIP is out of control, finish order is dictated by the number of items in progress, not by priority. The snowball: screw priority → no point planning → no predictability → no idea what products to invest in across the portfolio. Every agile "P word" bottoms out in queue size.

Q&A extensions: team size is a red herring — "you can have a team of 60 people as long as you're not working on 60 things"; the Agile Manifesto has no language around WIP or flow, and its "biggest perversion" is that Scrum and SAFe have become synonymous with agile; Lean and agile are not either/or.

3. Tools, Practices, and Methodologies

Monte Carlo simulation — Given a fixed release date and your story counts, produces a probability of completion per feature. Use it to answer "how many of these features will make that date?" — but model your actual WIP, because the naive version assumes one-at-a-time and strict priority order.

Limiting WIP / controlling queue size at every level — Cap items in progress not just at story level but feature, initiative, and portfolio level. This is the talk's central prescription: queue size, not priority, determines finish order and predictability.

Discovering your optimal capacity — The number of things your organization can genuinely work on simultaneously. "Maybe it's one thing, maybe it's two things. I guarantee you it's probably not 18 things." Use it as the basis for WIP limits; be careful at "two, three, four or more."

Start-order vs. finish-order correlation check — His challenge to every audience: match the order in which items finish against the order they started. No organization he's visited has ever done it; it's the empirical test of whether your prioritization is doing anything.

Board columns read as queues — Treat "up next," "analyzing," "creating," "validating" as queues whose size you watch. The visualization you already have is a queue map you're currently ignoring.

Little's Law (Dr. John Little's papers) — His recommended entry point for learning queuing theory; there is, he says, no good agile queuing-theory book — and "I kind of think there shouldn't be."

Variation literature: Shewhart, Deming, Wheeler — Recommended for understanding variation, the underpinning of why forecasts work.

His books, in read order 2, 1, 3 — When Will It Be Done? (how to forecast), Actionable Agile Metrics for Predictability (why forecasts work), and the third book (both "to the next level"). "I wanted to make it really, really difficult for all of you."

"The New Agile Economics" (talk) — His deeper answer on where the Agile Manifesto breaks down; find it on the interwebs via Google.

Lean + Agile combined — Not a switch. Conferences used to be "Lean Agile Something" until Lean got dropped from the titles; figure out how to combine both for your context.

Named but not endorsed: WSJF (SAFe's version dismissed as "not a thing"); Flight Levels and OKRs (asked about directly, he declined: "I really don't know. I'm sorry.").

4. Unanswered Questions and Omissions

How do you find optimal capacity? He insists "you will need some understanding of what that optimal capacity is" — then never says how to discover it. No heuristic, no signal, no method.

How do you limit WIP across 50 teams? The diagnosis is organizational (the WIP explosion), but the prescription is never scaled: who sets portfolio-level limits, how they're enforced, what happens when demand exceeds capacity.

What replaces prioritization? If "prioritization is waste" and "choose whatever order you want to, doesn't matter," what does a product owner do all day? Does priority matter again once WIP is controlled? The interaction is never addressed — and neither is the implication for Scrum's entire product-owner role.

The obvious fix for his own example is never stated. The team pulled one story per feature; the implied remedy — finish feature A before starting feature B — is left for the audience to infer.

Monte Carlo mechanics deliberately skipped. "We're not going to get into the specifics of Monte Carlo simulation here at all" — no data requirements, no tooling, no explanation of what the completion percentages were computed against.

The SAFe WSJF accusation is never substantiated. He defers it to Q&A ("if anybody wants to talk about that… we can") and the question never comes.

The costs of low WIP are ignored. Idle people, blocked work, dependency-driven parallelism — the audience raised exactly this ("sometimes it makes sense to work on two or more things at a time because they are closely related") and got "be careful as you start getting into those higher numbers."

Model, not evidence. The damning reorderings come from a simulation he built, not from before/after data in a real organization. He chides everyone for never verifying start/finish correlation — but doesn't present that verification himself.

"Maximize not optimize" is flagged and dropped. He makes a point of the Scrum Guide's word choice, then never explains why the distinction matters.

A self-undermining admission goes unchased. "Only when we deliver to the customer do we actually know if something's valuable or not" — if true, no upfront value ranking (his or Scrum's) is possible at all. He doesn't follow the thread.

The other P-words got skipped. Tech-setup delays cut the talk short; planning, predictability, portfolio, and product appear only as one-liners in the snowball argument, never developed.

No Monday-morning advice. Nothing on how to walk into a WIP-drowning organization and start — no first step, no change management, no counter to the manager who demands "utilization."
