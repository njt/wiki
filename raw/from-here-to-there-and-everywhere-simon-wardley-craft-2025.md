1. Key Points — Strategy Is Observation, Not Storytelling

Most "strategy" is copied narrative and magic frameworks. Wardley opens by confessing that as CEO of Fotango (16 lines of business, ~10M users) he copied strategy statements from other companies and "changed a few words." SWOT diagrams can't tell Themistocles where to defend the Hot Gates; only a map — position and movement over a space of meaningful options — can. Mind maps, system diagrams, and customer journey maps are all graphs, not maps: they lack an anchor, position, and consistency of movement.

Everything evolves through a common pattern — genesis → custom-built → product → commodity — and its characteristics change as it does. This is the load-bearing insight. It means there is "no such thing as one size fits all methodology": extreme programming/agile for the uncharted left, lean in the middle, Six Sigma/formal methods for the industrialized right. HS2 applied this (in-house agile / off-the-shelf / outsourced) and delivered under budget, praised by the Public Accounts Committee. Outsourcing contracts fail predictably because they try to specify the un-specifiable left side.

Maps depersonalize challenge. "I don't challenge their story, I challenge the map." A 15-minute mapping session killed a multimillion-pound robotics project at an insurance company — the bottleneck existed only because non-standard racks required servers to be physically modified. "These people weren't daft, they were trapped by past narrative." They were optimizing process flow while the system itself had evolved.

Climatic patterns make change anticipatable, not mysterious. Everything evolves; past success breeds inertia (Blockbuster was first with a website, online ordering, and streaming experiments — inertia killed them, not lack of innovation); each industrialization triggers co-evolution of practice (EC2 → DevOps; serverless → FinOps) and a Jevons-paradox explosion of higher-order systems; and you have no choice over evolution — competitors' efficiency forces you. Cloud, serverless, and AI are the same pattern repeating "since the Industrial Revolution."

The real architect is the coder. Architectural diagrams and ERDs are "wishes, intents and beliefs" — "the code is the architecture." Therefore vibe coding is not a tooling choice but an architectural one: you're handing decision-making to the AI. The most important architectural question today: where do you value humans in the decision-making process?

Coding is a craft; testing is engineering. Tests are contextual, composable micro tools with hypothesis-driven, systematic exploration and generated information. Coding uses one monolithic "kitchen blender" everywhere, relies on ad hoc exploration, gut-feel decisions, and manual inspection — while over 50% of the development budget goes to reading code, which "we never discuss" optimizing. The fix: learn from testing and build composable micro tools for coding.

Sovereignty of reasoning is the stakes. Whoever controls the tools, medium, and language controls how you reason — so AI concentration creates "new theocracies." Values (the trolley problem, "gold member" route priority that embeds social inequality into transport) get baked into training data. The counter is openness — including training data — diversity of sources, and critical thinking. And nation-states mostly have no map rooms; China does.

3. Tools, Practices, and Methodologies — The Mapmaker's Kit

Wardley Mapping — A map of your business landscape: user(s) as the anchor, a chain of needs (value chain within an org, supply chain across orgs), and an evolution axis (genesis → custom-built → product → commodity). Use it to observe the landscape, challenge plans without attacking the storyteller, and communicate strategy. Track record cited: a £425M saving on one UK government project; Canonical using one map to take 70% of cloud computing in 18 months on half a million pounds.

Premortem challenge + postmortem learning — Map, challenge what you're doing, execute, then compare what actually changed against the map. This is how the pattern library was built.

Climatic patterns (~30) — Economic "rules of the game" applying seemingly everywhere: everything evolves; past success breeds inertia; co-evolution of practice; no choice over evolution; Jevons-paradox explosions. Use for anticipating change and timing investment (where to invest — and where not to: "best architectural practice" became "legacy").

Doctrine / principles (~40) — Universally useful patterns: know your users then focus on user needs (in that order); know the details (understand the supply chain); understand what is being consumed (standard rack vs. custom rack).

Gameplay (~150) — Patterns for manipulating the market to your favor. The flagship: ILC — Innovate, Leverage, Commoditize, AWS's documented play, found in 17 pages of Wardley's mapping in Reaching Cloud Velocity ("basically how they destroy industries").

Method-matching by evolution stage — XP/agile in-house on the uncharted left (change is cheap), lean/off-the-shelf in the middle, Six Sigma or outsourcing on the industrialized right. Only outsource what can be specified. Proven at HS2 and the NASA/Planet Labs Carbon Mapper satellite.

OODA + Sun Tzu's five factors — Boyd's observe–orient–decide–act overlaid with purpose, landscape, heavens, doctrine, leadership. Mapping is the operationalization of "observe."

TDD as the engineering template — Hypothesis first ("my system doesn't have the new function"), systematic exploration via small discrete tests, information generated from the system (nobody paints in the red/yellow/green), composable micro tests as the development experience. "The best specification of a system is the test suite."

Composable micro tools for coding — Stop using monoliths; build contextual tools for the problem, the way testing already does. Build the tool first, then solve the problem. (Demonstrated in Tudor's follow-up session: "modernizing legacy through thousands of contextual tools.")

The vibe-coding boundary decision — Use a map to partition your system: outsource what you don't care about, vibe code prototypes, do software engineering + AI with code review where it matters. Revisit the boundaries as agentic AI handles more checks and balances. "Architecture is an expression of values" — one of which is where you value human decision-making.

Reading list — Sun Tzu's Art of War (buy two translations); The Flywheel Effect (serverless adoption at an insurance company); Reaching Cloud Velocity (AWS on AWS).

4. Unanswered Questions and Omissions — The Blank Spaces on the Map

How do you actually place components on the evolution axis? The insurance team mapped "compute as a product"; Wardley disagreed and just said "that's okay." The talk never explains how mapping disagreements get resolved or how evolution stage is calibrated — the entire method's accuracy hangs on this.

What do composable micro tools for coding actually look like? The talk's central call to action is deferred entirely to Tudor's next session. Nothing on maintaining thousands of contextual tools, who owns them, tool sprawl, or whether small teams can afford the build-the-tool-first approach.

Who reviews the reviewers? His answer to vibe-coded nuclear control systems is an intelligence-services-run code review company — "Promise you we won't put any backdoors in." The joke lands precisely because the real governance question (whose incentives, what oversight) goes unanswered.

What's the antidote to inertia? Blockbuster is diagnosed beautifully, but a successful incumbent hearing this talk learns no mechanism for detecting their own late-fee-shaped blind spot before it kills them.

"No choice over evolution" is asserted, not argued. Rational fast-following, regulated industries, and cases where waiting is correct are never addressed — the determinism ("never a question of if, only when") is presented as law.

Whose values go into the training data? He shows the trolley problem and gold-member route priority as dangers, then stops. The hard question — the process by which a society decides which values get embedded — is raised and abandoned, along with what DVLA actually did about route management after anticipating it in 2015.

Open training data is prescribed without engagement. Privacy, IP, and licensing complexities are skipped; the security counterargument is dismissed as self-interested lobbyists worried about "Skynet or terror."

The "over 50% of your budget is reading code" claim rests on a show of hands. No data source, and no discussion of how one would optimize code reading — the optimization he says we never discuss remains undiscussed.

Every mapping anecdote is a triumph. 15 minutes, £425M, 70% of cloud. No failure modes of mapping itself: when maps mislead, what mapping a large estate actually costs, or how bad maps do damage.

The 2018 conversational programming system vanishes. Presented as proof of concept (AWS re:Invent, Alexander Simovich), with no adoption story — undercutting the "it's already happening" narrative.

He notices the audience building without tests and moves on. The engineering rigor he preaches has an adoption gap he doesn't touch — and his endorsement of "the test suite is the specification" ignores test rot and coverage theater.

Sovereignty is teased as forbidden — "I'm not allowed to speak about apparently" — and never explained. Who's not allowing, and why, is the talk's most intriguing dangling thread.
