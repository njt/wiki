Evolutionary Architecture: The What. The Why. The How. — Maciej "MJ" Jedrzejewski

1. Key points — The Project Paradox is the disease; current context is the cure

The Project Paradox explains most bad architecture. "We have a lot of decisions to make in the beginning of developing product, but the knowledge that we have about the business domain is minimal. The problem is that when we get that knowledge, we don't have that many technical decisions to make." The remedy: shift technical decisions as far into the future as possible.

The one sentence he wants the audience to keep: "Always choose an architecture based on your current needs and based on your current context. Not a wishful thinking." No building for imaginary million-user futures — but also "don't close the door": keep technical options (e.g., swapping the relational database later) open as long as possible.

Architecture fails in two directions. Too naive: the startup product table that grows to "150 properties" by adding "another column and another column." Too complex: "the apocalypse of Redis, Kafka, Kubernetes, microservices" thrown at problems you don't have. And when reality forces a correction, "we usually react too late."

Evolutionary architecture is a four-chapter lifecycle: Simplicity → Maintainability → Growth → Complexity. Growth is optional — "not all products will come to that point." Chapter transitions are triggered by observed architectural drivers (performance, complexity, maintainability), not by vanity metrics like raw user counts.

Modularity from day one is the cheap insurance that makes evolution possible. Group requirements into highly cohesive areas (appointment scheduling, patient treatment, drug prescription, medical records, invoicing), mirror them as folders, let each use case own everything it needs, and give each module its own database schema. Extraction later becomes literal: "take the content of the folder, extract."

"Leverage what you have" before adding components. Scaling reads: fix indexes and queries → add read replicas → only then consider cache. Async messaging: in-memory queue → Postgres-as-queue with inbox/outbox → message broker only once deployment units actually split.

Deployment topology changes last. Even at 10,000 patients and three teams, "you still have very low user base" — stay in a single deployment unit. Extract a microservice only when teams "step on each other's toes," a module needs different security, or one module dominates (patient treatment: 40% of traffic, most frequently updated).

Chapter four fights complexity in the code, not the topology. The prescription module's problem is an anemic model — "several different services" mutating "our stupid entity" from outside. Event storming reveals the commands, actors, and policies; the aggregate then becomes the "guardian of consistency."

Strategic DDD yes, tactical DDD not at the start. Workshops yield subdomains → distill bounded contexts → draw a context map. "If you skip the strategic part, then you are hacked up." And bounded contexts aren't permanent: a car-leasing company that buys a truck-leasing company needs new boundaries.

Teams follow capabilities. Three teams = split along cohesive areas; "Team A will not work on drug prescription."

2. Pithy and provocative quotes

"Always choose an architecture based on your current needs and based on your current context. Not a wishful thinking." — The single takeaway he'd choose if he could keep only one thing from the talk.

"It's like you would hire the Formula One driver to prepare you a sandwich." — His test for high cohesion: invoicing shouldn't schedule appointments; everyone should do the one thing they're extremely good at.

"Kafka, Kubernetes, Redis, microservices — they are great. But usually we are using them for our imaginary problems." — On the over-complexity apocalypse; the tech isn't wrong, the justification is.

"You might say what the hell, InMemoryQueue, but what if I lose this event? Well, you will need to fix it." — Scale-appropriate pragmatism for a karate-club-sized system.

"Yesterday I had one problem and I added cache to solve it. Now I have two problems." — Why cache is the last scaling lever, after indexes, queries, and read replicas.

"Our aggregate is a guardian of consistency... no business logic is happening around, outside of it, that can change it directly." — The chapter-four fix for the "stupid entity" being mutated by outside services.

"If you skip the strategic part, then you are hacked up." — On jumping straight to aggregates, value objects, and entities without first finding cohesive areas.

"If you put trucks into cars, then you will have a mess." — Why bounded contexts must evolve when the business does (car leasing company acquires a truck leasing company).

"You don't need to be this new kid on the block like hey, let's do this, this, this and that... then on our CV that we worked with everything." — The CV-driven architecture jab in his closing advice.

"I was supposed to tell you a joke about evolutionary architecture by now, but it is still evolving, so I can't." — The talk's only joke, and its thesis in one line.

3. Tools, practices, and methodologies — The pragmatist's toolkit

Reference material

"Master Software Architecture" (his free book, 86 pages) — How to analyze business requirements and transform them into working software; the source of the grouping technique used throughout the talk. "Can be read during one evening."

Evolutionary Architecture by Examples (open-source repo, .NET, with Kamil Grzybek) — Working companion code for the patterns presented.

DDD Crew on GitHub — Free materials on distilling bounded contexts and context mapping; his go-to recommendation for strategic DDD.

Analysis and design

Requirement grouping into cohesive areas — Cluster features by business capability before writing code; this grouping becomes your module boundaries. Test each with the Formula One driver/sandwich heuristic.

Event Storming — Workshop to surface commands, actors, policies, and events; used at the design level to fix the anemic prescription model, and as the first step when rescuing an over-complicated system.

Domain Storytelling — Alternative workshop technique for identifying cohesive areas.

Strategic DDD pipeline: subdomains → bounded contexts → context map — Distill contexts from workshop output; the context map defines relationships between contexts and external systems.

Structure and enforcement

Folder-per-module modular monolith — One project, folders mirroring the business areas; no need for separate projects at the start.

Use-case-first code organization — Each use case folder contains its endpoint, request, and business rules (e.g., "cannot be scheduled without valid insurance"); dropping or extracting a use case means moving one folder.

Architecture tests — which he'd rename "solution structure tests" — Automated tests enforcing module boundaries (e.g., contracts module can't depend on forbidden modules; classes must be sealed). Available for Java and .NET, or "not that tricky to write on your own." He objects to the name because "software architecture is huge."

Schema-per-module logical database split — One database, one schema per module; cross-module data access only via the owning module's public API, never direct schema reads.

Contract extraction — Pull a module's public API interface into a separate project so invoicing references an interface and "knows nothing about drug prescription."

Building blocks as a shared project → versioned package — Exception middleware, API filtering, etc., shared across modules; becomes a NuGet/NPM package with major/minor/patch versioning once deployment units split.

Communication

In-memory queue — Event fan-out for tiny scale; acceptable event loss at karate-club scale.

Database-as-queue (e.g., Postgres) with inbox/outbox patterns — Durable async messaging without new components; "you are leveraging the component that you already have."

Events over direct calls for fan-out — When one module must inform five others, publish one event instead of calling five public APIs; listeners "can duplicate the data on their side... whatever they want."

Message broker (e.g., RabbitMQ) — Becomes necessary only once modules live in separate deployment units.

Scaling

The scaling ladder: indexes/queries → read replicas → cache — Fix the cheap things first; cache last.

Load balancer — The first new component, added when the web application needs multiple instances.

Partitioning and sharding — For write problems at the growth stage.

Code-level complexity

Aggregate (tactical DDD) — Command in → business rule checked inside the object → state change → event out; encapsulates all mutation logic.

Push state handling to the frontend where it belongs — Event storming revealed drug selection didn't need backend state management.

Rescue tactics (from Q&A)

Feature-by-feature decomposition — His preferred fix for an over-complicated system: workshops first, then move features one by one into proper modules.

Tactical forking — Fork the solution, delete everything outside one cohesive area, refactor, reintegrate. "Quite of a hardcore strategy."

Organization

Team-per-business-capability — Split teams along cohesive areas; no cross-team work on the same module.

4. Unanswered questions and omissions — The chapters he didn't write

No trigger metrics. He repeatedly invokes "architectural drivers" (performance, complexity, maintainability) as the reason to move between chapters — but never defines how any of them are measured, what thresholds matter, or who decides. How much performance decrease justifies extracting a microservice? Maintainability is the most-cited driver and the least defined.

The migration path is entirely missing. Every chapter shows the end-state diagram, never the transition. How do you split a shared database, move live medical data, avoid dual-writes, and keep 300 clinics running while extracting patient treatment? The one audience question about mid-flight data migration (to an event store) got explicitly punted: "this is the topic like opening a Pandora box... impossible to answer this question within two minutes."

Event sourcing raised, dodged. His answer was a referral to Oskar Dudycz plus "think if you really need it." Fair triage, but it leaves a hole in a talk about evolving data architecture.

Eventual consistency is hand-waved in the riskiest possible domain. Modules listening to events "can duplicate the data on their side... whatever they want." No sagas, no compensation, no discussion of what stale or duplicated prescription data means in healthcare.

Healthcare's actual hard parts are absent. The domain is chosen for realism, but privacy, audit trails, consent, and integration with external systems (national e-prescription services, insurers) never appear. EU regulation shows up only as a plot device to make a module complex. The context map's "external systems" exist only in the Q&A, not the case study.

The central tension is never resolved. "Not wishful thinking" and "don't close the door" pull in opposite directions: schema-per-module, contract extraction, and kept-open options are themselves speculative structure. Where is the line between healthy optionality and speculative generality? Unaddressed.

Governance under pressure. What stops a team on a deadline from bypassing the schema boundary or the public API? His own renaming — "solution structure tests" — concedes the tests only cover code structure. Nothing covers the database, the runtime, or the human incentive to cheat.

The shared-package problem is raised, then dropped. Versioning the building-blocks package and "maybe you will need another team to support" it are acknowledged as new costs — with zero guidance on managing them.

No engagement with the existing "evolutionary architecture" canon. The title has an established meaning in the field — fitness functions, incremental change as a first-class concern — and none of it appears. The talk is really a modular-monolith evolution narrative under a borrowed name.

The product that never grows. He notes "not all products will come to that point" of extreme growth, but never discusses what the modularization discipline costs a product that stalls at chapter two — or the monetary cost of the load balancers, brokers, replicas, and extra teams the later chapters demand.

How do you notice the drivers degrading in the first place? Observability and monitoring are never mentioned — odd for a methodology whose entire trigger mechanism is "we see that performance decreased."

The 99% SLA is stated and then never used. "Four days downtime for the entire year" is presented as a main consideration in chapter one and plays no role in any subsequent decision — including what happens to availability when you add a load balancer, a broker, and a split database.

Testing across module boundaries. Absent entirely — how do you verify behavior when invoicing depends on drug prescription's events, before and after extraction?
