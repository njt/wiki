Seven Habits of a Mostly Successful Team — Sander Hoogendoorn, Craft 2025

(Yes, "mostly" — his e-commerce employer had a serious outage during its biggest quarterly sale, extended to three days, and he was still repairing it that morning.)

1. Key Points: Technical Death Is an Organizational Disease — the Cure Is Small Steps and Fewer Rules

The diagnosis is "technical death." His 20-year-old deal-site company ended up with an ERP eight versions behind, user accounts stored in the CMS ("CMS systems do not contain user accounts"), every data-syncing mechanism imaginable, and microservices "with all sorts of weird names" on every cloud. Technical death = "your whole team is spending so much time on just maintaining all the software and keeping everything running that there is no time left to innovate."

The blame is always misplaced. "It's never the tech... it's always the organization" — managers, product managers, and marketing demanding the next feature. Monoliths are "okay-ish"; legacy can be beautiful (the Pantheon has functioned for 2,000 years — "my software doesn't do that, by the way"). The cultural fix: "you shouldn't ask for time for refactoring. You should just do it. It's part of your job."

The innovator's dilemma compounds it. A newcomer (he name-checks Temu) with better tech and a better business model arrives, and if you're frozen in your tech stack, you can't reinvent yourself. The only constant is change — so reprioritize weekly and budget for escape.

The seven habits themselves were ChatGPT-generated. He had ChatGPT write the talk description from summaries of his previous talks, then reverse-engineered the talk to fit: prioritize pragmatically, kill complexity, own the work, communicate, build microteams, deliver continuously, have fun.

Prioritization is governance, not backlog grooming. A mission you can actually build software toward ("Europe's leading deal site... surprise them on a daily basis" — not "make more money with higher margins"), a 70/20/10 budget, and a cross-functional Tech Board that kills ideas ruthlessly and shrinks everything else.

Small steps are the only escape from a live system. You can't close the shop and rebuild — it's "continuous renovation," like his bicycle repairman. Gall's law ("a complex system designed from scratch never works") plus Cynefin (most real work is complex/chaotic — unknown unknowns) mean big plans never finish and estimates are fantasy. Only experiments, allowed to fail, get you out.

Autonomy is created by removing rules, not adding process. Dee Hock's principle — simple principles produce intelligent behavior, complex rules produce stupid behavior — applied literally: no Scrum, no sprints, no retros, no estimates, no stories, no product owner, no pull requests, no blame. Like the crossroads where they removed the traffic lights and drivers started looking each other in the eye.

The team, not the individual, owns the software (Charity Majors). With no product owner, everyone becomes one — "product engineers" who walk to the business and build with it directly.

Knowledge sharing is the work. Event storming, pairing, mobs, lean coffee. Hoarding knowledge to stay indispensable is disqualifying.

Microteams + continuous deployment de-risk everything. Anyone picks anything off the boards; small groups self-form per item, ship, disband. A 13-person team releases to production 40–50 times per day. When the next train comes in two and a half minutes, missing one doesn't matter.

Fun doesn't fix motivation. Purpose, mastery (through shared learning), and autonomy do. Outings are nice; they're not a strategy.

Change starts with you. "Your old keys will not open new doors." Not everyone likes change — his mother went to the same campsite in France for 25 years, and that's fine for them — but in this industry, if you stop learning, that's you.

Meta-point, delivered live: his PowerPoint crashed mid-talk and he kept talking without slides — an unplanned demonstration of his own thesis that failure at the worst moment is survivable if you keep moving.

3. Tools, Practices, and Methodologies

The Tech Board — A cross-functional governance board (CTO, COO, CMO, logistics, finance, marketing, plus him) meeting every Thursday at noon over lunch; anyone in the company can pitch ideas. Use it as the gatekeeper for innovation work: kill misaligned ideas immediately, shrink oversized ones, reprioritize almost weekly to absorb change.

The four board questions — (1) Will this help us reach our goals? (2) Can we actually do this — with 13 people, sometimes boring technology wins? (3) Is it small enough? (4) Do we need this now? Use as a kill-filter; anything too big goes back to the pitcher to be split, "and they do, because they want to get their work through the board."

The 70/20/10 split — 70% of time innovating away from the legacy mess toward the dot on the horizon, 20% on small features for existing production systems, 10% keeping the lights on. Why: an explicit budget stops feature pressure from consuming everything.

Someday/maybe column (from Getting Things Done) — A JIRA parking lot for pitches that fail the four questions, "officially revisited every three months. In reality, we never do." Use as a diplomatic garbage bin.

The small-features board — A second JIRA board for production-system work capped at two to five days, governed by "the number twos of every department"; anything bigger escalates to the Tech Board. No exact estimations.

Mission statement / dot on the horizon — A single phrase the team can build software toward. Why: purpose is the only real motivator, and "make more money with higher margins" doesn't qualify.

Cynefin framework (Dave Snowden) — Maps problems into clear / complicated / complex / chaotic zones. Use it to accept that most development work lives in complex/chaotic territory — unknown unknowns — which is why estimates fail and only small steps and experiments work.

Small-step experimentation — Run work as experiments that are allowed to fail; the smaller the experiment, the faster you learn. Rule of thumb: if your steps feel small, make them smaller.

Rule removal (Dee Hock principle) — "Simple, clear purpose and principles give rise to complex and intelligent behavior. Complex rules and regulations give rise to simple and stupid behavior." Strip rules on when, where, how, and with what tools people work (office at most one day a week). "Simplify, because that's when you can amplify."

No-ceremony delivery — No Scrum, sprints, retros, stories, estimates, scrum master, product owner, or pull requests; fewer stand-ups. Why: the team owns work end-to-end as "product engineers" who talk to the business directly.

Event storming (Alberto Brandolini) — Collaborative sticky-note mapping of business processes with the business. His tip: it works even without the full palette of colored stickies.

Pair and mob programming — Optional, not mandatory; people do it because they like it. Use for knowledge sharing.

Lean coffee — Weekly session where team members pitch topics, the group votes, each topic gets five minutes, a bell rings, then extend or move on. Use as the structured-but-democratic knowledge-sharing ritual.

Microteams — Fluid, self-forming groups around each small board item: someone calls out what they're picking up, others join ("I know a little bit more about it, so I'll help you get started"), they build, check in, disband. Why: "no two topics require exactly the same people or basically the same skills."

Continuous deployment — Every small change automatically deployed to production; his 13-person team ships 40–50 times per day. Prerequisite he names: "you need to automate everything" (pipelines etc. — the section he mostly skipped after the crash).

Purpose–mastery–autonomy model (from a book he shows — Pink's Drive model) — Purpose from the dot on the horizon, mastery from sharing knowledge and learning together, autonomy from rule removal. Use as the check that team practices actually feed motivation — outings alone don't.

Vibe coding, with the caveat — Use AI tools, but remember: "everything seems to look faster; that doesn't mean you go faster."

The last resort — "If everything else fails... you remove your node_modules and do an npm install."

4. Unanswered Questions and Omissions

How do you get the organization to accept this in the first place? The whole diagnosis is that managers and product managers cause technical death by demanding features — yet the talk never explains how he persuaded a C-suite to fund 70% of engineering time on escaping the mess instead of shipping features. The Tech Board containing all the managers is presented as a fait accompli, not a battle plan.

What replaces pull requests? He mocks the argument that review "pulls senior resources away from critical, high priority work" and says the team just doesn't do PRs — but with no PRs, no retros, and 40–50 deploys a day, what's the quality gate? The "automate everything" section that presumably covered pipelines and testing was skipped after his PowerPoint crashed. The tester in his team photo exists only as a punchline ("nobody wants to wait for them").

No retrospectives + a serious outage = how does this team learn from failure? He preaches experimentation and learning, then names the outage still being repaired that morning — and never says how the incident gets examined without any retrospective ritual.

What happens to people who can't self-organize? The Joyce story ends with "figure it out for yourself," and he admits autonomy can't be taught ("draw two circles... figure out the owl"). No answer for those who never figure it out, and nothing on hiring or onboarding into a rule-less team.

The 10% "keeping the lights on" just visibly failed. The quarterly sale broke things for days. Is 10% realistic? What's the on-call and incident model for 13 people?

The Someday/maybe bin is a trust problem played for laughs. Colleagues believe rejected ideas get quarterly review; they don't. What happens when the pitchers figure that out?

"No estimates" vs. "max two to five days." The small-features board caps work by duration — that's estimation, just coarser. Who sizes it, and what happens when a "small" item balloons?

Generalizability is asserted, not argued. He's a 40-year veteran, author, and conference headliner whose implicit authority lets him abolish rules and refuse to choose for Joyce. For a mid-level engineer in a low-trust bank (the "basement Java developers" he jokes about), the transfer path is a single slogan: "you ignite the change." That's inspiration, not a method.

Success is never defined. Release frequency is the only metric offered. No uptime, quality, revenue, or retention numbers — "mostly successful" by what measure?

The actual remediation is invisible. We hear about the ERP eight versions behind and the data-sync chaos, but never the recovery trajectory — what got fixed, in what order, and what the habits actually achieved against technical death.

AI is dodged. He cut his prepared AI section entirely ("I'm not going to do that"), leaving one caveat. For a 2025 talk on team habits, what AI does to team size, skills, and hiring is the elephant in the room.

The people who don't want change get a shrug. He says "we need both" the campsite people and the change people — but every habit described assumes everyone opts into autonomy, sharing, and constant learning. The happy basement Java developer has no place in the story, and he never says what to do with them.

Capacity math is never reconciled. Thirteen people doing event storming, weekly lean coffee, mob programming, business-facing product ownership, 40–50 daily releases, and keeping the lights on — the calendar behind that is left as an exercise.
