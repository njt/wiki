Beyond Autonomous Teams – Simon Rohrer | Craft 2026

1. Key Points

"Autonomy" is the wrong word for what teams need. The word comes from autos + nomos — self-law, subject to its own laws. Rohrer argues no team in a complex organization actually wants or should have that. It's a "container concept" — like "sustainability" or "quality" — that people fill with whatever they want, meaning it carries little shared meaning. The German socio-technical tradition is more humble: teilautonom (partially autonomous) or zelfsturend (self-steering). Rohrer prefers agency — capacity to act within clear boundaries — paired with coherence.

"Product" is also the wrong word. Scrum's roots are in the New Product Development Game — about developing brand-new standalone products. In a complicated organization, what a single team delivers is rarely a product. Rohrer's group at Barclays tried "service," then gave up and called it "a banana." His current proposal: every team, team-of-teams, and department is a value center — borrowing from "cost center" but flipping the framing: every team exists because it delivers value.

The shape of your value determines your organizational trade-offs. A public cloud hyperscaler has tiny "atoms" of value (storage, compute, DNS) that can be sold independently — hence two-pizza teams work. A trading platform has one product sold by 80+ teams with a large shared kernel (orders, prices, risk, portfolios). A universal bank is more complex still. This essential coupling — the "molecular value shape" — constrains what your organization can look like and how you trade agency against coherence. You can't DDD or Team Topologies it all away.

Value designs organizations — fractally. Extending Conway's Law (and Sanchez & Mahoney 1996), Rohrer argues it's not just org structure that shapes systems, or products that design organizations — value designs organizations at every level. This is true all the way up and down.

Purpose is what you do, not what you aspire to. Quoting Stafford Beer: "The purpose of the system is what it does." A hospital A&E where 50% of patients leave sicker has the purpose of making people sick — regardless of its mission statement. This holds at every level: a team is what it does.

Strategy and governance flow both ways. Strategy isn't handed down like tablets. Roger Martin's example: a call-center worker feeding complaints back up shapes strategy. Governance sometimes must come from the top (Novo Nordisk's security incident is the cautionary tale — no centralized security policy across autonomous teams led to a shutdown), but platforms function as enabling constraints that let teams do more, not less.

3. Tools, Practices, and Methodologies

Value Centers — Replaces squads/tribes/pods with a single nested concept: every team and grouping at every level is a value center (because it delivers value, not incurs cost). Use one word at every level of the hierarchy to reinforce that every unit exists because it's valuable.

Viable Systems Model (Stafford Beer) — A cybernetic framework Rohrer modernizes into five questions every value center must answer at every level: What value am I delivering? How do we coordinate? How do we fit together? What is out there for us? Who are we? Some are "I" questions (individual/team agency), some are "we" questions (coherence across the bigger unit). Use as a diagnostic lens, not a prescriptive framework.

Better Value, Sooner, Safer, Happier (BVSSH) — A balanced scorecard: Better = quality, Sooner = flow, Safer = security/privacy/minimum viable compliance, Happier = customers/colleagues/citizens/climate. From the book Rohrer co-authored. Use as an outcome-based scorecard rather than measuring activity.

Governing vs. Enabling Constraints — Governing constraints (policy, standards) suit ordered domains where alignment is needed early. Enabling constraints (platforms, shared tooling) suit disordered domains where you cohere later. Use the distinction to decide what to centralize vs. what to let teams choose. The Novo Nordisk incident illustrates what happens when you treat security governance as enabling when it should have been governing.

Essential vs. Accidental Dependencies — Map your dependencies. Some are accidental (silos, unnecessary coupling) and can be removed with contracts, APIs, or decoupling. Some are essential — they are your value shape and won't yield to DDD or Team Topologies. Use this to decide where to invest in decoupling vs. where to invest in coordination mechanisms.

Context Engineering — Rohrer calls this "the most important thing for us to be concentrating on in the world of AI agents." Not elaborated in detail (he says he could do 40 minutes on it), but involves sharing context/skills across levels — individual to team to department to organization. Patrick Debois's work on sharing context at different levels is referenced.

Alistair Cockburn's "Unifying User Stories, Use Cases and Story Maps" — Recommended as "awesome" and "inspirational," particularly for its discussion of the power of verbs and different time horizons (CEOs think in years, teams in days, AI agents in minutes/hours). Use to align thinking across levels of abstraction.

Turn the Ship Around (L. David Marquet) — The "leader-leader" model. Leaders at all levels push decisions down to the level closest to the information. Use as the leadership model paired with value centers.

4. Unanswered Questions and Omissions

What does "coherence" actually look like in practice? Rohrer spends most of the talk arguing against "autonomy" and for "agency plus coherence," but coherence remains somewhat of a container concept itself — he admits this worry ("I worry that even agency and coherence are still container concepts"). Beyond "governing vs. enabling constraints" and "committees," there's little concrete operational guidance for achieving it.

How do you actually map your "shape of value"? The molecular value shapes metaphor is evocative but underspecified. How does a team or leader go about identifying essential vs. accidental coupling? What tools or exercises help? He says "take a step back, look for the accidental and the essential" but doesn't offer a method.

Context engineering is teased but not delivered. Rohrer explicitly says it's "the most important thing" for AI agents but punts on details ("I could do 40 minutes on this"). For a talk that ends up touching heavily on AI agents, this is a significant gap.

The AI agent organizational question is raised but dropped. He asks: "What value is each one of those [AI agent tabs] delivering? How are you bringing those together? How are you making that all coherent?" — then moves on. The implications of agentic development for value-center design, governance, and coherence are left entirely open.

No discussion of power dynamics. The talk frames agency and coherence as a neutral trade-off, but doesn't address who gets to decide the balance, how political dynamics shape that negotiation, or what happens when "coherence" is used as a cover for control. The Q&A touches on "what if people don't want to take responsibility" but the answer is thin ("give them psychological safety").

No failure cases for the value-center model itself. The Novo Nordisk case is used to argue against too much autonomy, but there's no case study of value centers working or failing in practice. The concept is presented as where the work is heading, not as something tested.

The cross-linguistic argument is asserted, not developed. Rohrer notes that German uses teilautonom, Dutch/French/Hungarian use "self-steering," and English uniquely conflates "agency" with "AI agent." This is interesting but the implications aren't explored — does the language we use actually change how teams behave? Or is this just etymological curiosity?

No mention of team size, composition, or lifecycle. For a talk about how teams should be organized, there's nothing on how big a value center should be, how long it should persist, or when it should be restructured. The Conway's Law / value-shapes discussion implies these matter but doesn't address them.
