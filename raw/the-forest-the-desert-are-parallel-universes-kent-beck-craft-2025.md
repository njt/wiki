The Forest & The Desert Are Parallel Universes — Kent Beck, Craft 2025

1. Key Points: You're Not Being Misheard — You're Being Heard in Another Universe

The origin mystery: great software gets you fired. At XP Universe (~2000–2001), Beck counted five teams in the audience that were "spectacularly successful" by their organizations' standards — "more code in less time, with no bugs, happy customers" — and all five were fired. His resolution: "It's not that the teams doing software badly are doing software badly, it's that they're doing something else very well. That something else just doesn't happen to be software development."

The metaphor came from his daughter Beth's manager. Beth proposed a project and was told: "That would be fine if we were in a forest, but we're living in a desert." For Beck, that was the moment: "It's not that we're failing to communicate, it's that we're communicating in two completely different universes. And those universes are optimizing for completely different things."

Two universes, not a spectrum. "It is not just a matter of degree. You can be in the desert and have a little foresty stuff in an area, but that's very different than being in the forest itself." Each is a self-consistent belief system with its own philosophical DNA: Rousseau and Mary Parker Follett (people are basically good; "power with") underlie the forest; Hobbes, Parkinson's Law, and Taylor (people are basically bad, must be coerced; work expands to fill the time allowed) underlie the desert. Taylor beat Follett — "It's still only a century, so we don't know how this is going to play out."

Same words, opposite meanings. Estimates, metrics, dependencies, compliance, accountability, status — every one is a "lightning rod." "No estimates" outraged both sides because each universe heard something different. "The problem's not the problem. The problem is the context."

The desert optimizes for managing the work; the forest optimizes for doing the work. Desert: "visible and quantified," predictability, "the illusion of control," blame diffusion. Forest: "messy, complicated, complex, chaotic" — because the projects are, and the social structure is built to match.

Crumbs vs. the whole cake. Software value follows a power law: most feature changes have zero value; occasionally one change makes a big difference. The forest is "optimized to find, seize on, implement and extract value from that idea." The desert is "designed to make that big spike of little changes that don't really make a difference come out on a regular basis, turn the crank" — "eating crumbs off the floor." His concession: "the crumbs are pretty tasty. You can make a lot of money in the desert."

Resources: consume less vs. create more. The desert minimizes consumption — stack ranking is survival triage ("the least productive 10% of the tribe just gets"). The forest creates resources: "if you design, you'll have time to design."

The desert has stronger gravity. "These are two attractors, and the desert has more gravitational pull than the forest does. The natural tendency is always going to be to move from the forest into the desert." Oases are satisfying but temporary; the forest needs constant care.

This is not a good/bad talk. Beck explicitly appreciates the desert: people there succeed "in spite of all the obstacles they put in their own way. It's amazing. Good on you." His goal is recognition first — you can't talk across the divide until you see there are two universes.

2. Pithy and Provocative Quotes

"The problem's not the problem. The problem is the context." — Why decades of "we know how to do this better" arguments have failed to land.

"That would be fine if we were in a forest, but we're living in a desert." — Beth's manager, the sentence that gave Beck "a decades old mystery" resolution.

"You always want that equilibrium of dissatisfaction." — Desert strategy: never report green status, or "they take some people from your project and they put them on to one of the red projects."

"If you don't squeeze every last bit out of that lemon, we're all going to die. And in the desert that's true." — Pressure-based leadership, and why it's internally coherent there.

"You want predictability and you want the illusion of control… As long as I've gotten onto the next job when that illusion shatters, I win." — The desert career strategy, described without judgment.

"You never plan for more than half of what you think you can accomplish. Never." — A forest rule the desert hears as "malfeasance" and "slackers."

"From a Shannon information theory [view], how many bits of information is here? Zero. And yet from a desert perspective, the fact of answering has value. I know when I can punish the people who do not meet this deadline." — Why his boss kept demanding a date for an 87-government-agency project that had no meaningful date.

"I'm not interested in thriving in a desert. I want to find ways of moving to the forest." — His dismissal of "Stockholm syndrome bloggers" who write about thriving under stack ranking.

"The one where you get the fruits without the roots, it just doesn't happen." — His answer to the top-voted question: management wanting forest results while acting in desert ways.

"If you could get done with 10 people what you're currently getting done with 1000, then your status at the country club goes way, way down." — Why executives will resist even a genuinely good coding genie.

"Is the forest scalable? I think it's the only thing that is scalable." — Big organizations "are more and more optimized for management, not for the job itself."

"I don't want your tired results, I want your rested, energized results." — His advice on a manager's 3 a.m.-PR engineer: "Tell him to go home."

"Small books are dangerous. That's why I write small books." — On Taylor's "chilling little" Principles of Scientific Management.

3. Tools, Practices, and Methodologies

The forest/desert diagnostic — A lens for reading organizational context before advocating anything. Use it because "without recognizing the context in which we're doing software development, we can't really understand what constitutes progress." The same proposal is heard as the opposite in each universe.

Half-full planning — Never plan more than half of what you think you can accomplish, because "the second half of the plan is not going to be achieved." Spend the freed time on community: helping teammates, learning something and bringing it back, forming structures "orthogonal to the formal org chart."

Lead with purpose, not pressure — Forest leadership means establishing a purpose worth people's time, maintaining it "when the path starts to wobbling," and seeing it through. The fuel is what Beck calls "discretionary emotional energy" — energy people choose to spend on the shared work.

Metrics for self-awareness, never as goals — While writing the TDD book, Beck timestamped every test run and discovered he tested "kind of once a minute at a time when testing once a day was considered fast." Rules: metrics are "always gamed if they've turned into goals"; a manager may discuss your metrics with you without incentives, but "you would never report individual metrics two levels up. Never, never, never" — only team-level metrics go up, to prevent distortion.

Accountability as "required to render account" — The forest definition: report honestly to your community — what worked, what didn't, "I spent too long not asking for help," and where you are today. In the forest this signals trustworthiness; in the desert accountability is pure blame ("if something good happens, I get the credit").

Resolve cross-team concerns at the boundary — Instead of declaring dependencies (which in the desert exist to diffuse responsibility and enable "scheduled chicken" — everyone knows the deadline will slip, nobody says it first), "you get together at the boundary between the teams and you work together to work it out."

Compliance as a resource — A Swiss life insurance company he works with went from product idea on Friday morning to first contract sold Monday afternoon, "including auditing, regulatory compliance, the whole thing, that whole year-long hundred million euro process compressed into a weekend and there wasn't coding on the weekend." In the desert, compliance's best use is "a good excuse not to improve."

The fruits-and-roots framing — From his and Beth's presentation "We Know How to Develop Software Well": everyone wants the fruits (no bugs, happy customers, more functionality, sooner, cheaper, more flexible, more visible), but the fruits come with roots — the practices. Use it on executives who want forest outcomes with desert behavior.

The cake strategy — How Beck now talks to executives: the forest wants the whole cake — "you have to sit up at the table, you have to use your manners, but then you get to eat the whole cake" — the rare big leap, like entering a new market trivially "because we prepared for it to be trivial."

Planting grasses (oasis-building for managers) — Concrete moves: "just let up on people a little bit"; send the overworking engineer home; respond to a broken build with "What are you going to do to avoid that in the future?" rather than punishment. Caveat: "Without constant care, your world's going to turn back into the desert because that's what's celebrated."

Resource-creation mindset — "If you design, you'll have time to design" (his Bluesky aphorism): spend time on the structure of the system so you'll have more time, which then lets you spend time on structure. Resources are "to be created and shared," not rationed.

Reading list — Mary Parker Follett on "power with" vs. "power over" ("if you haven't run into Mary Parker Follett, you're in for a treat"); Taylor's Principles of Scientific Management, with the caveat that "Taylor fudged all of his data."

Retrospectives — Because "the way that we're working could be even better"; preparing for change guarantees some work won't pay off, and that's accepted.

Augmented coding — Beck's current obsession: "I could not be more excited with programming after I figured it out 50 years. So the answers are all changing." Plus his newsletter, "which shockingly turned into a business."

4. Unanswered Questions and Omissions

What is the desert actually optimizing for? The whole talk resolves the fired-teams mystery by saying organizations are "doing something else very well" — but Beck never names it concretely. Blame diffusion? Executive status? Control? The mechanism stays metaphorical.

How do you tell which universe you're in? He polls the audience ("how many people here are working in a desert?") but offers no diagnostic — no observable signals beyond anecdotes like green-status meetings and stack ranking.

Can a desert actually become a forest? He says management must "be part of creating the environment that creates that," admits oases "are not going to last forever," and says the desert has more gravitational pull — then explicitly refuses to answer stay-vs-leave ("I'm not going to give you a blanket recommendation"). No transformation roadmap is offered.

Nothing for individual contributors. All Q&A advice is managerial. Beck even names the question he usually gets — a developer told not to write tests because "we have little people to do that for us" — and never answers it. What does an IC do inside a desert?

When is the desert the right answer? He concedes the crumbs are tasty, Taylor "won," and there are "genuine advantages" — but never says when desert assumptions are actually correct. The final question (does limited runway force you into the desert?) gets reframed as a resource-philosophy flip rather than answered.

The estimates impasse is left unresolved. Beck refused the date ("zero bits of information"), the boss kept asking every two weeks, and "I lasted about a year." What's the forest-side move that satisfies a desert boss's underlying need? The story ends with his exit, not a resolution.

AI is disconnected from the thesis. He's spending his time on augmented coding and says "the answers are all changing," but never connects it to forest/desert — even though his country-club status line implies AI-driven team shrinkage will collide with desert status economics. Does AI feed the desert (keystroke metrics, headcount cuts) or the forest?

Politics of the oasis are dropped. His forest team generated "envy, jealousy, certain amount of friction" at its company — mentioned, then abandoned. How do forest teams defend themselves inside desert organizations?

No counterarguments engaged. "Not a matter of degree" is asserted, not argued. Hybrids, teams that legitimately need both modes (hard deadlines, incident response, compliance-critical launches), and anything beyond anecdotal evidence (five teams, one insurer, one daughter's manager) go unaddressed. Community — the forest's "primary force" — gets sporting teams and knitting circles as its mechanics; Beth's theology study of thousand-year communities is invoked with no findings shared.
