# Finding a family: A categorization of enjoyable emotions

Claremont Graduate University. The Journal of Positive Psychology, 2017.

## Abstract

Responding to burgeoning scholarship examining discrete positive emotions, the overarching goals of the current review are to provide a summary of 28 enjoyable emotions and to offer an initial classification of these emotions into families. The families of discrete enjoyable emotions, many proposed for the first time, are as follows: (1) Self-praising emotions (authentic pride, fiero, naches, feeling respected), (2) other-praising emotions (admiration, elevation, gratitude, inspiration), (3) past-oriented emotions (forgiveness, nostalgia, relief), (4) future-oriented emotions (anticipatory enthusiasm, courage, determination, hope), (5) hazardous emotions (lust, schadenfreude, hubristic pride), (6) affectionate emotions (love, attachment love, tenderness, positive empathy), (7) arousal-defined emotions (euphoria, serenity), (8) violation-elicited emotions (amusement, awe, curiosity, positive surprise).

## Method for selecting enjoyable emotions

They used the term "enjoyable emotions" rather than "positive emotions" to avoid ambiguity — "enjoyable" means pleasurable to experience, a specific and consistent interpretation. As Ekman (2003) noted: "the pursuit of enjoyment is a primary motivation in our lives… People organize their lives to maximize the experience of [enjoyable emotions]."

The literature search (March 2015) used key terms: positive emotion, emotion, emotion review, positive psychology, positive affect, discrete positive emotions, enjoyable emotions, discrete emotions. Two selection criteria:

1. The term had to be designated in psychological literature as an emotion with defined triggers, appraisals, and predicted/observed psychological and behavioral patterns
2. The emotion must be enjoyable to experience or at least hypothesized to be pleasurable

The initial list of 49 terms was reduced to 28. Several emotions typically understood as positive were excluded: optimism (primarily a trait/disposition, no specific triggers), and most surprisingly, happiness and joy (no clearly defined, specific triggering events — see below).

## Method for creating families

Following Ekman and Cordaro's (2011) logic that contextualizing emotions by grouping them into families is important, emotions were visually mapped into groups based on the degree to which their definitions, eliciting events, and associated outcomes shared key features. The authors then collectively revisited and debated the classification.

The resulting eight families are:

### 1. Self-praising emotions
Positive appraisal of oneself. Contrasts with other-praising.

**Authentic pride**: Felt "when a positive outcome (for oneself) is attributed to one's own efforts" (Michie, 2009). A self-conscious emotion requiring self-evaluation. Distinct facial expression: mild Duchenne smile with compressed lips. Per Tracy & Robins (2007), authentic pride attributes success to internal, unstable, controllable causes ("I won because I practiced"), whereas hubristic pride attributes to internal, stable, uncontrollable causes ("I won because I'm always great"). Associated with desire for prestige (not dominance), accomplishment, persistence, high self-worth, altruistic actions, and achievement.

**Fiero**: "The emotion felt when meeting a difficult challenge, either with others in the context of competition or alone" (Ekman, 2003). A specific type of pride — the joy of victory. Parkinson (2005) conceptualized it as a "triumphal feeling coming as a release after a period of intense struggle." Sparse literature as a distinct emotion. Ekman postulated fiero functions to motivate ambition and achievement.

**Naches**: "Pride in the achievement of others, with whom you have a relationship" (Platt et al., 2013). Similar to vicarious pride and "basking in reflected glory." A necessary social component — experienced when witnessing achievement of offspring or protégés. Nakamura (2013) found intense pride can be evoked by other-oriented experiences. People experiencing naches often display a Duchenne smile.

**Feeling respected**: "How worthy and recognized one feels" by another or group (De Cremer & Tyler, 2005). Triggered by feeling valued and perceiving strong social standing. Differentiated from respecting another (not an emotion). Linked to group investment, reduced anti-majority attitudes, job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and rule compliance.

### 2. Other-praising emotions
Also called "self-transcendent" emotions. Elicited when responding to an "exemplary other" (Algoe & Haidt, 2009). Algoe and Haidt designated elevation, admiration, and gratitude; the authors add inspiration.

**Admiration**: Felt when witnessing "extraordinary displays of skill, talent, or achievement" (Algoe & Haidt, 2009). Must be non-moral triggers (otherwise it's elevation). Function: promote individual learning, change, and emulation of role models. Linked to voting behaviors, mentoring relationships, adolescent role model identification, and intergroup respect.

**Elevation**: Experienced when witnessing an instance of moral beauty (Haidt, 2003). Includes feeling warm, uplifted, inspired, and optimistic about humanity. Distinct from amusement, joy, enthusiasm, pleasure, and gratitude. Empirically linked to prosocial behaviors: charitable donation, organizational citizenship behavior, reduced racial bias. Trait elevation correlates with agreeableness, openness to experience, benevolence, and spirituality.

**Gratitude**: Elicited when a benefactor performs a generous act for the self. The most widely researched positive emotion. Algoe & Stanton's (2012) find-remind-and-bind framework: gratitude signals communal relationship norms and motivates "an upward spiral of mutually responsive behaviors." Linked to life satisfaction, improved relationships, prosocial actions, social support, trust, reduced aggression, and reduced depression/anxiety.

**Inspiration**: A eudaimonic feeling experienced when "facing challenges in the process of goal attainment" (Straume & Vittersø, 2012). Thrash & Elliot (2004) distinguish "inspired by" (transcendence, imaginative recreation) from "inspired to" (approach motivation, urge to move toward a stimulus). Correlated with but factorially distinct from positive affect. Linked to easier life transitions, increased efficiency and goal progress, creativity, productivity, and enhanced well-being.

### 3. Past-oriented emotions
Triggered by a past event. All three are preceded by a negative event.

**Forgiveness**: Two definitions: (1) reducing unforgiveness, or (2) decreasing and eventually eliminating unforgiveness by replacing negative with positive (Worthington et al., 2007). Includes self-forgiveness and forgiveness toward others. Imagining forgiveness dampens physiological stress indicators (heart rate reactivity, skin conductance, blood pressure). State forgiveness associated with lower stress and resolving Eriksonian psychosocial crises.

**Nostalgia**: A longing for a lived past (personal) or evocation of feelings for a time before one's birth (vicarious). Previously seen as pathology; now conceptualized as enjoyable. Most commonly triggered by negative stimuli (loneliness, social exclusion, boredom) but counteracted through increased perceived social support. Prototypic experience characterized more by positive than negative affect. Linked to social coping, goal-directed strategies, and enhanced psychological health.

**Relief**: Marked by contrast between current positive state and previous negative state. Two distinct triggers: near-miss relief (narrow avoidance of aversive outcome) and task-completion relief (completion of an onerous event). Activates reward and valuation circuitry. One of few emotions differentiable by vocal/facial expression and brain activity. Potential harmful outcomes: relief-seeking may motivate substance use. Beneficial: near-miss relief motivates counterfactual thinking; task-completion relief reinforces endurance.

### 4. Future-oriented emotions
Focus on a desirable future outcome. Each motivates behavior toward a favorable outcome.

**Anticipatory enthusiasm**: Feeling of "wanting" elicited by cue of imminent reward. Serves resource acquisition needs. Promotes narrowed attentional focus and heuristic processing. Sympathetic nervous system activation (cardiovascular arousal). Neurobiological basis in dopamine neural network (nucleus accumbens shell). Linked to acceptance of weak persuasive messages, financial risk-taking, and extraversion.

**Courage**: "The ability to act for a meaningful cause, despite experiencing the fear associated with perceived threat exceeding the available resources" (Woodard, 2004). Rarely described as a discrete emotion but included in Arnold's (1960) list. Hannah et al.'s (2007) model includes positive emotional components: self-efficacy, hope, resilience. Primarily studied as disposition; sparse research as discrete emotion.

**Determination**: The urge to persevere toward an unrealized goal. Facial expression: furrowed brows, frowning or closed lips. Triggered by goal inconsistency with appraisal of high coping potential. Asymmetrically activates left frontal cortex. Linked to greater well-being, better physical health, task engagement and perseverance. Debate exists whether it's an emotion or cognitive state, though most people characterize their feelings of determination as emotional.

**Hope**: Believing in a desirable outcome (Lazarus, 1991). Most research uses Snyder's cognitive model (agency + pathway cognitions), but a parallel line designates hope as a discrete emotion. Averill et al. (1990) found four prerequisites: appraisal of attainment as realistic, acceptable, important, and willingness to take action. Often preceded by negative situational context with little perceived control. Functions as a coping resource in despair.

### 5. Hazardous emotions
Unified by their darker natures: selfish motivations operating at a cost to others. Pleasant valence but negative connotation. Direct contrast to the affectionate family.

**Lust**: Amplified sex drive characterized by craving for sexual gratification. Evolutionary purpose: motivating individuals to seek union with any appropriate member of the species. Activates the hypothalamus (hormonal center, one of three pleasure centers). Triggered by viewing beloved partner, thinking about one-night stands, or pornography. Can be associated with harmful outcomes (sex offending) when triggered by inappropriate cues — whether beneficial or harmful depends heavily on triggers and context.

**Schadenfreude**: Taking pleasure in the misfortunes of others when the self is not the cause. Triggered by learning of loss/suffering of an enemy or "when an envied person experiences a deserved misfortune." Self-evaluation threats intensify the pleasure. Associated with spiteful behavior, increased envy, sexism, vengeance; negatively related to prosociality. Neurologically distinct from envy (diminished vs heightened reward-processing).

**Hubristic pride**: Attributing positive outcome to internal, stable, uncontrollable causes ("I won because I'm always great"). Contrasts with authentic pride's attribution to effort. The two-factor model (Tracy & Robins, 2004) has faced criticism (Holbrook et al., 2014). Linked to maladaptive outcomes: shame, low self-esteem, aggression, hostility, impulsivity, interpersonal problems. More strongly related to antisocial behaviors than authentic pride.

### 6. Affectionate emotions
Other-focused with feeling favorably toward another, but unlike other-praising emotions, don't require perceiving the other as praiseworthy — just worthy of affection.

**Love**: "Love is comprised of any other positive emotion that is felt in the context of people's connections with others" (Fredrickson, 2013). Evoked when perceiving another as especially desirable or open to special closeness. Urge to play, explore, savor with loved ones. Associated with nurturing, caregiving, investing in another's well-being, sharing activities, long-term planning.

**Attachment love**: A "surge of care toward a trusted caregiver" (Griskevicius et al., 2010). Typically the feeling a child has toward a parent, but also experienced in romantic relationships. Elicits nurturance from others. Associated with trust, safety, acceptance, lack of responsibility. Conceptually different from tenderness: more like feeling loved rather than loving someone perceived as vulnerable.

**Tenderness**: "The impulse toward caregiving behavior" (Kalawski, 2010). Overlapping construct with nurturant love. Evoked by perceiving target as vulnerable but not necessarily in need. Physiological response: slow breathing, relaxed muscle tone, tilted head, slight smile. "Tenderness should produce altruistic motivation directed toward the ultimate goal of addressing the target's vulnerability" (Lishner, Batson, & Huss, 2011). Distinct from sympathy: evoked by vulnerability, not current need.

**Positive empathy**: "Understanding and vicariously sharing others' positive emotions" (Morelli et al., 2015). Independent from empathy for others' distress. Those with high state empathy experience stronger crossover of positive emotions than negative emotions. Related to increased prosocial behaviors, social connectedness, greater well-being. A subtype exists: empathic cheerfulness — taking on positive mood in response to another's negative mood.

### 7. Arousal-defined emotions
Organized primarily by activation level, not trigger or appraisal. Serenity (low arousal) and euphoria (high arousal).

**Euphoria** (also ecstasy, elation): Triggered by any event perceived as strongly positive — near-death experiences, sports victories, early-stage romantic love. Left-hemisphere frontal lobe process. Inconsistent terminology across the literature. Elation associated with decreased recall of negative memories, increased recall of positive events. Elated moods produce heuristic rather than elaborated processing when judging others.

**Serenity**: Feelings of inner calm, relaxation, sense of being in the moment. Items: calm, relaxed, at ease. Demonstrated discriminant validity from happiness, pride, surprise. Elicited by calming stimuli, yoga, relaxing music/imagery. Associated with self-esteem (in drug-dependent populations), increased helping behaviors (via relaxing video games). Terms "tranquility" and "contentment" may tap the same construct.

### 8. Violation-elicited emotions
Triggered by novel and unexpected external stimuli that violate expectations and shift cognitive processes. "Violate" means a change, disruption, or challenge newly present in the environment. Each involves a shift of attention toward the stimuli in an effort to understand.

**Amusement**: Triggered by humorous or unexpected stimuli that violate expectations. Ratings increase the more startling/unexpected the trigger. Cognitively: shift between knowledge structures, potentially facilitating creativity. Physiological markers: increased smiling, somatic activity, skin conductance, respiratory/cardiovascular activation. Also called mirth. Humor induces amusement.

**Awe** (sometimes wonder): Response to novel stimuli that challenges one's current understanding. Requires perception of vastness (something greater than the self) and need to accommodate understanding into existing mental structures. Keltner & Haidt (2003) propose five types: threat, beauty, exceptional ability, virtue, supernatural (all but threat lead to enjoyable states). Facial expression: widened eyes, raised head, slightly raised eyebrows. Goosebumps associated. Linked to self-transcendence, decreased impatience, increased helping behavior, lowered perceived time pressure, greater life satisfaction. Piff et al. (2015) provide evidence that self-diminishing effects mediate the link between awe and helping behavior.

**Curiosity**: Affective state related to desire to investigate, learn, and incorporate novel experiences. Aroused by novel questions, complex concepts, ambiguous statements. Epistemic curiosity specifically refers to desire for new information motivating exploratory behavior. Linked to longer life, reduced degenerative CNS disease, positive interpersonal outcomes, greater attraction and closeness.

**Positive surprise**: Elicited by interruption of ongoing activities, heightening consciousness of the surprising stimulus. Debate whether surprise is pleasurable — some hold it's neutral (any pleasantness comes from subsequent emotions), others contend it can be pleasant (e.g., unexpected gift). Appraised as "high" on pleasantness. Mediates between positive affect and creativity. Higher consumer satisfaction regardless of initial mood. Specific facial reaction: raised eyebrows, open eyes and mouth.

## Why joy and happiness were excluded

The exclusion is perhaps the paper's most surprising decision. Joy is commonly considered a basic positive emotion, but is often broadly equated with general positive affect. Happiness has been defined as both an emotional state and a broad perception of life satisfaction. The terms are used interchangeably, and it's unclear whether scholars mean general positive affect or distinct emotions.

Even among articles defining them as discrete emotions, there's little consensus on triggers: some say joy occurs in safe/familiar contexts requiring little effort; others say joy follows novel events; Fredrickson links joy to play motivation.

The authors concluded it might be premature to conceptualize joy and happiness as discrete enjoyable emotions. They suggest joy might be a midpoint of enjoyable arousal between serenity and euphoria — a hypothesis generated by the classification system itself.

## Key structural findings

**Contrast pairs**: The families form meaningful contrasts: self-praising vs other-praising, past-oriented vs future-oriented, affectionate vs hazardous. The arousal-defined and violation-elicited families lack contrasting pairs.

**The classification is explicitly provisional**: "The current manuscript is the first attempt to put forth a comprehensive list and categorization of enjoyable emotions, and as such it may not be as ordered as it is poised to be in the future."

## Limitations acknowledged

- First attempt; future scholarship expected to refine
- Didn't consider unenjoyable emotions that might share key characteristics
- Other bases of classification (facial expressions, evolutionary perspective) could have been equally beneficial
- Other definitions of "positive emotion" (optimal functioning, positive outcomes) would have yielded different lists
- Some emotions have extensive research; the paper provides brief synopses, not exhaustive reviews
