Reducing Risk in Projects, Increasing Resilience — Dave Snowden, Craft 2025

1. Key Points — You Can't Predict, So Manage the Preconditions

Complexity science is not systems thinking. Systems thinking says "know where you've got to end up and we'll close the gap." Complexity says "understand where you are and start journeys with a sense of direction." Gap-closing under high uncertainty pattern-entrains you. "Far too many people talk about north stars as if they were destinations rather than navigation aids."

In a complex system, the only certainty is unintended consequences. So the job is not prediction but triggering emergence and monitoring patterns: "Good patterns you give more energy to, bad patterns you give less." The more you commit to an outcome, the higher your risk — hold options open.

Hindsight does not lead to foresight. The combinatorics make "joining up the dots" a retrospective fiction: 10 dots yield over 3.4 trillion possible combinations, 12 dots over 4.8 quadrillion. This is why root cause analysis and lessons-learned are broken — they only work after the fact.

You do not see what you do not expect to see. 83% of radiologists miss a gorilla image 48 times the size of a cancer nodule — and the 17% who see it come to believe they were wrong after talking to the majority. Weak signal detection therefore cannot rely on experts or periodic reports; it needs distributed, real-time human sensing.

Humans are terrible individual decision-makers but good small-group ones. We evolved to compromise in groups of seven or fewer; beyond that we retreat into professional silos. Design decision-making around natural group structures, not around org charts.

Manage the ecology, not the machine. The historical shift: military-derived scientific management (which he defends as more adaptive than its critics admit), then the 80s engineering metaphor — BPR, Six Sigma ("six stigma"), the learning organization — where "engineers don't like uncertainty, so they tried to eliminate it." Now: an ecological metaphor — manage the substrate and see what works rather than deciding in advance what's right.

Fund what works, not what people say will work. Small, auditable, distributed spending decisions reveal sustainability; advocacy reveals nothing.

Culture changes through micro-narratives, not abstractions. Water-cooler stories determine culture more than questionnaire answers. "I want more stories like this, fewer stories like those" beats "we need an agile mindset."

3. Tools, Practices, and Methodologies

Estuarine mapping (affordance mapping) — Map everything operating in your system onto a grid of energy cost of change vs time to change. Two half-days: afternoon to map, next morning for actions. Iron rule: if the group can't agree what something is or where it sits, "break it down until you agree" — no arguments, no authority intruding. Read three zones: the counterfactual (won't realistically change — where systems-thinking vision statements nearly always land), the liminal (you can't change it but a higher authority could — added during Novartis clinical trials work; a CEO looked at it and said "nobody's asked me"), and the volatile (can turn on a dime, low energy cost, high impact). Then nudge: "I want this to never change," "make the cost of that less." It's a pre-process that changes the dispositional state of the environment so the project is more likely to succeed. Watch for dark actants — visible effects with no attributable cause, like dark matter.

Actants: actors, constraints, constructors — Vocabulary from actor-network theory plus constructor theory ("the science of can and can't"). Constraints that contain give predictability; constraints that connect reduce it. Constructors transform without themselves changing — processes, rituals, object orientation. Practical example: coders who changed into formal clothes before testing software found more errors.

Scaffolding — Make a constraint so rigid it can't change, for baseline predictability (driving on the left). Also heuristic-based control: Naples drivers "follow the next car, match speed, avoid collision and ignore the rules."

Distributed decision making — Identify four decision-making roles via a half- or full-day simulation (currently being used on end-of-life decisions in a British hospital). Any small group (≤7, one person per role group) that assembles those roles, documents context/decision/consequences at micro level, and accepts one anonymous member — the Panopticon effect: observed by an unknown observer, you'll be honest — can spend $500–$10,000 without permission. The executive runs 100 such experiments and funds what worked. Development-bank version: village head, priest, oldest girl at school, youngest boy considered a man, plus an anonymous bank agent can spend $500 on the village — fully auditable.

The new risk matrix (unnamed; "you are the first group ever to see this") — Two axes: natural vs forced emergence; fine vs coarse granularity. Four strategies: traditional strategy (research, experts, commit), parallel safe-to-fail probes (Cynefin), opportunistic commitment (monitor, then pile resources in), and the new quadrant — many small groups making decisions and running micro-experiments, i.e., monitoring emergence while applying microstimulation. Map each aspect of a project into the quadrants and match mitigation accordingly; start bottom-left before committing top-right.

Walking the Gemba / microsensing (human sensor network) — Kill project reports; everyone captures micro-narratives continuously as they work. Origin: US Army in Afghanistan — soldiers kept field records as micro-narratives instead of patrol reports, got 100% compliance and real-time human metadata. Enables real-time exception reporting and instant workforce polling: push a question to 2,000 employees, get patterned results in minutes, iterate three times. Non-experts see gorillas that experts miss. Open API; he's looking for partners.

Micro-reporting for taboo signals — Continuous capture lets people flag "something doesn't feel right, not serious enough to report yet." Identity stripped, keywords and metadata kept, aggregated into a board-level alert: "you've got an emerging problem in this division." Solves whistleblowing's too-late problem without exposing individuals.

Multimedia capture — "Text is less than 10% of what you know." Capture pictures, voice, voice tone. Dutch care homes: nurse photographs the patient, writes the story, records the patient's story — all becomes a micro-narrative.

Naive-agent training data sets — Take past failed and succeeded projects, gather everything reported at the time, have naive agents (who don't know what the project was) interpret it, extract common story patterns, and use that as AI training data for anticipatory alerts on live projects. Same method produced an Al Qaeda training data set for trawling the web. Key principle: don't assume general intelligence by trawling everything.

Assemblage / cartoon-based cultural mapping — With Comic Agilé and Gaping Void: present six cartoons to the whole workforce; each person picks the one representing the culture they live in, tells the story of why, and self-indexes it. Output is a pattern map (not a spider diagram of abstract terms) where clusters are attitudes. Change management becomes "more stories like this, fewer stories like those."

Hexi — Decompose every agile method, tool, and framework into its "lowest coherent unit" and recombine freely; software-based, with assemblies stored and modifiable downstream. Example: peel a sprint out of Scrum, replace it with a DSDM three-month timebox. The alternative to single-vendor, single-framework adoption. Kit available; scrum.org has produced theirs.

Back of a table napkin test — If you can't redraw a framework from memory, you're dependent on a picture or an expert, and it's useless for sense-making.

EU Field Guide on managing complexity in a crisis — Co-authored with the EU; free (Brits pay postage).

4. Unanswered Questions and Omissions

The new risk matrix has zero evidence behind it. "Early days," unpublished, no case study, no worked example of a real project mapped onto it — yet presented as "absolutely critical in any project environment."

The four roles are never named. Distributed decision-making hinges on roles discovered through a simulation he runs; there's no method given for anyone else to run one. Same for the "epistemically balanced training data sets" — 25 years of DARPA work compressed into one sentence, no how-to.

The surveillance problem is never addressed. Continuous micro-capture of engineers' work, real-time workforce polling, anonymous observers — framed purely as removing reporting burden. Consent, privacy, GDPR, and what happens when workers know their micro-narratives are being pattern-analyzed: all absent. And if formal reports get edited with hindsight, why won't micro-narratives get gamed once people know the patterns being watched?

A tension he doesn't resolve: he demolishes lessons learned for relying on hindsight, then builds anticipatory alerts by training AI on patterns from past project failures. Novel failure modes — the ones that actually kill projects — aren't in the training data.

"People are objects too" gets one sentence. A people-object with standard I/O that handles ambiguity and generates training data is sketched, never specified — including the ethics of literally objectifying people in an architecture.

No failure modes for his own methods. What happens to a $500 distributed decision that goes badly wrong? How do you manage interference between hundreds of parallel safe-to-fail experiments running simultaneously? Portfolio-level interaction effects are never mentioned.

No outcome data. Proof points are an AstraZeneca award and two unnamed oil companies ("a bit sensitive"). Nothing on how to measure whether estuarine mapping or microsensing actually reduced risk.

Resilience — half the talk's title — is never defined or directly tackled. It's implied through distributed decision-making and sensing, never addressed as its own subject.

The wholesale dismissal of systems thinking and destinations leaves no room for non-negotiable outcomes — regulatory deadlines, safety-critical systems, contractual commitments. When is gap-closing the right tool? Never asked.

Who judges "good patterns"? He forbids holistic thinking ("you pervert your perspective of the system to whatever has the most power") yet his methods require someone with power to decide which patterns get more energy and which stories to amplify. The power question re-enters through the back door, unexamined.

The natural-groups science is asserted, not cited. "Compromise in groups of seven or less," macrodemes and monument-building — presented as settled theoretical biology, with only "loosely maps with Dunbar's number" as a reference point. And the grid's time axis is admitted to be incomplete — the "two other types of time" are R&D for next year.
