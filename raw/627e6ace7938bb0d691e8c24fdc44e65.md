1. Key Points: You're the DRI, and You Live in Toronto Now

You cannot rely on having a good manager. Good managers give feedback, support, and opportunities — but they're often busy, distracted, or don't know how. Even the best one is temporary: "your career transcends the period of time that you'll spend with this person." No one will ever care more about your career than you.

The market changed, and the metaphor is raccoons. Tech workers were the pampered Instagram raccoons (Melanie on a trike, Piper the painter, Tamo with his own crypto); now "we live in Toronto now" — the harsh environment where raccoons survive by being "more strategic, more determined and more resilient." The response to a bad market isn't powerlessness; it's using the power you do have deliberately.

Optimize the arc, not the job. "Expect more from your career and less from your current job." Jobs you don't enjoy can meaningfully develop your skills; jobs you love may not develop you at all. Making that trade-off explicit is how you decide well.

Good goals pass a control test. A proximate objective should challenge you but feel possible, teach you something, be something you want, and be within your control. "Get promoted" fails: if the goal is something somebody else has to give you, what is your work in it?

Opportunities are planted, not just given or picked. Her 2023 book deal was the harvest of years of side writing, thinking, and talks — "being a person who could write a book was also really hard. It was just much less apparent what I was working towards." Creating your own opportunities is also what lets you decline the dandelions instead of accepting everything because scarcity feels real.

Titles are a couple of words; the work is 40+ hours a week. Her saddest hiring-manager cases are people hung up on a title (staff engineer) whose actual preferences don't match the work. People who fought harder for titles cling harder — understandable, but don't hold a title "at the expense of the work you actually do and the growth you get."

Coachability = receptiveness + actionability. Feedback is just information: for improvement, about the giver, or for the bin. Bad feedback ignores context, negates your input, or undermines you. The Netflix standard is the fix: direct feedback only counts if it helps the person succeed.

The sweet spot of growth is out of your comfort zone, with help. The "drowning school" (deep end, fingers crossed) and the frog-boiling school capture how violent hard growth feels. Develop strengths; only fix weaknesses that are actually holding you back.

Quit from power, not from confusion. There's "a huge difference between quitting your job because you don't know how to change it and get what you want from it, than quitting from a place of power when you know what you want and you're going towards it." She reviews quitting annually — and credits that practice, plus organizational change, with her five-year tenure.

Five signals to pay attention to: you've stopped learning; you're building elaborate coping mechanisms; you're morally conflicted about hiring; your confidence is shrinking; your health is affected.

Interview regularly. In this market you must know how hard another job would be to find and whether it would be better. Market data either fuels negotiation or reassures you.

Build your raccoon squad. One person can't meet all your needs (true in romantic relationships too). Manager, skip-level, peers, mentors, sponsors, coaches, work BFF — "Don't go alone."

3. The Toolkit

DRI your career — Treat yourself as the Directly Responsible Individual for your career, like a project owner. Use it to stop outsourcing career decisions to your manager.

Proximate objectives — Your next achievable goals, replacing review-cycle development plans that get made and forgotten. Test each against four criteria: challenges you but feels possible; you learn something; you want it; it's within your control.

Career dreams + connecting strategy — "Someday, maybe" ideas, concrete or directional. Strategy = identify gaps between now and the dream, generate ideas to close them. "You do not need a five year plan. Some sense of direction that will help you make the next decision is enough."

The written plan — Write down: the what (specific enough to know when you've achieved it, broad enough to demonstrate genuine capability expansion), the why (the motivation you look at when discouraged), your resources, who will support you, and the opportunities you have or can create.

Planting flowers (opportunity creation) — Do the generative side-work now for the opportunities you want later: submit to CFPs to speak; take on onboarding, documentation, and team stuff to become a manager; go toward hard problems and keep leveling technical skills for staff. Follow what you actually enjoy — writing, the stage, organizing, "debugging terrible problems that nobody else wants to look at."

Flower triage for offered opportunities — Roses: take them. Tulips: you may already have enough (her Dutch partner means a house full of tulips); decide consciously. Dandelions: "that's not for me. That's for somebody else."

Negotiation practice — Reframe low-value or token invitations as negotiation practice; when somebody asks you for something, "it is a great time to negotiate."

The constraints conversation — Write down your current constraints (rest, stability, workload), discuss with someone you trust, then bring them to your manager — e.g., more responsibility with a manageable workload. "Often much more is possible than you think."

The raccoon squad (support system map) — Manager; skip-level (broader reach, more information, underestimated opportunity-finding power); peers (context, unblocking, feedback); mentors and sponsors (advice, plus advocacy "when you're not in the room"); coaches (someone invested in you without org or social-circle baggage); the work BFF ("data shows that our life is better when we have one").

The coachability model — Two axes: receptiveness (listen and work through feedback, self-aware and prepared, fit it into your mental model, believe you can learn from anyone) and actionability (experiment, iterate, respond to explicit and implicit feedback, return to helpers with what you tried, seek more perspectives). Expect to sit in different quadrants with different people — high-trust relationships belong up and to the right.

Five steps to being more coachable — (1) Build self-awareness: when someone annoys you, ask what they triggered; watch what people around you do better. (2) Get a broader perspective: outside your team, org, industry — "tech is not that special." (3) Shed defensiveness: get curious instead. (4) If you screwed up, admit it — starting with what could go better gives people a starting point for constructive feedback. (5) Ask for advice, not feedback.

Feedback triage — Sort feedback into: information to improve with, information about the giver, and trash. Bad feedback has three markers: ignores broader context (voice criticism with no thought of misogyny), negates your input (boss doesn't tell you what he wants, then complains you didn't guess), undermines rather than helps you succeed.

First, Break All the Rules (Marcus Buckingham) — Great managers develop strengths and only address "controlling weaknesses" — ones actually holding the person back. Apply it to yourself; your manager isn't the only source of help.

Mentoring vs. managing vs. coaching — Not interchangeable; good managers pick the right tool at the right time, and match mentoring to the person's actual level.

The annual quitting review — Regularly ask: where am I, what am I doing, what am I getting, where do I want to go, what might I do instead? Distinguish quitting from power vs. quitting from confusion.

The five quit signals checklist — Not learning; elaborate coping mechanisms (political dances, blame-shielding documentation); moral conflict about hiring; shrinking confidence; physical effects. Any of these means "you need to pay attention" — change it, possibly by changing jobs.

Regular interviewing — Go to market periodically to learn how hard another job would be to find and whether it would be better; use the data to negotiate or to confirm "a good deal under capitalism."

Collecting wins (confidence repair) — When confidence is low, that's job one. Her model: blogging weekly in grad school to be "in the world" and rebuild a self-esteem corroded by an absent supervisor.

Mining implicit feedback — The higher you go, the rarer explicit feedback gets (nobody has full context on your job). Mine the partial-context information from people around you to figure out "what can I do better within my human constraints."

4. What the Talk Leaves Open

How to actually negotiate. "It is a great time to negotiate" and the "negotiation practice" reframe are offered, but she never says what she asked for, whether she accepted the token invitations, or what happened. The emotionally satisfying reframe skips the mechanics.

What to do when you don't know what you want. The framework (dreams → strategy → proximate objectives) assumes you can name a goal. She grants herself an "exploratory phase" post-book but offers no method for people stuck there indefinitely — the audience members who couldn't produce a goal on request get no next step.

How to build a raccoon squad from zero. Mentors, sponsors, coaches, and work BFFs are prescribed but never acquired. Sponsors advocate for you when you're not in the room — how you get one is unexplained. Coaching costs money; that constraint goes unmentioned.

The privilege baked into "planting flowers." The book took "a year of my life where I didn't really have Saturdays." Side-project career-building assumes spare time, energy, and financial slack. Caregivers, visa holders, and people living paycheck to paycheck get no version of this advice.

Individual agency vs. systemic forces. "We live in Toronto now" names a hard market, but every remedy is personal strategy. Layoffs, ageism, visa dependency — and above all AI's reshaping of software work — are never named. A 2025 careers talk for developers that never mentions AI's effect on the job market avoids the biggest force making Toronto hard.

The promotion/pay contradiction. Promotions are "not within your control" and "a level of capitalism" — but the Q&A exposes the tension: titles bring money. Her answer ("it depends how much you care about money... go and get the max that you can") deflates the question rather than resolving it, and the moderator's point about orgs lacking promotion headcount gets "there's a lot of constraints right now."

How to separate biased feedback from true feedback. She binned years of voice-and-accent feedback as sexist, then "eventually I learned what I actually needed to take from it" — the pacing was real. How she made that determination is exactly the hard part, and it's asserted, not taught.

What to do about a genuinely bad manager. The talk covers busy, distracted, unskilled managers. Abusive environments get one sentence and no exit strategy, no managing-up script, no escalation path.

What if the market says no. "Interview regularly" assumes interviews are available. If the answer to "how hard will it be to find another job" is "impossible right now," the talk has no plan B beyond staying and reframing.

Feedback trauma. "The work that we do on ourselves to untangle previous feedback trauma" is called "a real gift for our resilience and growth" — then abandoned. "Some of us need therapy for this" is a throwaway line for what is, for many, the prerequisite for everything else in the talk.

Ambiguous quit signals. Five signals, but no weighting: what if you've stopped learning but genuinely need stability this year? She validates not being "up for a challenge 100% of the time" but never shows how to hold that against the "you need to change it" alarm.

The titular framework is thin. "DRI" gets one sentence of definition; the talk is really a collection of career-advice modules under a catchy brand — and "this is not advice, this is things to think about" is a hedge that sidesteps accountability when the advice doesn't fit someone's situation.
