The “funnel of doom” is the default, broken hiring model. Most companies treat recruiting like a sales funnel: reach out to 100 people, 20 reply (not the top 20%, just the 20 who had a bad day), weed them out through stages, and hire the remainder. This “remainder hiring” regresses to the mean and never reliably produces talent-dense teams.

Treat every hire like an executive search. Instead of a wide funnel, start with a “pillar of excellence.” Define what the top 1% looks like for that specific role (scoping), identify the ~50 best people in the world for it (market mapping), and then relentlessly pursue and activate those individuals. The atomic unit of hiring is the person, not the job spec.

Scoping is the most underinvested cornerstone. Before talking to any candidate, objectively stack-rank the skills and experiences you need, articulate why the role matters, and define what success looks like. This shapes sourcing, pitch, assessment, and close. Avoid lazy shortcuts like “which logos on the resume”; instead, ask targeted questions tied to those specific attributes.

Recruiting is a confidence engine, not a decision-making engine. Hiring managers (not recruiters) own the hiring decision and must feel the joy and pain of good and bad hires. Recruiters provide the confidence to make the right call. If talent is truly the #1 priority, founders and leaders must be deeply involved—no outsourcing.

Caring is free and the top driver of candidate satisfaction. The number one thing candidates report is “the company generally wanted me; they made me feel like I belong.” Out-caring competitors is an unfair advantage that costs nothing but intentionality. Every touchpoint—from the first message to the personalized closing gift—should demonstrate that care.

Closing happens throughout the process, not at the end. Uncover motivations early, tailor the entire experience to those motivations, and remove objections continuously. At the offer stage, it’s a culmination, not a sudden sales pitch. Closing is a team sport with daily standups, candidate-specific Slack channels, and curated interactions.

The current market is a “tale of two cities.” Demand for AI researchers, forward-deployed engineers, and roles blending engineering/product/design is at an 11/10, while overly specialized ICs and new grads without enough experience are waning. Time is the most fixed, precious asset—comp is high, but the race is against the clock.

Build a recruiting team of excellent humans with a chip on their shoulder who put team over self. Great recruiters thrive at 90–110% capacity—busy but not overwhelmed. The first recruiting hire should decouple immediate execution (filling roles) from building the system; don’t collapse both into one person.

Tools, practices, and methodologies

Scoping (the cornerstone): Before any outreach, objectively define and stack-rank the skills, experiences, and attributes that define the top 1% for this specific role. Articulate why the role matters and what success looks like. This becomes the blueprint for sourcing questions, pitch, assessment, and close. Avoid defaulting to company logos; focus on transferable, objective criteria.

Targeted network mining: Instead of asking “Who’s the best X?”, ask highly specific questions tied to the scoping attributes (e.g., “Who’s the most collaborative with designers?”). This triangulates names and builds a target list of ~50 people. Do this in repeated one-on-one sessions with trusted, high-taste individuals, holding them accountable for follow-ups.

Hiring Ideas Slack channel: A company-wide channel where anyone drops a profile, post, or product they found impressive. The team rallies around it, quickly strategizing how to engage that person. It creates a culture where everyone is a talent scout and builds social credibility for surfacing great people.

Work trials / side-by-side projects: For technical and other roles, candidates do a real (but scoped) project alongside the team, often on-site over a day or two. They choose from a set of problems, and the company accommodates their schedule (including Saturdays). This provides the highest signal on both technical skill and collaboration, and gives the candidate strong self-selection data.

Candidate-specific Slack channels: Every candidate gets their own channel for the team to coordinate strategy, share updates, and rally around closing. It turns closing into a team sport with daily standups for high-profile candidates.

Personalized, thoughtful closing gifts: Learn a candidate’s personal interests (e.g., a classically trained violinist) and give a small, unique, and highly personal gift at the offer stage. It’s not about the money; it’s about demonstrating that you truly see them.

Post-offer pre-boarding community: Between offer accept and start date, actively prevent renegs by sending a company laptop, hosting dinners with other accepted candidates, and maintaining regular touchpoints. This builds belonging and excitement before day one.

Home games and breaking bread: Whenever possible, bring candidates into the office for the vibe (“Hogwarts library” feel) and center interactions around meals. Sharing food is a disarming, humanizing act that accelerates connection.

Bespoke, non-robotic interview processes: Don’t let availability dictate who interviews a candidate. Hand-pick interviewers based on shared interests or background. Tailor the process to the individual, not a rigid stage-gate funnel.

Hiring manager-led debriefs (no committees): The hiring manager gathers input from the team but makes the final hire/no-hire decision. The team’s job is to provide the best signal to enable that decision. Debrief immediately (same day) and move fast once a candidate is ready.

Decouple the first recruiting hire: Don’t hire one person to both fill immediate roles and build the long-term system. Either hire a system-builder and supplement with a contingent recruiter for short-term execution, or hire a high-powered IC recruiter and bring in external help for the foundation.

Recruiter as talent engineer: Encourage every recruiter to use tools like Cursor to build their own automations and workflows, reducing dependency on engineering time and elevating the craft.

Unanswered questions and omissions

Diversity, equity, and inclusion: The entire conversation focuses on “top 1%” and “trusted networks,” which can easily replicate existing biases and homogeneous networks. How does this approach avoid groupthink and ensure diverse slates? No mention of structured interviewing, bias mitigation, or inclusive practices.

Scalability beyond elite, early-stage teams: The model is intensely high-touch and founder-dependent. How does it work when hiring for roles that aren’t “top 50 in the world” (e.g., customer support, mid-level generalists) or when the company grows beyond a few hundred people? The “funnel of doom” exists partly because it scales; the alternative’s limits aren’t addressed.

Retention after hiring: The talk is all about attracting and closing. What happens after the honeymoon? How do you keep these high-talent-density individuals engaged, growing, and not poached? No discussion of onboarding, development, or internal mobility.

Compensation philosophy and internal equity: Adam says they try to “mitigate comp as a reason” and focus on intrinsic motivators, but acknowledges NBA-sized offers. How do they handle internal equity when new hires command massive packages that outstrip tenured employees? The tension is acknowledged but not resolved.

The “renegs” trend is mentioned but not explored: Why is it happening more? What specific countermeasures work beyond pre-boarding? How do you rebuild a pipeline if a critical hire reneges last minute?

Employer brand dependency: Cursor (and SpaceX) have strong pull. How does a no-name startup execute this playbook when candidates aren’t already intrigued? The advice assumes a baseline of inbound interest or at least recognition.

Burnout risk for recruiters and teams: The “90–110% capacity” sweet spot, daily standups, bespoke processes, and relentless pursuit sound exhausting. How do you sustain this intensity without burning out the recruiting team or the hiring managers? No mention of workload management or boundaries.

Measuring success beyond anecdotes: Adam says “what gets measured gets done,” but the only metric mentioned is candidate satisfaction. What are the specific KPIs for this approach (e.g., offer acceptance rate, time-to-fill for these bespoke searches, quality of hire metrics)? How do you know it’s working quantitatively?

The role of AI in recruiting itself: While he mentions “talent engineer,” there’s no deep dive on how AI could change sourcing, outreach, or assessment. Given Cursor’s product, this feels like a missed opportunity to explore how AI might disrupt the very craft he’s describing.

Counterarguments to the “funnel of doom” critique: Many companies successfully use funnel-based hiring with rigorous top-of-funnel sourcing and high standards. The talk sets up a stark binary, but doesn’t engage with hybrid models or situations where volume hiring is unavoidable. Is there a middle ground?
