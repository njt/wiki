Shaped by Demand: The Power of Fluid Teams — Dan Terhorst-North, Craft 2025

1. Key points: The demand is fixed; the teams are the variable

The real org-design problem is alignment at scale. Cross-functional teams solved the 8-person problem in the early 2000s — before that, "the eight people would be in eight different teams," often in different companies. The interesting question of "the last quarter century" is how you get 80, then 800 people pointing in the same direction — and "you can only start to address that once you solve the eight people thing."

Org design needs a stated goal, and consultancies don't have one. His definition: "build the best structure we can to do the best work we can for now, knowing that it's going to change." Big consultancies deploy cookie-cutter approaches with no outcome in mind — ask them the point and "they go billable hours."

"The work" is five things, not one. Delivery (ship features), discovery (problem-space and solution-space), kaizen, BAU (reports, controls, compliance), and failure demand. Only the first three are discretionary spend; BAU and failure demand are mandatory. Most backlogs show only delivery.

Failure demand is the silent capacity killer. HP LaserJet's firmware team (Gene Kim's case study) ran at ~95% failure demand — two hours a week of discretionary time. Eight months of "really dull" automation and test-writing got it to 40%, i.e., three days a week. The root problem: "they didn't know how bad their failure demand was because they weren't looking at all the demand."

The scaled-agile orthodoxy of "stable, long-lived feature teams" rests on a myth. Tuckman's forming-storming-norming-performing came from a meta study where 50% of groups were therapy groups and 25% were HR training — "only 25% were actual groups of people doing work." Later studies (US military) show the stages happen concurrently and fast. At ThoughtWorks, shared culture plus a high hiring bar meant new teams were "performing within days, sometimes hours."

Long-lived teams presume homogeneous work. Bank of America's estate — 30-40 ancient legacy systems, work flipping from mainframe procs to SQL tuning quarter to quarter — meant "Scrum isn't set up to deal with that." And some work isn't feature work at all: kaizen, automation, technical debt "need different shapes of teams."

The inversion: instead of shaping demand to fit teams, shape teams to fit demand — "the demand is the one thing... that's the fixed piece." Hence demand-led planning: make all demand visible, allocate discretionary spend, pitch it to everyone in one room, and let people self-assemble under explicit constraints.

Constraints do the managing. Governing constraints (3-10 people; full autonomy defined as never blocked on another team; all pitched work must be done) plus enabling constraints (movers, tenure) steer behavior — e.g., "only two people are allowed to stay in the team" to force tacit knowledge out of people's heads.

Identity belongs to the program, not the squad. "The team is the whole group... you happen to be with this configuration of half a dozen jokers for the next quarter." Spotify's 2012 tribes had the right idea but "got stuck into squads" being long-lived and stable; squad membership should be "kind of an implementation detail."

Leadership and management decouple. The lead is "the person who cares enough about that work to pitch it"; a manager "manages a system of work that has people in it," not people. Appraisals and recruiting go out of band to guilds.

Autonomy is a product of alignment, not its opposite. Rules of the road are what let anyone drive anywhere. Autonomy without alignment is anarchy; alignment without autonomy is autocracy. The goal is "the minimum amount of guidance, the minimum constraints, such that people can do good work" — then "they will surprise you."

It compounds. John Lewis went from two exhausting days with literal tears to planning 100 people's quarter "by lunchtime" — because after a few cycles, planning becomes deltas: what's changed, what's new, where to optimize.

3. Tools, practices, and methodologies: the demand-led planning playbook

The demand taxonomy — Five kinds of work: delivery, discovery (problem-space + solution-space), kaizen, BAU, failure demand; first three discretionary, last two mandatory. Use it to see "all the demand" before planning — mandatory demand is "hiding in plain sight," and backlogs only ever show delivery.

Failure demand measurement — Track what fraction of capacity goes to work that shouldn't exist. HP LaserJet drove theirs from 95% to 40% by obsessing about the number and doing "really dull" automation and test-writing for eight months. Why: it's the cheapest capacity you'll ever find.

Kaizen as improvement of a system of work — Broader than process improvement: technical improvements, automation, learning and development. Teaching someone a thing is "first class work" because it changes the system. Why: kaizen "gradually gives you more and more discretionary spend."

Demand-led planning (formerly "business mapping and delivery mapping") — Quarterly whole-program event: identify all demand, allocate discretionary spend, sponsors pitch, everyone self-organizes under constraints. Works at 30-200 people; sweet spot 60-80. Do it in person — "You can do this virtually. It's a bit rubbish."

The no-zero rule for spend allocation — Set the feature/kaizen/discovery ratio each quarter ("maybe we're going to go 80, 20 this quarter"), but no lever hits zero: features are your "walking around money, your reputation"; no kaizen means nothing improves; no discovery means "you're flying in the dark."

Governing constraints — 3-10 people per team (under three and "you're always in meetings"; over ten and "you're getting in each other's way"); full autonomy, defined precisely as "this team can get work done without ever being blocked on another team" — requiring skills, knowledge, experience, relationships, permission, and resources; and all pitched work must be done: "that's the commitment, our commitment as a group of people."

Bumblebees (or butterflies) — People explicitly not allocated to teams, roving between them. Heuristics: max three teams per bumblebee (context switching), max three bumblebees per team (bringing people up to date). "That number three is a heuristic... Try things out, see what works for you."

Enabling constraints — Tuned to encourage behavior: min/max movers (John Lewis started with "only two people are allowed to stay" to force knowledge transfer, later flipped to "no more than two people can leave a team every quarter" to stabilize operating metrics) and min/max tenure (stay at least two quarters, no more than two or three).

Big hairy audacious goals as aspirational OKRs — Each new team declares a BHAG ("our movie title") with stretch key results. Aspirational means no punishment: automating two-thirds of a ten-step process "was an epic quarter. Well done, team."

Flow metrics — Throughput (things done in the last two weeks), lead time ("that's not a number, that's a range" — two days to 14 months), work in process. Teams define their own "things" — an iOS team's differ from a data platform team's. Why: newly formed teams need to measure themselves from day one. (He name-checks Nigel Thurlow.)

Forward-looking OKR reviews — Always "how much is left," never "what have you done." Why: "the plan is going to change"; the review finds the best way from here to the goal.

Academically robust Likert survey — Post-event survey built with an academic student, because "academically defensible Likert surveys are incredible. Most surveys are not defensible" (cites Nicole Forsgren on survey design). Statements like "I feel connected to the work I'm doing" scored 4s and 5s — evidence people connect to chosen work in a way they didn't when "the work was just handed to them in a backlog."

Preparation kit: road shows, lunch-and-learns, AMAs, pitch coaching — Because "the scariest thing is uncertainty." Coach sponsors on pitching (product managers are natural; engineering managers less so; by the second or third session they're polished). Get the suits — money, risk, compliance — comfortable "with me taking 100 people in a bag and shaking them up."

Facilitation practices — Two or three facilitators who've done it before, working before and during the event; listen to them ("they're seeing things that you're not seeing"); big room, natural light, "fresh fruit and coffee as well as rubbish candy"; contain the loudmouths, draw in the quiet wallflower "who probably has enormous domain knowledge"; expect forming and storming in real time — "minutes or hours," not weeks.

Guild-owned employee lifecycle — Appraisals and recruiting happen out of band, owned by guilds/practices: "have testers appraising other testers because they speak the same language." Why: a team lead appraising a tester has a conflict of interest — "if the team is having a lousy time and the tester is doing brilliant work, the tester is going to have a lousy review."

Break bread — End every planning cycle with the whole group mingling ("we all go to the pub or whatever your preferred way of breaking bread is"). Why: the program is the real team; this is where belonging gets built.

4. Unanswered questions and omissions

What do you do at 500 people? John Lewis called to say the 90-person version "doesn't work for 500," and his answer was "It's not supposed to. There's other things that you do at that scale" — those other things are never described. The talk's ceiling is ~200 people, acknowledged but not resolved.

What happens to unstaffed work and unpicked people? He jokes about the lonely reporting project and "this one sad person," and one contractor once walked across the room to save the day — but there's no answer for when the room doesn't self-correct, or for the person nobody picks.

Where's the counter-evidence? He demolishes Tuckman for weak methodology (75% of groups "immediately disqualified themselves"), then offers anecdotes in return — "observed in an empirical, anecdotal sense... before Dave Snowden comes for me with pitchforks." No throughput, quality, or retention data comparing fluid vs. stable teams; the only measurement shown is a sentiment survey.

Careers, pay, and promotion are explicitly deferred. Decoupling seniority from management and moving appraisals to guilds is "a separate whole separate topic." How promotion works when your lead changes every quarter is never addressed.

Deep specialization vs. self-assembly. BoA had 30-40 ancient legacy systems. If only a handful of people understand the mainframe procs work, can a self-organizing room actually staff it? Bumblebees cap at three teams — what happens when demand for a scarce expert exceeds supply?

Long-horizon ownership. The Q&A answer — "the program team owns the world" — covers on-call, but not architectural stewardship, multi-year product coherence, or who's accountable for a system's evolution when everyone rotates off it.

The cost objection is never engaged. 100 people for two days, quarterly, plus "you do actually want to fly everyone to the same location." The only rebuttal offered is the rage-quit anecdote.

Remote and distributed organizations are dismissed, not solved. "You can do this virtually. It's a bit rubbish, not going to lie." No answer for globally distributed companies.

People who need stability. Enabling constraints force movement (max tenure, max stayers). Quarterly mandatory re-teaming may be brutal for people with caring responsibilities, health needs, or deep-focus specialisms. No opt-out is discussed.

Work that doesn't slice into autonomous 3-10 person chunks. The governing constraint says a team must never be blocked on another team, but no method is offered for demand that inherently spans teams within a quarter.

Bank of America New York is diagnosed but never resolved. We learn why Scrum failed there (heterogeneous estate, quarter-on-quarter work shifts) but not whether fluid teams were applied or fixed it.

The incumbent middle-management layer. If leads emerge from pitching and managers manage systems of work, what happens to the existing engineering managers? One rage-quitting program manager "is fine now" — the broader political transition is untouched.

Budgeting and funding cycles. Quarterly demand-led planning implies quarterly funding, but corporate budgeting is annual. He says the people with the money must be comfortable, but never explains how the money actually flows.

How to start. Prerequisites are listed (evangelize, coach, facilitators, venue), but there's no pilot guidance, no sequencing, and no failure modes beyond "tears and tantrums."
