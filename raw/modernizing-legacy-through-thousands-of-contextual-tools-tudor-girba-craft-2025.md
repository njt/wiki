Modernizing Legacy Through Thousands of Contextual Tools — Tudor Girba, Craft 2025

1. Key Points: Code Reading Is Your Biggest Expense, and Nobody Has Ever Optimized It

The core diagnosis: Developers read code more than half their working time (he's "asked thousands of people" and cites studies), yet nobody ever talks about how they read — only about what they read. Not discussed → not explicit → never optimized. He calls this "the single largest expense we have in our job" and asks the audience to spend "at least 15 minutes per day" on it.

The historical analogy that carries the whole talk: Code reading today is "where testers were 25 years ago" — bottom-left on the Wardley map (manual, genesis). Automated testing won by becoming "little tools" that compress arbitrarily large functionality into a green/yellow/red signal. Swap the word "test" for "tool" and the same transformation applies to every question you have about a system.

When testing was transformed, the old world collapsed: Processes built on manual regression "went boop, they disappeared." Conversations shifted from scattered data points to coherent models, and engineering became hypothesis-driven, "much more like a scientific method."

Scale is a choice, not a fate: LifeWare (Swiss insurance, ~35M lines of code, ~70M with artifacts) runs 150,850 tests in 18 minutes on a cluster — versus Kent Beck's 4,000 tests taking 20 minutes in 2002. "The size of the system doesn't really matter, and it doesn't have to matter unless you're doing things manually."

Tools come to you, not the reverse: "I don't go to the tools. Tools come to me." The "I" in IDE means integrated — "every time you leave, the I has failed." Digital boundaries are artificial; you can manufacture any experience you need.

Reading is the last step, not the first: "The editor is actually the last thing you have seen. It is only when you know what to read, then maybe reading makes sense." Every act of reading should trigger the question: what view would let me see this problem instead?

TDD has a limit: TDD presupposes you already know the assertion. Often you need to see the narrative first, explore, then decide — "It's not always hypothesis first." And tools must be domain-specific: "My tools are not generic. My tools know about that specific problem."

Ephemeral tools are legitimate: Not every tool deserves polish. The Polish-translation use case was "ephemeral" — he built "enough of a tool to do the job," assembled at development time, then scaled it via the cluster for system-wide coverage.

Two roles, inverted budget: Stakeholders (anyone with a stake) have the right to ask questions; facilitators manufacture answers as micro-tools. Today most budget goes to manual inspection — "digging through some mine to find the nuggets" — and the energy should flip to using information.

The endgame for legacy: Once answers are cheap, legacy stops being a defensive modernization problem and becomes leverage for "creating new value that was not possible before."

3. Tools, Practices, and Methodologies

Moldable development — The named methodology: manufacture thousands of small, contextual tools, each answering one specific question about your system. Adoption path: start at the bottom of the map, decrease time to answer. Site: moldabledevelopment.com.

Wardley mapping — The framing device: X-axis evolution (genesis → commodity), Y-axis visibility. Used to locate code reading at bottom-left and to split the map into "use information" (top) vs. "extract information" (bottom).

Tests-as-tools framing — Automated tests compress functionality into green/yellow/red signals; yellow means "the assertion has been invalidated, so the hypothesis no longer holds." Treat tests as the template for all other tools.

Glamorous Toolkit — The environment in the demo: free, open source, "no licensing model whatsoever," 15 years of work. Positioned not as something to adopt for your problems but as "the first large case study of moldable development" to learn from.

Inspectors + contextual views — Inspect live objects (test results, clusters, machines, workers, tasks) and pivot views on the same object to group, drill down, or switch hypotheses (e.g., from "slow job" to "out of memory") while reusing prior tools.

Comparison-failure workflow — A domain-aware failure class: the view highlights the pixel diff in the rendered document, is portable directly into business conversations, and supports one-click accept that auto-refactors assertions — individually or as a batch for all same-cause failures.

Cluster introspection — Every developer on the 100+ person team gets thousands of AWS processors on demand. Views show allocated machines, per-worker performance, spikes, and task timelines; red bubbles flag tasks running longer than the preceding job, breaking scheduling compression.

Embedded results — Executing a test yields the resulting application with a URL, rendered as a fully interactive browser inside the development environment.

Instrumented runtime + source queries — Take one test case exercising a use case, instrument the execution, and query all code points involved (e.g., translations). The query "takes runtime information and source code information," yielding a dedicated browser per hit, plus snippet rewriting with a diff view and accept button.

Distributed analysis jobs — Wrap the query in a job, scatter it over the cluster, and get "places that have to be changed" with coverage for the whole system.

The reading trigger — "Anytime you read, ask yourself: what would be an interesting view that helps me not read this, but see through this particular problem?"

Stakeholder / facilitator split — Stakeholders ask questions; facilitators manufacture answers as micro-tools; the environment composes them into larger narratives, and views "grow and compound" over time.

Guiding metrics: time to answer, time to question — "Not metrics that you want to optimize for, but metrics that you want to guide through." Start by shrinking time to answer; the time-to-question bottleneck reveals itself.

Pixel-identical replay (LifeWare practice) — Rebuild a system from data alone by replaying it and demanding identical customer documents — TDD at the scale of an entire business.

"Rewilding Software Engineering" — The book (with Simon Worley, per the transcript), written in the open, free, at moldabledevelopment.com; he explicitly requests feedback on content and presentation.

4. Unanswered Questions and Omissions

The cold-start problem — the biggest one. The talk is titled "modernizing legacy," but the case study is a company doing TDD since 2002 with 150,000 tests, full instrumentation, and a live moldable environment. It never stopped being modernized. How do you bootstrap thousands of tools on a system with no tests, no instrumentation, and no reflective IDE — i.e., actual legacy? Never addressed.

Cost is a black box. The only concrete figure is "a couple of days" for the translation script. No headcount, budget, or ROI for 2,000 tools. The maintenance question in Q&A is answered by analogy to tests ("build tools for managing those tools"), not by any shown mechanism.

Stack portability is asserted, not demonstrated. The demo is Glamorous Toolkit end to end. The claim that the body of work covers "all sorts of different technologies, all sorts of different languages" gets one sentence. How contextual live-object views work against a Java monolith or a COBOL mainframe — where you can't inspect running objects in an IDE — is the gap most audiences will hit first.

Tool rot and trust. What happens when a view is stale or a query is wrong — especially with one-click batch refactoring of 36 tests? No versioning, ownership, validation, or deprecation story for tools.

Discovery at scale. "Tools come to you" — but the context-detection mechanism is never explained. With thousands of tools per system, how are they organized, found, and deduplicated? The Q&A answer ("you just see a few that made sense in little context") asserts the problem away.

The AI-shaped elephant. A 2025 talk about making code reading cheap, with zero mention of LLMs or AI assistants and whether they change the calculus. Conspicuous by its absence.

The named-but-unsolved bottleneck. He identifies "time to the interesting question" as the real constraint and offers no practice for improving it beyond "start a conversation."

Organizational replicability. LifeWare's "peculiar" two-decade culture of treating software engineering as a competitive advantage is explicitly flagged as unusual — yet there's no adoption path, facilitator training, or management buy-in guidance for companies without that culture.

The demo was staged. "I edited a five minute wait. I cheated a little bit." Real-world messiness — flaky tests, non-determinism, environments you can't mirror live from a conference stage — never appears.

No outcome data. No before/after metrics from LifeWare (throughput, defect rates, time-to-answer trends); persuasion rests entirely on the demo. Also unexamined: the cost of giving every developer thousands of on-demand AWS processors, and the security posture of live remote access into an insurer's development environment.

Generality of the magic. The comparison-failure workflow depends on a system with crisp, pixel-comparable outputs (insurance documents). How the approach generalizes to systems without such clean exterior constraints isn't shown — and the Wardley map, introduced at length, ends up doing almost no analytical work beyond placing reading at bottom-left.
