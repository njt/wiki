Beyond the Warehouse: Data Stacks That Actually Work — Thomas in 't Veld (Tasman Analytics)

1. Key Points: Lean Stack, Static Target, One Truth

The 85% failure statistic is a hook, not the story. Citing VentureBeat's "85% of data projects fail," in 't Veld diagnoses three recurring failure modes from ~60–70 client engagements over six years: over-designing the stack, neglecting data modeling, and failing at activation. His three takeaways: prioritize stack choices by business value; don't underestimate data modeling; build semantic layers because they're "critical to do anything with AI in analytics."

An analytics stack is not a production engineering stack. Production systems can tolerate duplicate events (better to send a pro-subscription event twice than miss it); analytics systems can't, because duplicates double-count revenue. Event streams are not logs — logs absorb duplication and unstructured data; analytics pipelines must be unique, complete, and objective.

Prioritize ingestion by business output, not completeness. List the insights stakeholders need (campaign performance, revenue reporting, A/B analysis, user journeys), rank them, and load sources in that order. "In most subscription startups... revenue reporting trumps everything else" — so RevenueCat subscription data goes in first. Otherwise you spend three months building ingestion before thinking about use cases.

Shift left: fix data at source. Raw third-party data is messy and "probably wrong when you load it." Understand Facebook's pivots and schemas, app store limitations, attribution quirks — before building models on top. Don't try to repair source problems in the data model.

The domain model is the talk's centerpiece: a "narrow waist" and a static target. Query-driven development — bolting on a new query each time a business question appears — is "the most surefire way to get your data stack and your data team into trouble." Instead, recognize the business is a relatively static target (users, transactions, app events, campaigns persist through growth), and model entities once in the middle of the stack. He combines Inmon (entity modeling in the warehouse) with Kimball (dimensional/one-big-table modeling in the data marts). The superpower: swap HubSpot for Salesforce and only the extraction logic changes — downstream models and dashboards survive.

Infrastructure as code, all the way down. GUI-heavy modern data tools are his stated frustration. Data engineering is "25, 30% actually building the pipelines and it's 70, 75% building the testing suite," run through CI/CD on every change.

Activation is the #1 problem even in successful builds. Companies spend heavily on stacks and consulting, "and then still end up with the marketing team downloading raw data into Excel to do their own pivots." His four pillars: presentational models linked to decisions (not teams); very short feedback loops; treating every insight as a data product with scope and acceptance criteria; and internal analytics on your own dashboards ("is my CEO actually looking at it every single day. And the answer is probably no").

The semantic layer: defined once, used everywhere. It carries not just metrics but entities, dimensions, and join rules, is version-controlled, and is enforced in CI/CD — "the only possible way that you can get safe automation and... safe AI agentic applications." Three build paths: homegrown text files, dbt's semantic layer, or Omni. In Q&A he added pragmatism over purity (Databricks' native metrics are fine), and: "If you end up with two different types of semantic layers, you've done it wrong."

Security through modeling. The main cyber risk is querying raw data — it exposes PII and opens the door to prompt injection and SQL injection. A well-designed data model that surfaces only safe, business-actionable data is the security control.

2. Pithy and Provocative Quotes

"The world is littered with the corpses of companies that did everything right. They spent €200,000 on the best possible data stack. Still couldn't make a good decision." — His framing of why tool choice alone doesn't save you.

"Your data stack is not a production engineering stack." — The core principle behind event uniqueness and tracking plans.

"Don't treat the data source as canonical gospel. It will probably be wrong when you load it." — On why shift-left beats cleanup in the model.

Treating event streams as logs "makes data modeling really, really, really shitty down the line." — His blunt case for tracking plans.

On conference-goers proudly displaying sprawling dbt DAGs: "Turns out people are really proud of this stuff... I get the hair on my arms starting to stand up... This is not a good data model." Data modeling, he adds, "is a bit of a lost art" — more preached than practiced.

"At that point you're just building vanity projects rather than something that helps your business forward." — On building dashboards or agents in a vacuum.

"I've never met a stakeholder who's able to describe in detail exactly the type of dashboard reporting that they need from scratch." — Why a dashboard is the start of a discussion, not an endpoint.

"If you end up with two different types of semantic layers, you've done it wrong." — His zero-tolerance line on metric inconsistency.

On legacy, inconsistent event data: "Burn it all, start from scratch... burn it with petrol and fire and just move on." People "overestimate the importance of old event data" — it's written in stone and can't be regenerated anyway.

"Don't use MySQL is probably my main recommendation there." — His database advice for a non-data person: just put your credit card into Databricks or Snowflake.

"The worst thing you can do is try to do Agile while still trying to pre plan everything six months ahead of time." — The only certainty he claims about methodology.

"Keep receipts." — Keep copies of the emails inviting stakeholders to definition sessions, so you can point to when they had their chance to define the logic.

"Always assume your stakeholder does not know the data as well as you do." — And if they still won't use the dashboard: "maybe they're not as data driven as they want to be, which is not your problem, it's their problem."

3. Tools, Practices, and Methodologies

Tools

dlt (DLTHub) — Python-based ingestion-as-code for third-party data. Testable, configurable, integrated into CI/CD so you build "additive functionality rather than accidentally messing something up." A "massive difference from the old world of ingestion tools like Stitch or even Fivetran."

Rudderstack / Snowplow — Event data pipelines with strong auditing of the event architecture; both have built-in tracking-plan support.

Avo — Tracking-plan tool (Icelandic team) for event governance: consensus, scalability, versioning, design-first. Build plans in a UI, collaborate with product/engineering/data teams, version-control and document every event spec, then feed it into your systems. Ensures an iOS button click generates the same event as an Android one.

RevenueCat — Subscription data platform for apps. "If you're not using it to use subscriptions, go use RevenueCat." In subscription startups its data is the priority source because revenue reporting trumps everything.

Databricks / Snowflake (vs. BigQuery, Redshift, MotherDuck) — Cloud warehouses, "pretty equivalent" in functionality; differentiated in unstructured data and AI/ML support. Their access-permission structures are "probably their biggest moat" versus newcomers. For beginners: "just put your credit card into Databricks or Snowflake."

Prefect — Orchestration.

dbt — Transformation, used "almost exclusively still" — though he's "looking at alternatives" given how dbt is evolving. The dbt semantic layer (cloud/enterprise plans only) exposes metrics as a robust, performant API for building applications at scale.

Omni — BI built by the old Looker team after Google's acquisition; metrics-first, infrastructure-as-code everywhere, version-controlled, with a good browser interface. At small fast-growing teams it doubles as the semantic layer with no extra subscription. Prefers dimensional models.

Metabase, Hex, notebooks — Consumption tools for different use cases, all drinking from the same source of truth.

Looker — Pioneered the YAML-configured semantic layer 5–8 years ago; integrates with Snowflake RBAC so C-level-only reports enforce warehouse-level access too.

Sigma — BI tool more comfortable with one-big-table models than dimensional ones — pick modeling style to match consumption patterns.

Elementary (plus a second testing tool, garbled in the transcript) — dbt-native testing/monitoring to keep the stack accurate.

Homegrown semantic layer — Literally "a bunch of text files" specifying dimensions, base queries, key join rules, and canonical metric definitions, fed into an agentic engine. Got him "correct SQL consistent every single time." Integrates with agents but not easily with BI tools.

Practices

Shift left — Fix data issues at source, not in the data model; also applies to data retention, which he says should be owned by data engineering.

Business analysis via Scrum — Sprint 1 analyzes tasks, sprint 2 delivers; shared roadmap and backlog. Prioritization of data sources comes from ranking the insights stakeholders need.

Domain model as a paper exercise — Half a day, stakeholders in one room, define central logic "once and for all": what is a user, does a user need an ID or just an email. Do it before writing anything.

The narrow waist — Entity modeling in the warehouse (users, transactions, subscriptions), fanning out into dimensional or one-big-table data marts for consumption. Vendor-proof and query-change-proof.

Data products, not dashboards — Apply product thinking (scope, acceptance criteria, who/what/why) to every piece of insight, documented up front.

Growth loops / internal analytics — Measure dashboard usage via query logs (Snowflake, Databricks, MotherDuck) and BI usage analytics; learn, adjust, iterate — weekly.

Buy tools, build the glue — Never build what's off-the-shelf (e.g., text-to-SQL engines); do build your own CI/CD layer and tests. Enterprise with extreme event volumes may be the exception.

Never let agents query raw data — The data model is the security boundary against PII exposure, prompt injection, and SQL injection.

Data mesh — Name-dropped as the philosophy for aligning metric definitions across multiple data teams in large orgs; details deferred to a hallway conversation.

4. Unanswered Questions and Omissions

The 85% stat is never interrogated. It's the hook, but he offers no evidence his approach moves the number — no failure post-mortems, no before/after from his own 60–70 clients, no success metrics for a stack that "actually works" beyond dashboard-usage analytics.

Enterprise is waved off. "Enterprise, very different beast altogether" — and when data mesh came up as the multi-team answer, he deflected: "Come talk to me afterwards. Long discussion." The hardest organizational problem in the talk is a hallway conversation.

The dbt elephant in the room. He name-checks the dbt–Fivetran merger, says he's "looking at alternatives... given how DBT as a tool is evolving," and never says what's wrong with dbt or what the alternatives are. Meanwhile the dbt semantic layer sits behind paid cloud/enterprise plans — flagged as "sometimes a blocker" for exactly the startups he serves, and left unresolved.

What happens when the business does change fundamentally? The whole architecture rests on "as long as your business doesn't change fundamentally" — pivots, M&A, new product lines are unaddressed. And there's an unresolved tension with his own claim that "you almost never have the time to try and go back and fix a broken data model," versus his later "the best time to fix it is a year ago and the second best time is today."

Thin AI content for an AI & Data Summit. MCP is dismissed in half a sentence ("That's not just building an MCP server"), agents appear mainly as a consumption pattern for the semantic layer, and there's nothing on agent evaluation, hallucination risk, or how to actually operationalize agentic analytics beyond a reference to yesterday's demo.

The Power BI Copilot question was dodged. Asked how to harmonize Databricks and Power BI Copilot, he answered about vendor lock-in and "SQL is a commodity language" — never the harmonization steps.

Definition politics are sidestepped. "Have a marketing lead and a sales qualified lead if you need to" is a workaround, not an answer to who owns the semantic layer when stakeholders genuinely disagree.

"Burn it with petrol" vs. retention obligations. Telling teams to torch untrustworthy event data sits awkwardly next to his own point that financial data must be kept 10–15 years — and he admits the audit-and-repair path is "a very expensive migration job, which actually we don't really like to do. Come talk to us about anything else, but not that part."

The "people problem" never arrives. He opens by saying scaling data teams is "primarily a people problem" and promises his "one opinion" on running data teams — the talk then stays almost entirely technical, and the opinion is never really delivered.

Missing topics you'd expect: data contracts, streaming/real-time requirements, unstructured-data modeling for AI workloads (despite citing it as the warehouses' differentiator), and any depth on data quality tooling beyond a passing mention.
