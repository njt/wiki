# Conference notes: Digital and sexual citizenship in an age of social media bans

This conference is an initiative of the ECU Ethical Digital Futures Group.

**Uncle Kevin Fitzgerald** opened by reflecting on what it means to be on Noongar boodjar, and on the recent influence of American culture on Australia. Welcome to Country is a way of paying respect to everyone, including young people and old people. 

**Prof Tama Leaver: All Stick, No Carrot? Deploying Australia’s social media ban for under 16s**

This talk covered the history behind the Australian Social Media Minimum Age Amendment; evaluations of whether it works; and discussion of where we might go from here.

Part of the origin story is Peter Malinauskas and his partner Annabel West read Haidt's *The Anxious Generation*, and then looking at some way to respond in legislation. Jonathan Haidt sets out a firm and unambiguous argument that social media is harming young people. This has been incredibly politically influential, with Malinauskas and others arguing that, "the evidence is clear that social media is causing our children harm".

These claims were picked up by the 36 Months campaign, created by an ad company. This was conceptualised as an advocacy *and advertising* campaign right from the start. That was then picked up from News Corp, which had just recently found out that Facebook and other social media companies were pulling out of the News Media Bargaining Code (which would have funnelled money towards Australian news organisations). 

Tama argues that the evidence on this is not as clear as Haidt, politicians, 36 months, or News Corp have claimed. He cites Candice Odgers' vital critique of Haidt's work, which draws on her extensive, long-running research in the area, and Jeff Hall's review of the literature which demonstrates that the evidence is much muddier.

Tama Leaver joined other academics and advocacy organisations that argued against the bans, many of whom also pointed out that children were largely excluded from conversations about framing these bans.

It is clear that the Australian government approach to youth social media bans fits the definition of a moral panic, and can be seen as a (spectacularly effective) act of 'regulatory theatre' which primarily acts to convince parents that action is being taken on the harms of social media to young people.

The 'exemption framework' that was intended to be built into Australian legislation was meant to encourage social media platforms to create a 'safe and healthy' version of their apps that are more age-appropriate. However, this was traded away between the ALP and the Liberals at the last minute, allegedly because the Liberals did not want the ALP to be able to 'go soft' on Big Tech.

Once it came to trialling how the ban would be implemented, there was a huge problem: how do you actually verify someone's age, especially given that they decided that government-issued ID cannot be the only form of ID accepted. They decided to use a 'waterfall approach', stacking a range of different age estimation tools. This approach...has some flaws. To say the least.

There was an odd distinction made between platforms that were banned (eg. Facebook, Instagram, Reddit, Snapchat, Youtube) and not banned (most controversially, Roblox).

There was a report put out in late 2025 (with Tama on the team) on Young Australians' Perspectives on the Social Media Minimum Age Legislation. Young people didn't think the ban would work, and also wanted to emphasise that they enjoy social media and use it as a space for creativity and connection.

Once the ban came into effects, the Australian government, Meta, Snapchat, Tiktok, and others started claiming huge numbers of accounts being deactivated. Headspace started talking about hearing more from young people worried about the potential impact of the ban. Early research suggests that, shockingly, young people were still using the platforms they were 'banned' from. One report suggested that the only way to make the ban work would be for a significant proportion of young people to decide that they agreed with the bans.

Anika Wells started talking about the 'addictive dopamine drip' of particular social media features, and in March 2026 quietly put through a change to the definition of social media, which includes recommender systems, endless feeds, feedback systems, and time-limited material.

In response to hearing that 85% of young people in Australia are still using these platforms despite bans, the Australian government said they would double the fines social media platforms might face (this has gone to a committee, in part because of Greens and Liberal pushes). However, no platforms have yet been fined at all.

In the wake of the youth social media ban, and efforts to push these bans overseas, there have been great efforts from researchers to start making their work on the problems with youth social media bans more accessible, including Panic First, Evidence Later?

Tama argues that if anything good is to come out of all this, it'll be an increased focus on digital literacy and the development of a Digital Duty of Care (which was already in development *before *bans came up).

From here, we should do more to listen to young people, not rely on flawed age estimation technolody, and actually look at the evidence.

During the questions, Tama argued that we should absolutely avoid a version of a Digital Duty of Care in Australia (currently in development) which focuses on increased parental surveillance of young people's Internet use. He argued that we already do too much to treat children's lives as something that parents should surveil though a series of digital dashboards.

**Panel: Face Value: Image culture, bodies, and identity on social ****media****Amy Dobson, Julia Coffey, Kiah Hawker**

Young people's selfie practices are often pathologised. This research (which has just been published!) looked at what young people are actually doing when they create and edit selfies. it showed that young people analyse images in forensic detail, unpacking not just whether edits have been used for selfies but also have effective that editing has been. Young people tend to see obvious editing as crass, but also see it as vital that selfies show the 'right' image.

As part of the research they sat down with young people and watched their process of taking and editing selfies. Researchers initially saw selfie editing as a social experience, but during workshops it became clear that for most young people it's private and 'shame-based' - it was only during group workshops that young people learned more about other people's practices.

During these workshops, young people came up with the term 'structural edits' to refer to changing the shape of the body in images, from changing waist shape through to smaller edits (like shaving down the bridge of the nose), and this is seen as 'breaking the rules'. Young people saw 'touch ups' like removing a birthmark or freckle as more acceptable. Part of the distinction young people make is between edits that make you look 'like I would on my best day' as opposed to the kinds of edits that would require surgery to achieve.

They note that there's so much moralising that goes along with body image, social media use, presentation of different images, and everything bundled up here. Many young people talked about a sense of shame at having engaged in heavy editing (often framing this as a past practice). Some young people in the workshops still engaged in heavy editing, and were able to articulate why in complex ways.

Many young people talked about bullying (including related to gender and sexuality) and self-image pressures, particularly through high school, and a tentative and fragile sense of progress around their self image. For example, some young people talked about a shift towards 'body neutrality' or acceptance of their bodies, rather than body *positivity*. Some young people felt shame about continuing to edit their images, but did it anyway - feeling 'critical but caught' (as Roz Gill put it). 

One young person talked about how they would be relieved if the editing technology they used was banned - that they would be forced to just deal with how they look.

Young people often seem to be reading adult discourses around selfies into their practices, while also really understanding the gendered, racialised, and classed social structures that shaped their body image and presentation.

Kiah's work focuses on a deep analysis of the technical tools themselves - how the tech that underpins filters and editing tools is rapidly changing. We're seeing a shift from augmented reality filters to generative AI and more complex machine vision. This is an important shift because it's not longer a filter that sits on top of the image - it's technology that creates something 'new'.

Previous filters kind of preserved privacy in the ways that they overlaid images and features on faces. EffectHouse, Tiktok's free editing tool, automates editing processes in new ways. It makes it much easier for people to create filters and share them, often with limited understanding of what is actually happening. Kiah tried to use EffectHouse to break Tiktok's terms of service, which it immediately did.

In 2025, Meta moved to bring all filter creation in-house. This means there's no longer a public code of conduct or moderation process - only their creators can make filters, and there's no public oversight into what the rules are around that.

When the technology applies 'beautifying' filters automatically, it's harder to unpack and unerstand.

**Dr John Sutcliffe, From Moral Panic to Digital Paternalism: rethinking youth social media governance**

Sutcliffe draws on a critical youth work perspective, talking about how the focus has been on harm and protection, with limited discussion of participation and rights. He draws on the anology of the beach - it's a big part of Australian culture, it's a lot of fun, but it's also dangerous. We don't ban people from the beach, we educate them to try to make it safer.

We often treat teenagers as less deserving of rights, while still requiring them to stand up to high standards of behaviour. We put "more and more hoops" in place of young people driving. Moral panics are often targetted at parents. We are presented with a paternalistic idea that parents (and particularly 'father') know best. This approach presumes that adults are more mature, logical, and competent than young people.

Digital paternalism revolves around a mode of governance that is pre-emptive, infrastructural, protectionist, and reconfigures the agency of young people.

Digital participation requires involvement, agency, expression, learning, and expression. Digital paternalism *reduces* participation, autonomy, civic engagement, and experiental learning. 

If we take a rights-based approach to regulation of digital spaces we might ask questions like:

- Is the current restriction proportionate to the risks?
- Does it interfere with participation more than necessary?
- Have young people been genuinely consulted?

We can look at how to build children's skills, and engage in co-design instead of excluding youth from spaces.

**Prof Leila Green: How should parents react when government policy undermines children's digital citizenship?**

[Warning: there's significant discussion of youth suicide in this section]

Green notes that Albanese's claim that he's "spoken to thousands of parents, grandparents, aunties and uncles" who were worried about children's use of the Internet. In media attention to the ban, only one young person Leonardo Puglisi had a significant voice.

Green sets the question out: what if we spent a fraction of the resources expended in the social media ban on, instead, educating kids about cyberbullying dynamics, reframing the perpetrator as having done wrong (rather than the victim, as is often the case?

Haidt focused on families' losses of children to suicide - and in particular white, middle-class families. While youth suicide in Australia remains a tragic issue that needs addressing, the numbers of children lost to suicide are dropping - from 110 in 2020 down to 70 in 2024. The children most at risk of suicide in Australia are not white, middle-class kids. Indigenous kids, rural kids, and queer kids are most at risk.

Leila Green asks what better purposes the time, money, and energy spent on youth social media bans (and the campaigns for them) might have served if they'd focused on disabling Big Tech's harmful algorithms, and on helping the communities of young Australian children and teens most at risk of self-harm and death by suicide.

Ultimately, it makes sense for parents to keep doing what they were doing in terms of thinking through their parenting around digital spaces. Australian parents weren't the issue in this case, and they had relatively limited power during 'consulation'.

**Prof Alan McKee, Sexual Shame and Healthy Sexual Development**

McKee argues that we don't know what the social media ban is actually trying to achieve: this morphs depending on who you ask. Is it meant to address young people's anxiety? Is it doomscrolling? Is it about young people having an unhappy time online? The lack of precision here makes it hard to measure whether it has achieved its goals - and that can be seen as being by design.

Discourses around social media mirror some of the messages around porn, which centre on creating shame: "we can't stop you doing this, but we can make you feel bad about it".

Research by McKee and others worked to set out what was required to help people develop healthy sexual development. He also also worked with others to better understand the effects of pornography - overall, there is no conclusive evidence that pornography leads to negative outcomes like domestic violence and misogyny. However, a lot of people did talk about feeling shame and guilt when they consumed pornography.

This means that young people experiencing problems related to pornography tend not to talk to adults about it, because they feel they have done something wrong. We also see this when it comes to social media bans: if young people feel that they've done something wrong in accessing social media, they're less likely to talk to adults about problems they're having.

McKee argues that we should be giving age-appropriate answers to kids' questions about sex, and making it easy and normal for kids to talk to parents or other trusted adults if they have questions or problems. He particularly recommends this guide.

McKee asked parents what kind of labour is involved in talking to kids about sex and pornography. I asked about how we talk about pornography as just one body of material - how do we talk about conditions of production and other ethical issues. McKee noted that he's carried out research on criteria for finding ethical pornography.
