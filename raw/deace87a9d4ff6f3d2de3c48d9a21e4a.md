Key Points — "Plain Text" Is a 1960s American Teleprinter Fossil, and Every Byte Is Somebody's Opinion

What people actually mean by "plain text" is "7-bit ASCII that opens in Windows Notepad." ASCII was designed for mechanical teleprinters — "a computer was a typewriter with a memory" — not screens. Its quirks are fossilized constraints: Ctrl+C is literally the "end of text" control character (stop printing, something's gone wrong); the Windows CRLF vs. Unix LF split traces to whether your OS lineage had device drivers (Multics → Unix → Linux/Mac) or ran on cheap hardware without them (CP/M → MS-DOS → Windows); DEL is all-bits-set because you can't unpunch a paper tape.

The eighth bit was a regulatory vacuum. Every country invented code pages to reinterpret the top half of ASCII — same bytes, different letters. Data isn't lost in transit; it's reinterpreted. Hence the Harry Potter book delivered to a hand-copied, mangled Moscow address; Billy Joel's live album permanently titled "Kohuept" in streaming databases; and "Chinese in the Windows event logs" that looked like a breach.

Unicode won by being free, universal, and opinion-free. Its mission: "a single, consistent way to represent each letter and symbol needed for all human languages across all computers and devices." But it deliberately refuses to answer the hard questions — it hands you tools (code points, combining characters, normalization forms, collations) and leaves the judgment calls to engineers who mostly don't know they're making them.

"Are these two strings the same?" has no context-free answer. Two renderings of "Motley Crue" can look identical, sound identical, be binary-unequal, and still be equivalent under all four normalization forms. Alphabetical order is cultural, not mathematical: a live show of hands couldn't agree which of four orderings of Berlin/Aachen/Zürich/Aarhus/Örebro was correct, because Latin1_General, Finnish_Swedish, and Danish_Norwegian collations each encode a different alphabet. "Even in this room, we can't agree what alphabetical order means."

Text is political because orthography is political. Aarhus's spelling flips — the 1948 reform distancing Danish from German post-WWII, then the 2011 reversion "because Google wasn't finding it and they thought it was bad for tourism" — determine sort order in your database. Apple makes the Taiwanese flag vanish when your iPhone region is set to mainland China. "If you try to find any technology which has no politics in it, there's nothing there."

Encoding failures impersonate security breaches. The "we've been hacked" incident — Chinese in the event logs — was a faulty network switch dropping one byte every three minutes, shunting UTF-16 text sideways into the CJK block. Twenty-seven hours to diagnose. Weird text in your logs is an encoding story until proven otherwise.

UTF-8 is "one of the most brilliant hacks in the history of technology": the leading-bit rules make every ASCII document ever written valid UTF-8 unchanged, while leaving room for every human language, emoji, and "the friendly aliens from Alpha Centauri."

Emoji is not new machinery — it's old Unicode machinery repurposed: skin tones via combining characters, the female astronaut via zero-width joiner, flags via regional indicators. Apple and Google "donated emoji to Unicode and went, it's your problem now."

The root cause is parochialism, not technology. "Most people grow up with something that just makes sense to them... and then you travel or your company does an integration with somebody in another region and you're like, whoa, I had no idea this could get so complicated." The Hungarian alphabet — 44 letters, 35 on the keyboard, 41 in the Scrabble set, 36 independent in Unicode, digraphs, one trigraph, Q/W/X/Y only for loan words like whiskey — looks obvious to Hungarians and impossible to everyone else. And vice versa.

The takeaway is a shibboleth, not a spec: ask "do you know Pike Matchbox?" — the letters shared between Ukrainian Cyrillic and Latin, visible on every Ukrainian license plate. If they know it, they've thought about encoding. If not, "they're going to send you a file that works on that machine. Maybe it works on your machine, maybe not."

Tools, Practices, and Methodologies — The Encoding Survival Kit

7-bit ASCII — The 128-character teleprinter encoding everyone implicitly means by "plain text." Know its design tricks: digits convert to numbers by masking the top nibble; case-insensitive comparison by clearing one bit; DEL = all bits set because tape.

Code pages (CP437, KOI8) — Per-locale reinterpretations of the eighth bit. Recognize them to diagnose mojibake. CP437's top half gave IBM PCs box-drawing characters, half the Greek alphabet ("you couldn't use code page 437 to write email in Greek, but you could use it to write physics"), and the smiley faces and playing cards you see when a PC "crashes really hard." KOI8 ordered Cyrillic by sound-alike, so text mangled by a lost eighth bit stayed readable.

Unicode code points — One number per letter or symbol, free, covering everything from Egyptian hieroglyphics to Sumerian.

Combining characters — Stackable diacritics (e.g., U+0308) that decorate whatever precedes them; the source of zalgo text and a Samsung Galaxy rendering bug that could "brick somebody's phone."

Normalization forms — NFC (canonical composition: squash to fewest bytes), NFD (canonical decomposition: widest form), NFKC/NFKD (compatibility: maps bubble letters and fraktur to plain equivalents — "it's not the same letters, but it is the same letters"). Use for string comparison: "if you've got one of the systems where people can put little fancy letters in their username and then your search stops working, that's why you need to switch the normalization you use."

Database collations — Latin1_General_CI_AI, Finnish_Swedish (ö is the 29th letter, sorts after Z), Danish_Norwegian (å sorts last). Always specify collation explicitly in comparisons and ORDER BY; never assume there's one right sort order.

The two-column sort pattern — For Danish orthography, "you need two columns in your database because you need one that you're going to display, but you need a different one that you're going to use to sort."

UTF-16 LE/BE — The in-memory encoding of Windows, Java, .NET, and JavaScript. Know your endianness: swapped, "ASCII teleports into the Unicode block that is used for Chinese."

UTF-8 — The wire format. Leading 0 = ASCII; leading 10 = continuation; 110/1110/11110 = start of a 2/3/4-byte sequence. Backward compatible with every ASCII document in existence.

Zero-width joiner ("zwidge") — Composes emoji: woman + ZWJ + rocket = female astronaut; unsupported devices degrade gracefully to "a lady next to a rocket ship."

Regional indicator symbols and tag sequences — How flags work. GB is a country code; England's flag is "a hack" (black flag + tag letters G-B-E-N-G); Windows refuses country flags entirely.

ICAO passport transliteration (å → aa) — The machine-readable zone convention ("readable by 1960s American mainframes") that collided with an airline system that "just take[s] the circle off," leaving Magnus with a passport and ticket that didn't match at the US border.

Pike Matchbox — The shibboleth. Ask it before accepting anyone's "plain text" file.

Incident-response-by-broadcast — Half-joking, but it's literally what he did: posted the mystery log text publicly and got an instant diagnosis. Suspect encoding before assuming breach.

Unanswered Questions and Omissions — The Metadata, the Malice, and the Steelman That Never Show Up

No prescriptive guidance for working developers. Nothing on encoding detection, byte-order marks, charset declarations in HTML/HTTP, or language tags (BCP 47). The natural industry answer to "there is not enough data in the text" is metadata — and it's never named.

Right-to-left scripts get one clause; East Asian legacy encodings get nothing. Arabic and Hebrew are mentioned in passing and dropped. Shift-JIS, GB2312, Big5 — historically the bloodiest encoding wars, and the reason "code pages" broke so badly — are entirely absent.

Security is only ever a false alarm here. Homoglyph attacks, IDN spoofing, lookalike-character phishing never come up, even though the talk spends an hour proving how easily identical-looking text can be different bytes.

The 27-hour incident yields no prevention lesson. What do you do about a switch that drops one byte every three minutes? No checksums, no protocol integrity, no monitoring advice — the story ends at diagnosis.

Unicode's own governance goes unexamined. Who decides what gets encoded? The recurring fight over emoji priority versus still-unencoded minority scripts is never raised — a strange omission in a talk whose thesis is that everything is political.

The Magnus border problem has no proposed fix. No standard change, no airline process change, no traveler recourse. The anecdote ends with "even he had to answer some questions."

The critics' steelman is never engaged. For pure 7-bit ASCII content, "plain text" genuinely does interoperate — that's the whole point of UTF-8's backward compatibility. The YouTube objectors are quoted only for ridicule, never answered.

Folklore and slips presented as settled fact. CP/M "evolved out of a system called Minix" (Minix postdates MS-DOS entirely); Windows skipped 9 "because everyone would think it was Windows 95" (a legend, never officially confirmed); "every letter is going to take two bytes" in UTF-16 (false since surrogate pairs — which his own emoji skin-tone examples require); emoji credited to "Shigetaka Morita" (the widely credited creator is Shigetaka Kurita); ASCII pinned to 1965 (first published 1963).

Nothing on text processing at scale. Search engines, machine translation, LLMs — the systems where these edge cases now matter most — are never mentioned. Nor accessibility: screen readers versus combining-character soup, emoji verbosity for blind users.
