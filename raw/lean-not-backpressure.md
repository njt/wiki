# Lean, not backpressure

The author takes issue with Lucas Costa's framing of a key concept as "backpressure," arguing that the more fitting metaphor is **lean manufacturing**. Where backpressure signals upstream processes to slow down, Costa's actual suggestions are about getting upstream to do things *differently* — prioritizing quality over quantity.

kqr writes that "lean" has two halves: the famous waste-reduction side, and the less celebrated side about "managing the unstable input of people." The latter is what matters here.

## Three Lean Practices Highlighted

1. **Single-piece flow** — working on one item at a time so downstream can reject defective work before too much accumulates.
2. **Autonomation (jidoka)** — equipping machines to detect problems and halt on their own.
3. **Poka-yoke** — designing processes that make correct results inevitable by construction.

## The Robot Angle

The author observes that managers who blame human workers for quality failures suddenly find that logic breaks down with AI coding tools. "It's a robot! It can't be responsible for its actions." This forces a reckoning: processes must be designed to tolerate imperfect output rather than demanding perfection from the worker — whether human or machine.

kqr quotes Deming's insight that "a bad system will beat a good person every time," and adds a favorite safety principle: if your system "requires humans to not make mistakes, then whose fault is this really?"

## Bottom Line

The piece argues that the shift to AI-assisted work makes obvious what was always true: the system, not the individual worker, bears responsibility for quality. "We always had to do that, even with people, but with robots it's painfully obvious."
