1. Key Points — The Retro Exists to Deliver Value; Everything Else Is Ceremony

Retros have decayed into ritual. The talk's origin story: a retro she attended produced "What did we do? Well, communicate. What are we going to do? More communicate." Going through the motions is "not valuable, it's not helpful to anybody. And it's painful." She traces the lineage back to "lessons learned" and post-mortems — "the after death meeting... who can we blame today?" — where you wait until the project dies to reflect, then "move along."

The retro is half of a loop whose other half is the product. Drawing on the Larsen/Derby framework (now V2 with David Horowitz), she insists the retro reinforces the value you're trying to deliver. "You're not doing a retro because it's the end of the month, we do a retro." And it's not team-bonding either: "We're a team, we should be doing trust falls today" is not the objective.

Five stages, and the one everyone skips is load-bearing. Set the Stage, Gather Data, Generate Insights, Make a Decision, Close. Generate Insights — the deliberate-conflict stage — is "the step that people skip the most and then wonder why you're just going through the motions." Skip it and decision-making collapses into hierarchy: "who's ever the most senior person in the room says I think we should do this and everybody goes, okay."

Set the Stage is focus plus motivation, not agenda-recitation. Focus the topic — she "hate[s] the concept of the entire sprint as the focus. How stupid," because it produces the generic "communication" action item. Be explicit about outputs ("one or two highlights, one or two improvements" — she once saw a retro produce 20 action items and "nobody did anything"). State the decision rule upfront, and start with majority, not consensus. Then motivate by making it their problem — gather their stories of pain, then ask "wouldn't it be nice if we could diminish some of that?"

Gather Data should manufacture collaboration before the conflict arrives. Structured brainstorming (solo → pair → four) gives thinking time, safety for half-formed ideas, and forces convergence so people enter the hard part already collaborating — "not a competing situation." It also caps volume: ten people with three ideas each is unworkable.

Generate Insights means engineering disagreement, framed on impact rather than preference. People can both "like" sessions while disagreeing completely on their impact — impact is where insight lives. Dig past vague cards ("registration" could mean anything). Hard rule: "the insight is never a person's name" — behaviors and systems, not individuals. You know the stage is ending when seven options have narrowed to two or three.

Standard decision tools are biased; anonymity is a dysfunction symptom. Dot voting suffers anchoring (don't let the powerful leader vote first) and bandwagoning. Anonymous voting is worse: "The only way we can make a decision is if we don't talk to each other... You don't have a high performing team."

Close on next steps and reinforcement, not summary. "If you have to repeat what you did for the day, it stunk." Celebrate the collaboration-and-conflict behavior itself — that positive reinforcement is what earns you the bigger elephant at the next retro.

Depth is earned iteratively. Don't open with psychological safety as a first topic — "the elephant will be too big." Pick topics in the team's control, give people the right to pass, cap the first retro at 30 minutes, and size duration to the topic, not to Scrum's three-hour timebox.

3. Tools, Practices, and Methodologies — From Ducks to Elephants: The Working Kit

The five-stage retrospective (Larsen/Derby/Horowitz, Agile Retrospectives V2) — Set the Stage, Gather Data, Generate Insights, Decide What to Do, Close. Use it as the spine, keep it tied to the value loop, and never skip stage three.

"Which duck are you today?" check-in — a numbered duck image; everyone says their number aloud. A one-minute vibe check of the room. If everyone's a 2, skip the shout-out activities and lean into thinking activities. It's not goal-setting — it just tells the facilitator how to motivate.

"What should our retro focus on?" board — a standing board where team members post candidate topics, so the focus isn't the Scrum Master's bias. Her answer to the facilitator-bias question in Q&A.

Explicit goal statement — state the intended outputs precisely ("I want one or two highlights, one or two improvements"). Prevents the 20-action-items-that-nobody-does retro.

Decision-rule declaration — say upfront whether the decision will be consensus, majority, or leader-decides-with-input. Undeclared rules burn participation: people who discover mid-retro that they never had a voice won't come back. Start with majority; consensus invites gridlock and "bad power dynamics."

1-2-4-All (her variation of the liberating structure) — think solo, converge in pairs, merge into fours, each level producing one item. Delivers thinking time, psychological safety for imperfect ideas, and — critically — pre-loads collaboration before the conflict stage so it's not "my idea versus yours."

Hand-raise quieting — facilitator raises a hand, the room follows, the current speaker finishes their sentence. Her go-to for large groups. Proven, she notes, on hotel security at a conference suite party.

Impact-over-like framing — sort items by impact, not preference, and explicitly invite disagreement ("Does anybody disagree that that's high impact?"). Two people can both like the sessions while one came for the networking — you only learn that through the impact argument.

"Never a person's name" ground rule — insights must be behaviors, issues, or system contributions. If it needs to be about a person, "do that outside of your retro."

The baby elephant (learned from her colleague Jake) — a plush baby elephant placed on a chair: "The elephant's here. Anybody gonna talk about it?" For unnamed topics. Don't throw it at people, and don't deploy it for topics too big for the team's current maturity — psychological safety is not a first-retro topic.

Numbered pre-committed voting — number the final options; everyone writes their vote on a sticky before walking to the board. Counters anchoring and the unconscious pull to join the winning dot cluster. With heavy power dynamics, skip dot voting entirely (teaming-up risk). Anonymous-then-discuss is the acceptable fallback, "I still am like the nonest part."

Hopes and Wishes (Diana Larsen) — a closing appreciation variant where people offer "I hope... / I wish..." statements. Lets appreciation flow without singling anyone out — she uses it because "it's harder for me to take a compliment than a criticism."

Definition of Ready as a control-circle move — the dev team couldn't fix marketing, so they required marketing copy to be present before work could be pulled. The complaint became a system change, and marketing delivered copy the next sprint. Template for "pick topics in their control."

Right to pass — explicit permission, stated in Set the Stage, to observe any activity. It "gave back a feeling of control" to John and got a full day of participation from a guaranteed holdout.

The 30-minute first retro — "Trisha Broderick. No more than 30 minutes. You can get through all five of these stages in 30 minutes." Duration thereafter follows the topic and activities, not an arbitrary timebox. (Her simulation with the ~80-person room: half a day.)

Topic-trigger cadence — for non-sprint teams, a board that accumulates topics until a threshold triggers the retro.

Whining-team redirect — start from their biggest problem, even if it's lunch. "They don't feel heard," and until that's aired, "they're not gonna be able to participate in your other topics anyways."

Hybrid-as-remote — put the in-room people on the online whiteboard too. "Treat it more like a remote than a hybrid."

Kudos-distribution watch — if one person always (or never) receives kudos, "there might be some signals happening in my team."

Small-team groupthink guard — groupthink can be worse in five-person teams that have been together a while; design activities to balance who's talking (she self-identifies as "an extreme extrovert" who will fill any silence).

4. Unanswered Questions and Omissions — The Elephants Left on the Chair

"Value" is asserted, never defined or measured. The talk is titled "Shifting Towards Value," and the diagram claims the retro reinforces value delivery — but there's no metric, no follow-up loop, and no evidence offered for how you'd know a retro actually produced value. The left half of her own loop diagram is never operationalized.

Action-item follow-through is hand-waved. She assigns an owner in the close ("who's gonna take our action items back") and explicitly declines facilitator ownership — but never addresses the chronic failure mode: what happens at retro N+1 when last retro's actions died quietly. Her own 20-action-items anecdote names the disease; the only cure offered is "narrow it."

She engineers conflict but provides no conflict management. Stage three's whole point is promoting the groan, yet the only guardrail is "never a person's name." No de-escalation, no guidance for when it goes personal anyway, no facilitator intervention moves. The "groan zone" term is used repeatedly without attribution or boundaries.

Remote gets one dismissive sentence. Asked directly for remote tips, the answer is "every single thing I did can be done on an online whiteboarding tool" — no tool named ("I am not going to highlight a specific one publicly unless they start paying me"), no treatment of remote-specific failure modes: disengagement, time zones, async participation, camera-off silence.

The facilitator's own power goes unexamined. The Q&A raises Scrum Master bias; her board suggestion fixes topic selection but not the deeper questions — who should facilitate, whether managers belong in the room, and what happens when the elephant is the boss sitting there. The weaponized-retro anecdote reveals the stakes; the remedy offered is a vague "make it safe in different ways."

Majority rule's losers are ignored. She prescribes majority to dodge consensus gridlock but never addresses the person whose item never wins the vote — the serially-overridden minority that erodes exactly the participation she's trying to build.

Cadence guidance is "it depends." Asked how often to run retros, the answer is that it depends on frequency and that she sometimes runs more than one per sprint. No actual decision criteria.

The gating concept is outsourced to another talk. When a team can handle a psychological-safety retro depends on "collaborating and not just cooperating" — a distinction she repeatedly defers to her keynote. Anyone who only saw this talk is missing the criterion for her most consequential advice (when to bring out the big elephants).

No counterargument to retros at all. The room contained people who came because they're "tired of retrospectives." The implicit answer is "do them better" — never "when shouldn't you do one," or any evidence that well-run retros outperform the alternatives for a given team.
