Key Points: No Checklist, Four Puzzle Pieces — and You Build It by Doing It

Resilience is the ability — yours and the collective's — to handle it "when crap goes down," and crap is the norm. Inland hurricanes, COVID, workplace chaos. Anyone selling a step-by-step recipe is "full of it." What exists instead are patterns: four "pieces of the puzzle," explicitly not the complete picture (a nod to her family's tradition of hiding one puzzle piece from her).

Most "teams" aren't teams. The spectrum runs individuals → groups → teams. A group cooperates — think relay race: four people, each with a part, and "if that second person falls down, no one blames the anchor." You could even get a bonus for hitting your PR while losing the race. A team collaborates — "let's build this together" — which is where better ideas and what she and Diana Larsen call co-intelligence come from. Her indictment: "so many of our organizations are not only organized for groups, but they're optimized for group behavior." That fails the moment knowledge work requires discovering what value even is.

The leader's job is to stop being the hub — and nobody taught leaders how. She was taught strategy, visioning, delegation; never facilitation or mentoring. Job descriptions that say "drive" are structurally at war with self-organization. And when leaders de-hub, "the more panic sound[s]. Uh oh, I have no job."

Chaos reverts teams to groups. Her high-performing self-organizing teams, hit by COVID, "just went, just give me my part, I'll do it when I can at home." Her confession: the thing she should have been doing more as a leader was teaching the team to be resilient and to learn.

Compassion is a workplace requirement, built through intentional collaboration. "It's business, it's not personal" is a line from "a fictional movie about a mob family. Stop it." Co-building something end-to-end (not splitting modules) surfaces traumas and triggers — her co-author's estimate panic from years of "are you done yet?", her own "unhappy resting face" — and that true connection is what makes people invested in each other's growth, which produces safety, which produces best work.

"I trust them" is a lie until behavior changes. The Queen of Hearts card trick proves a leader with a power dynamic can always steer you to the answer in their head. "Manipulation is not leadership and it's not trust." The alternative is intentional teaching situations: hand off your expertise, or "you will stay the expert if you continue to act like you're the only expert" — and stay the bottleneck.

Courage can't be commanded, but silence can be priced. "Have courage" works exactly as well as "calm down" (never). Her lever, as someone "in a position of privilege and power": "What is my silence saying when I choose not to speak up?" Once she saw what silence transmitted, speaking up stopped being about bravery — "I don't care anymore" about being called the fun police.

Learning is the only way through complexity — and everyone lies about loving it. Learning is daily work, not a quarterly side project. Five inhibitors, all illustrated by her 18-month handstand journey: baggage (goals set by perceived success — the mentee who became an executive and called to say "I hate my life"), change (non-linear; steps backward are normal; don't overwhelm — "conference Tricia" going from 18-month releases to "feasible daily deploy" terrified her department, so she pulled back to CI and automation), unknown (unknowns "smack you in the face" — she learned to hold a handstand but never learned to fall), time ("six weeks to a handstand. They lie."), and failure (required, and gaming metrics to fake the check mark isn't learning).

Unexpected achievements go unrecognized because only the goal counts as the check mark. "Nobody's gonna talk about whether or not your project was on time... People are gonna talk about the impact you're making on other people." Her proof: thanking Kent Beck in person 25+ years after his book — "not because of XP, but because of the journey that it took me on."

The meta-lesson: there is no done. She hit the 10-second handstand exactly once, at 18 months to the day, and never again. That's fine, because "it was never about the check mark." And the closing loop: "How you build resilience is by being resilient."

The Toolkit: Things You Can Actually Use

The Four Pieces of Resilience (compassion, confidence, courage, complexity) — The framework from Lead Without Blame (Diana Larsen & Broderick). Use as a diagnostic lens on yourself and your team; she's explicit it's "not the complete puzzle."

The relay race test — A one-question audit for fake teams: could someone get a bonus for their leg even if the race was lost? If yes, you have a group (cooperation), not a team (collaboration) — and collaboration is where co-intelligence lives.

The hub audit — Ask: "What is it that I know that's making everybody dependent on me for that question? What makes me the single point of failure, the bottleneck?" Then deliberately share it. Bonus check: if your job description says "drive," it's fighting your own self-organization efforts.

Intentional collaboration to build compassion — Co-build something end-to-end with a colleague instead of splitting modules. The shared work surfaces triggers and traumas, builds true connection, and gets people invested in each other's learning and well-being.

Intentional teaching situations — When you know the content, don't teach it all yourself and don't steer people to your answer. Ask "who wants to take on building this?" Her result: the course was "so much better than what I would have built," and confidence was built in both directions.

The Queen of Hearts self-check — If, in a conversation, you could have arrived at your answer no matter what the other person said, you're manipulating, not leading. The audience-side tell that it's happening to you: catching yourself thinking "just tell me what you want me to say."

The silence check — Before staying quiet as a leader, ask what your silence is transmitting to the people affected. Use that cost, not bravado, as the fuel to speak up.

Small exploratory steps — In complexity, take steps whose only purpose is to unlock unknowns, not to reach a decision. Her example: before deciding whether to write a book, she met one author, waited months, met another — steps "to just get curious." Extends "last responsible moment" from decisions to exploration.

Change pacing — Don't overwhelm people with change. When "feasible daily deploy" terrified her department (coming from 18-month releases), she pulled back to "some CI first... some automation." Big noble goals die when the intermediate steps are invisible.

Goal baggage interrogation — Ask why a goal is the goal before pursuing it. Her handstand goal was "10 seconds or nothing" because "anything else seemed not cool" — while she couldn't even kick up to a wall. Her mentee's "I have to be an executive" produced a phone call: "I hate my life."

Recognize unexpected achievements — Deliberately celebrate non-goal wins on long journeys. Counting only the final check mark means missing exactly what others remember you for: support, inspiration, impact.

Build a learning community — Identify who you'd text "today was horrible" and who sits in the front row for you. "Community is about when it's tough. Who are you reaching out to?" Not karaoke, not trust falls.

Record your practice + daily consistency — She records every day of her 30-minute handstand practice and does it daily. Recording captures the wins nobody else witnesses (her daughter missed the one 10-second hold of her life); daily practice is what made 15-second holds routine.

"You won't if you stop" — The mantra for the dip moments when learning feels pointless and late.

Named concepts to steal: Adam Grant's Mount Stupid (the cocky phase after initial competence — she was there when she discovered she didn't know how to fall), Dunning-Kruger (the trap of thinking you know all the unknowns), and "conference Tricia" (the post-conference persona who tries to do all the things at once — a useful name for your own change-overload pattern).

What She Left on the Table

The de-hubbing mechanics are offstage. She says leaders were never taught facilitation or mentoring and that they panic when they stop being the hub — but never says what the leader's job becomes, or offers a single practice for the transition. The hardest leadership move in the talk gets no how.

The COVID confession is unresolved. "There's actually something that I should have been doing more as a leader" — teaching teams to be resilient. She never names what she did differently afterward, or whether the teams that "reverted really quickly back to group" ever recovered. The talk's most concrete failure story has no ending.

Courage advice assumes power she admits others lack. The silence reframe is explicitly "as a leader, as somebody in a position of privilege and power." She can shrug off being "the fun police"; someone without her tenure, platform, or book contract faces real retaliation. What courage looks like when silence is survival is never addressed.

Systemic causes, individual cures. Layoffs, chaos, orgs "optimized for group behavior," bonus structures that reward relay-race behavior — she names all of these, then prescribes only individual mindset shifts. She describes exactly how incentives reinforce fake teams and never once says how to change incentive design.

No line between resilience and burnout. "Resilience is being committed, digging in when it gets hard" and "you won't if you stop" — with zero boundary for when persistence becomes sunk cost, endurance of abuse, or plain exhaustion. The handstand is a hobby with a soft landing; the same advice applied to a toxic job is harmful. Burnout is never mentioned.

The recipe presumes privilege. Eighteen months of daily 30-minute practice, conference communities, friends who'll do handstands with you at an Agile conference. What resilience looks like without time, safety, or an existing network is unaddressed — and "find a community" is prescribed without any guidance for building one if you're isolated, remote, or the only one struggling.

The "token female" moment coaches no one. She got mad, told her colleague "I need a minute," and walked away — then the story jumps to the supportive Agile 2025 announcement. Did she ever go back to the colleague? What should he have said? What should you say when you hear that question about a colleague? The most charged moment in the talk raises the topic and drops it.

Anecdote only. The four pieces are hindsight patterns from one career and one handstand. No research, no data, no counterexamples — notable given she name-checks Dunning-Kruger and Mount Stupid. She also never explains how the four pieces interact: is there a sequence? Dependencies? Can you skip one? And "compassion for yourself" is in her own definition but virtually absent from the practices.

Trust repair after manipulation is a dead end. She says manipulation means "you're not actually building two-way trust" — but never says what to do once you've been caught doing it, on a stage or in a retro.

No ammunition for the learning fight. She skewers "once a quarter, if we have time" learning but gives no argument a worker or manager could use to defend daily learning against delivery pressure — the same pressure (layoffs, uncertainty) she says makes our self-deceptions "even bigger." Relatedly: "learning means failure" and "when we game metrics... we're not learning" — with no treatment of the organizations that punish failure, which is why people game metrics.

And the transcript cuts before the verdict: after disclaimers ("do not try one right after this session," "don't approach me with your technique unless you are actually a gymnast or in the circus," "this is the dumbest thing I have chosen to do to myself on a stage"), the live handstand attempt happens — and we never learn whether she held it.
