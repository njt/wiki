                                           ABSTRACT

Whether imposing higher taxes on business owners adversely affects business activity by
constraining investment and inducing capitalist flight is a central question in the ongoing debate on
how to tax the ultra wealthy. To shed new light, I exploit a series of related Norwegian reforms
during 2021–2024 that increased dividend tax rates, removed migration-related capital gains tax
loopholes, and nearly doubled the effective marginal tax rate on business wealth. These reforms
spurred international debate and, allegedly, an “exodus” of Norwegian billionaires (Financial
Times, 2023). While I document clear effects on migration, these responses are concentrated
among the top 0.1% of the wealth distribution and mostly confined to 2022 and 2023. My results
indicate that at most 100 individuals left due to the reforms. I find no evidence of significant
outmigration or reduced inflows of startup founders or inventors. I estimate that absent any
outmigration, wealth tax revenues would have increased by 75%from 2021 to 2024. Reform-driven
outmigration lowers the revenue gain to 71.5%. My findings further show that outmigrating
owners’ firms remain economically active and do not reduce investment. In a broader set of
analyses, I examine whether business outcomes are affected, regardless of whether owners
outmigrate, since firms may reduce investment both due to liquidity constraints or distortions to
owners’ savings decisions. I compare firms owned by Norwegian individuals subject to the wealth
tax with firms owned by foreigners and other tax-exempt entities. By merging historical ownership
data from Orbis with the Norwegian shareholder register, I perform these analyses on both
Norwegian and foreign-domiciled firms. Across settings, I find no evidence that Norwegian-
owned firms pay out more dividends or invest less after the reforms, nor is there any evidence that
the reform induced more bankruptcies. These findings indicate that the short-run economic costs of
wealth taxation, in terms of migration and firm investment, are more limited than the public
debate implies.

1        Introduction
     The past decade has seen renewed interest in wealth taxation as a tool to raise tax revenues
and address economic inequality. The motivation for more aggressive taxation of wealthy in-
dividuals is partly driven by the modest personal tax burdens faced by billionaires. A handful
of recent studies show that once undistributed profits are accounted for, there is significant
tax regressivity at the very top of the income distribution (Bruil, Van Essen, Leenders, Lejour,
Möhlmann, and Rabaté 2022; Aaberge, Francesconi, Modalsli, and Vestad 2024; Balkir, Saez,
Yagan, and Zucman 2025; Bach, Bozio, Guillouzouic, and Malgouyres 2025; Ring, Seim, and
Zucman 2026). The wealthiest individuals tend to be business owners that retain most of their
profits inside closely-held corporations, rarely realize capital gains (Msall and Næss, 2026), and
thereby avoid personal income taxation. A wealth tax (or similarly, a tax on unrealized capital
gains) could remedy this regressivity in that it does not condition on corporate distributions.
However, the fact that wealthy individuals retain earnings in their firms also fuels the vocal
opposition to wealth taxation. Imposing large personal tax bills on billionaire entrepreneurs
may force them to deplete their firms of capital, potentially reducing investment and thus eco-
nomic growth. This concern pervades policy debates despite virtually no supporting empirical
evidence.
     There are two main drivers for why there is little empirical evidence to guide policy makers.
First, most recent experiences with a wealth tax involve abolishing it. Hence, there are few
empirical laboratories in which we can study how introducing (more) wealth taxation affects
economic activity. Introducing a wealth tax may reduce economic activity through irreversible
firm exits and reductions in investment that cannot be fully understood by studying the removal
of wealth taxes. Second, identifying the causal effects of wealth taxes on wealthy entrepreneurs
is particularly challenging. While it is possible to obtain quasi-experimental variation in wealth
taxation due to quirks in the tax code, these settings rarely allow one to study the wealthiest
households. For these taxpayers, whether couples face a double exemption threshold (Jakobsen
et al. 2020) or their housing stock is assessed more heavily (Ring 2025, Ring and Thoresen
2025) is immaterial in affecting their overall tax burden.
     I tackle these empirical challenges in two ways. First, my empirical laboratory consists of
a recent reform in Norway that substantially increased the tax burden faced by the wealthiest
households. Second, I identify the effects on firms by exploiting the fact that the wealth
tax only applies to Norwegian tax residents. Hence, firms owned by non-resident foreigners
should not be affected—offering a natural control group for the firms of wealthy Norwegians.1
This allows me to control for a wide range of firm characteristics, such as firm size, without

      The reforms implemented during 2021–2024 were controversial. Upon announcing higher
wealth and dividend tax rates in 2021, a political and media frenzy quickly ensued. The public
debate persisted well into 2025, by which time scores of billionaires had, not so quietly, migrated
to Switzerland (Financial Times 2023, Bloomberg 2024, Guardian 2025). Migrants purportedly
left due to several changes to the wealth tax code that particularly affected entrepreneurs. The
top statutory tax rate increased from 0.85% to 1.1% while preferential treatment of stocks in
private and public companies was substantially reduced. Directly before the reform, each $1 of
book equity in private firms entered taxable wealth at a discounted value of $0.55. After the
reform, $1 of book equity increased taxable wealth by $0.80. Hence, the reform almost doubled
the top marginal tax rate on business wealth from 0.47% to 0.88%. I show that this had a
material impact on the annual wealth tax paid by Norwegian business owners. At the same
time, the dividend tax rate increased from 32% to almost 38% by 2023, which exacerbated the
effect of the reform on business owners who rely on dividend distributions to pay their wealth
tax.
     The government responded to the widely publicized outmigration of wealthy business own-
ers by implementing new policies to ensure that outmigrants eventually paid income taxes on
accumulated retained earnings. In November of 2022, the government removed a loophole that
forgave tax liabilities on unrealized gains after a taxpayer had spent at least five years as a
foreign tax resident. Closing this loophole, but allowing taxes on unrealized capital gains to be
permanently deferred brought the rules closer to the current scheme for outmigrants in Den-
mark. This policy change was followed by stricter rules in 2024, when it was announced that
taxes on unrealized capital gains must be paid within 12 years, bringing the exit-tax scheme
closer to current rules in, e.g., Germany.
      This paper pursues two lines of analysis. I study whether the 2021–2024 reforms led to
capitalist flight and the extent to which firms owned by affected Norwegian business owners
increased their dividends and reduced their investment rates. I use a broad mix of publicly
available and restricted-access data sources. These include the shareholder register that lists
all shareholders and their ownership shares for all Norwegian corporations during 2003–2025.
These data also contain the address (including country of residence) of the shareholders. I link
these with extensive public tax lists containing taxable wealth, income, and total taxes paid
for wealthy Norwegians, and with journalistic estimates of actual wealth for the wealthiest 400
Norwegians. I obtain firm financial data from BvD Orbis Historical, which provides a long panel
of financial statements for Norwegian firms. Orbis Historical also contains financial statements

for a large sample of other European firms, as well as ownership data. These ownership data

     The most salient and widely reported effect of the reforms was the departure of wealthy
individuals to Switzerland. Consistent with this, Blandhol (2025) documents significant excess
outmigration at the very top of the Norwegian wealth distribution in 2022. My analyses extend
the time horizon to evaluate whether outmigration was a transitory response to pre-empt the
closure of tax loopholes or persistent responses to wealth taxation. I also examine migration
responses and their dynamics in several policy-relevant subpopulations. I first use adminis-
trative data to show that there was significant excess outmigration following the reform for
individuals in the top 0.1% of the taxable wealth distribution. This excess outmigration lasted
to the fall of 2023, after which migration rates returned to pre-reform levels. My estimates
suggest that about 1.78% of the top 0.1%, or about 0.24% of the top 1% migrated due to the
reform. This amounts to 74–98 individuals. The slow-down of outmigration coincides with
the closure of tax-related migration loopholes, which suggests that these loopholes were an
important driver of the response. However, the closing of the five-year loophole may also have
accelerated wealth-tax-motivated migration.
     Since these outmigrating individuals were particularly wealthy, the revenue effects are
clearly discernible. Contrasting observed aggregate tax revenues with those that would have
prevailed absent any outmigration, I estimate a total wealth-tax revenue loss of about 0.65
BNOK, or about 2% of counterfactual revenues. Nevertheless, these avoidance responses do
not come close to countering the strong mechanical effects of the reform, causing an increase
in wealth tax revenues of about 71.5% net of reform-driven outmigration. I further find no
evidence that firms of owners that outmigrated between May 2022 and May 2024 are less likely
to remain active or invest less by the end of 2025.
     To understand the anatomy of the response, I study outmigration responses in several
subpopulations. I consider high-profile billionaires featured in the top-400 wealth ranking by
the magazine Kapital. I construct a novel dataset that provides a high-frequency outmigration
panel by merging the wealth rankings with address information from the shareholder register
and newspaper mentions of migration events (e.g., “tax refugee” status). These data indicate
substantial reform-induced outmigration among Norway’s wealthiest individuals. Interestingly,
most of this migration occurred in the fall of 2022—directly before the five-year loophole was
closed. In this sample of high-profile billionaires, there is no additional outmigration during
2024 or 2025, when the exit-tax loopholes had mostly been closed. I also consider individual
shareholders in Norwegian firms. I find excess outmigration of 2.19% among the top-1% of
business owners, but no response among the remaining 99%.

     The fact that many wealthy individuals chose to leave the country after the reform begs
the question of whether this effect spread to startup entrepreneurs and innovators who may
rely on external financing. To address this, I use data on the near universe of LinkedIn profiles
from RevelioLabs to study migration rates of startup founders. I then contrast the migration
rates of Norwegian entrepreneurs with other Nordic entrepreneurs. The profile classification
system allows me to isolate “startup” and “tech founders” from the broader pool of owner-
managers. And unlike standard administrative data that are typically limited to one country,
the LinkedIn data allow me to study the effect on inflows of entrepreneurs from other countries.
These analyses do not indicate any excess outflow or reduced inflow of entrepreneurs in Norway.
I further link the LinkedIn data with patent records to study the effect on inventor mobility.
Again, there is no evidence that more inventors left or fewer inventors arrived in Norway relative
to other Nordic countries. This begs the question of whether startup investors, as a distinct
group, actually fled Norway following the reforms. To examine this, I consider the subsample
of business owners who had previously invested in innovative, grant-receiving startups. In this
subsample, there is no excess outmigration. On the contrary, it appears that outmigration rates
dropped below pre-period trends as migration-related tax loopholes were closed.
     My next analyses concern whether the tax reforms affected firms’ financing and investment
behavior, regardless of whether their owners outmigrated. I first demonstrate that whether
higher wealth taxes reduce corporate investment is theoretically ambiguous.2 This result arises
naturally in an entrepreneurial life-cycle model with financial frictions. When the elasticity of
intertemporal substitution (EIS) is low enough, the owner may wish to save more to offset the
adverse effects of wealth taxation on future consumption. In this case, wealth taxes may lower
the user cost of capital. In a simple calibrated model, the 2021–2024 reforms only decrease
investment if the EIS exceeds about 0.66, a value that lies between existing estimates from
Norway (Ring 2025; Holm, Jamilov, Jasinski, and Nenov 2026). The implied change in the
investment rate ranges from −2.8 to +3.5 percentage points for values of the EIS between 0.2
and 6.
    My empirical approach compares firms owned by Norwegian individuals subject to the
wealth tax with firms owned by exempt owners such as foreign corporations or individuals.
To account for the fact that foreign-owned firms tend to be larger and operate in specific
industries, I employ a rich set of granular industry and size fixed effects. To measure a firm’s
exposure to the reform, I allocate an owner’s wealth tax liability to her firm in proportion to
ownership-weighted revenues. I then consider the ratio of allocated wealth tax to revenues,
and find that this increased by about 1.6 percentage points from 2020 to 2024. To investigate
whether increased wealth taxation required business owners to deplete their firms of capital via

     A final set of analyses examine whether the reforms disrupted entrepreneurial finance,
which depends on entrepreneurs’ personal wealth (Chodorow-Reich, Nenov, Santos, and Sim-
sek 2024; Ring 2023). I first examine whether the reform induced excess outmigration among
startup investors, defined as individuals with minority stakes in young firms that have received
innovation grants. This reveals no indication of excess outmigration. Rather, there appears
to be less outmigration once migration-related tax loopholes were removed. I also indirectly
examine whether wealthy Norwegians reduced their startup investments after the reform. I
consider both ownership in innovation grant recipients and young firms receiving R&D credits.
For innovation grant recipients, there is no indication of reduced investment from Norwegian in-
vestors. For innovation-tax-credit recipients, the data indicate reduced investment, particularly
when weighting by invested capital.
     Literature. A growing body of research examines the behavioral responses to wealth
taxation (Seim 2017; Zoutman 2018; Bach, Bozio, Guillouzouic, and Malgouyres 2020; Jakob-
sen, Jakobsen, Kleven, and Zucman 2020; Berg and Hebous 2021; Londoño-Vélez and Ávila-
Mahecha 2021; Brülhart, Gruber, Krapf, and Schmidheiny 2022; Alstadsæter, Bjørneby, Kopczuk,
Markussen, and Røed 2022; Fagereng, Guiso, and Ring 2023; Jakurti and Süssmuth 2023;
Le Guern Herry 2024; Ring 2025; Mas-Montserrat, Durán-Cabré, and Esteller-Moré 2025;
Londoño-Vélez and Avila-Mahecha 2025; Ring and Thoresen 2025), where the emerging con-
sensus is that wealth taxation has minor real effects in terms of individuals’ saving behavior (as
emphasized by Brülhart et al. 2022 and Ring 2025). However, the tax base may still be elastic
due to evasion, avoidance, and migration.

     A much smaller body of research examines mobility responses. Brülhart, Gruber, Krapf,
and Schmidheiny (2022) find significant elasticities of taxable wealth with respect to marginal
tax rates in Switzerland, of which one quarter are driven by within-country but across-tax-
regime migration responses. Iacono and Smedsvik (2024) similarly finds that a Norwegian
municipality that reduced its local tax rate saw a large increase in the municipal-level amount of
taxable wealth. Agrawal, Foremny, and Martı́nez-Toledano (2025) further document significant
within-country mobility responses in Spain. After the 2011 reintroduction of the wealth tax,
which Madrid chose not to levy, the wealth-tax-paying population (top 0.5%) in the other
regions fell by 1.7% within six years. Considering outmigration, Jakobsen, Kleven, Kolsrud,
Landais, and Muñoz (2024) find that the 2007 abolition of the Swedish wealth tax, which
averaged about 0.5% of net wealth for the top 2%, reduced their annual outmigration rate by
0.05 percentage points. For Norway, Grindaker and Vestad (2025) estimate that the emigration
probability of the top 1% increased by 0.27 percentage points per percentage-point increase in
the wealth tax rate, a response they note partly reflects the November 2022 tightening of the
exit tax.
     An even smaller body of research considers the real effects of wealth taxation on the firms
owned and financed by wealth tax payers. The existing evidence is inconclusive and identified
from gradual changes to the wealth tax rules. Bjørneby, Markussen, and Røed (2023) find that
higher wealth tax exposure increases employment while Berzins, Bøhren, and Stacescu (2020)
find the opposite.3
     The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 describes the 2021–2024 reforms and the data.
Section 3 studies outmigration and tax revenue effects. Section 4 presents a stylized user-
cost model and then estimates the effects of the reforms on firms’ payouts, investment, and
bankruptcy. Section 5 examines potential disruptions to entrepreneurial finance. Section 6
concludes.

base is taxable net wealth, consisting of financial and real assets net of debt. As of 2021,

     In March 2024, the government indicated that they would implement a payment deadline

the owner would not have to pay dividend taxes on dividends received while abroad.

2.2    Data
Orbis Historical. The Orbis historical data contains all financial statements for firms present
in the BvD (now Moody’s) Orbis dataset. The coverage for Norwegian firms is nearly complete
due to Orbis purchasing these data from Experian who obtain them from the Norwegian business
registration office.

Shareholder Register. The shareholder register for 2003–2025 is obtained from the tax
authorities. These data contain the most recent address of shareholders as of delivery. Hence, I
use several different vintages of the shareholder register (2014 through 2025) to obtain a panel
of shareholder locations. The shareholder register contains the name of the shareholder and
their birth year (organization number if a domestic LLC).

Administrative microdata. For some analyses, I use administrative micro data accessed
via the microdata.no platform. This platform allows researchers to access a broad set of
administrative registers via a secure interface through which one can perform statistical analyses
but not extract or view the microdata themselves. I use this service to analyze migration rates
across the taxable wealth distribution during 1994–2025. These data cannot be and are not
linked with the other data sources. See Appendix B.3 for details on the use of this data.

Newspaper Tax Lists. Individuals’ taxable income, taxable wealth, and total taxes paid
are considered public information in Norway. Each year, since 2013, the tax authorities send
complete “tax lists” to media organizations who may choose to disclose information on persons
of interest. The tax authorities explicitly allow media organizations to publish “top-100” lists:
top 100 individuals per municipality in terms of taxable personal income, taxable wealth, and
taxes paid. In addition, since 2019, it is common to publish top-15 zip-code lists. I obtain all
of these lists for the years 2012–2024 (zip-code lists available from 2019). These lists contain
full names, birth year, and address (zip code, city) information.

Magazine wealth rankings. Another benefit of using publicly available data sources is

startups. I use receipt of these grants as a way to isolate startups from other firms that wealthy

     While the existing wealth tax literature considers elasticities with respect to wealth tax

4.1    The User Cost of Capital
     This section provides a formal mapping between the parameters that determine personal
taxes (e.g., dividend tax rate, wealth tax schedule) and the user cost of capital from the per-
spective of the firm. The goal is not a comprehensive treatment of how personal taxes affect
corporate investment, but rather a stylized mapping that highlights the theoretically ambiguous
effect that personal taxation has on firm investment. The analysis builds on the user-cost frame-
work of Jorgenson (1963) and Hall and Jorgenson (1967) and derives the required return from
the owner’s tax treatment and intertemporal problem. Since the owner cannot borrow against
future business income, investment and consumption decisions are not separable (Hirshleifer,
1958). Hence, the EIS matters for the user cost.
     Consider an established owner with $1 of retained earnings. At date 0, the owner chooses
k ∈ (0, 1) to retain in the firm and distributes the remaining 1 − k as dividends. This is the
retained-earnings margin. The firm earns annual operating income F (k) before depreciation
and corporate income tax for T years and is liquidated at the end of year T . Capital depreciates
at rate δ, and corporate income is taxed at rate τ CIT . The firm replaces depreciation but cannot
otherwise adjust its capital stock or hold financial assets. It distributes its after-corporate-tax
profit each year. The owner pays dividend tax τ Div on these distributions and the liquidation
proceeds. The owner also pays annual wealth tax θS τ w k on the shares, where θS is their
marginal wealth-tax assessment fraction.
     The owner may save personally but cannot borrow. Let st+1 ≥ 0 denote the amount saved
at date t. Personal saving earns rsave after personal income tax and has wealth-tax assessment
fraction θP . The owner consumes the liquidation proceeds over J retirement years, beginning
at date T . Let ct denote consumption at date t, let β ∈ (0, 1) denote the discount factor, and let
u be increasing, differentiable, and strictly concave. I compare two tax systems in which all tax
parameters remain constant over the owner’s remaining life and solve the owner’s consumption
and saving choices under each.

5     Disruptions to entrepreneurial finance
Whether startup investors outmigrated at higher rates after the reforms. A promi-
nent literature in financial economics emphasizes the importance of well-functioning local cap-
ital markets in facilitating entrepreneurship (Guiso, Sapienza, and Zingales 2004; Kerr and
Nanda 2009; Samila and Sorenson 2011). Several papers emphasize how physical distance be-
tween financiers and firms may exacerbate financial frictions (Lerner 1995; Petersen and Rajan
2002; Bernstein, Giroud, and Townsend 2016). Hence, I examine whether there were excess
outmigration rates of startup investors after the 2021–2024 reforms.
     Figure 17 plots cumulative outmigration rates of the subset of business owners who were
investors in startups as of 2018. I define startups as firms awarded grants by Innovation Norway.
Interestingly, there is no evidence of excess outmigration among these investors. If anything,
these investors appear to outmigrate at lower than counterfactual rates following the closure of
the five-year loophole.
     These results do not imply that no startup financiers left the country due to the reform.
Appendix Figure OA.13 focuses on individuals in the top 1% of business owners. The findings
show (imprecisely) that those who outmigrated between the spring of 2022 and the spring of
2024 were equally likely to be startup investors (at rates of about 15–20%) as nonmigrants. Nev-
ertheless, on average among startup investors, there was no tendency of increased outmigration

6                                                 Discussion
      This paper studies the controversial 2021–2024 Norwegian tax reforms that increased the
tax burden of business owners. While I document clear effects on outmigration, these effects
are confined to about 100 wealthy individuals who outmigrated during 2022–2023. There is
little evidence of outmigration or reduced inmigration of other entrepreneurs or inventors, and
there is little evidence to suggest that the wealth tax reduced corporate investment in the short
run.

A.5     Dividend taxes and rate-of-return deduction
     In the current tax system (“aksjonærmodellen”), dividends paid from a given firm to a
given individual are tax-exempt up to a threshold approximately equal to the tax-authority-
determined risk-free rate times the shares’ adjusted cost basis (“skjermingsfradrag”). The cost
basis is adjusted in that it increases with previous unused exemptions. This implies that even
though some portion of dividends meant to finance wealth tax payments may not trigger an
immediate dividend tax liability, it will nevertheless have an equivalent tax cost by reducing
the adjusted cost basis.
     Consider 2024. The dividend tax rate is 37.84%, the top marginal tax rate on book value
business wealth is 0.88%, and the relevant risk-free rate is 3.9%. Further consider a simple case
in which the tax value of a firm equals the book value of equity. If the (adjusted) cost basis
equals the value of equity which equals $100. In that case, the wealth tax payment is $0.88
but $3.9 of dividends may be paid out without incurring additional immediate dividend tax.
However, in 2022, the adjusted cost basis equaled only 18% of the pre-stock-discount assessed
value of Norwegians’ personally-held unlisted stocks (Grindaker and Vestad, 2025, Figure 4.3).
Applying this 18% to 2024, suggests an effective exemption rate on tax-value equity of 0.7%
(18% × 3.9%). Thus dividend payments to finance the wealth tax (0.88%) can only be partially
exempt from dividend taxation, and only to the extent that the owner would not already make
use of the exemption for dividends for other purposes, e.g., financing consumption.
