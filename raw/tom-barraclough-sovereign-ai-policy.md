# Tom Barraclough: Sovereign AI policy and digital regulatory architecture

**Subtitle:** AI sovereignty unpacked: what small nations need to survive the intelligence explosion

**Host:** Ben Reid
**Guest:** Tom Barraclough
**Published:** June 23, 2026
**Publication:** Memia (Substack newsletter/podcast)

---

## Main Thesis

Small nations like New Zealand cannot achieve AI sovereignty through top-down government construction of foundation models. Instead, sovereignty must be understood as a multidimensional, multi-stakeholder concept spanning compute, data, training, literacy, and individual agency—enabled by open digital regulatory infrastructure that turns law from static PDFs into structured, versioned, referenceable datasets.

---

## Key Discussion Points

### 1. Law as culture, not neutral code

Barraclough argues law functions as an "interpretive culture" rather than a deterministic runtime. Courts apply judgment shaped by shared interpretive context, not mechanical rule-following. This has profound implications for any attempt to formalize law as computable rules.

### 2. The PDF problem

Organizations run on layered, non-networking PDFs. Barraclough cites Jack Clark's term "paper plumbing"—a broken document architecture that fails at the moment it's most needed. This is the infrastructure problem that makes law opaque to machines.

### 3. Syncopate and DocRef

Barraclough's startup turns law and regulation into structured, granularly referenceable datasets, enabling versioning, annotation, cross-agency interoperability, and AI accessibility. This is digital regulatory infrastructure: law as API.

### 4. Rules-based order under strain

Bringing more people into political discourse hasn't built shared interpretive consensus—it's exposed that the "institutional software" is past its use-by date. Barraclough cites AOC's framing: "we didn't have a rules-based order. We had a pre-rules based order."

### 5. Radical digital regulatory infrastructure

A Cambridge journal paper in progress proposes publishing law as open, versioned, linkable datasets—roots that "filter up through all of these different digital systems." This is the infrastructure play beneath the sovereignty conversation.

### 6. AI sovereignty unpacked

Barraclough was initially skeptical of "sovereign AI" (government builds a foundation model). He developed a multidimensional framework rejecting the notion of a single government-controlled model in favor of distributed sovereignty across compute, data, training, content moderation, literacy, and individual agency.

### 7. The "one-in-a-million shot" problem

Even China can't nail every layer of the sovereign AI stack simultaneously. Barraclough argues getting all layers right requires an improbable perfect alignment of political, economic, legal, and technical factors.

### 8. Te Hiku Media as positive model

Community-owned, values-driven AI development in Te Reo Māori, featured as the closing epilogue to Karen Hao's *Empire of AI*, demonstrates what distributed, multi-stakeholder sovereignty looks like in practice.

### 9. Confidential computing and personal sovereignty

Apple's Private Cloud Compute and the startup Tinfoil offer hardware-verifiable privacy through cryptographic attestation rather than contractual assurances.

### 10. AI-accelerated lawmaking

Barraclough demonstrated a live DocRef/MCP/Claude workflow that drafted a new API standard from existing regulatory corpus in an afternoon—work that traditionally takes nine months. He notes the tension between speed and the public good of regulatory stability.
