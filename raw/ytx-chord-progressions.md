Harmonic thinking is a radial model, not a linear list. Keithson visualizes a key centre with multiple independent "strands" of harmonic options radiating from it. When improvising or composing, he draws from these strands simultaneously, not in a fixed order.

The journey from diatonic to sophisticated harmony is about accumulating functional "strands." He started knowing only the seven diatonic chords. Over time, he added secondary dominants, modal interchange, two-fives, tritone substitutions, diminished chords, and five-chord alternatives as separate, learnable systems that all attach to the same key centre.

Non-diatonic chords are justified by their function, not their origin. Every strand is framed around where chords lead: secondary dominants are "the V chord of each of the chords in our key"; diminished chords "function in very much the same way" as dominants; tritone subs work because they "share the same third and seventh."

Thinking in scale degrees and chord types makes everything transposable. For modal interchange, he doesn't memorize specific chords per key. He remembers "chord types over scale degrees" (e.g., "on my flat 2, I've got a major 7"), which gives him "the same options" in any key.

Complex harmony is a tool, not a virtue. He explicitly warns that "a simple or boring chord progression doesn't invalidate a song." Different musical goals (lyrical storytelling, dance hypnotism) may be hindered by complex harmony. "Especially you jazz snobs out there, you know who you are."

Tools, practices, and methodologies

The Key Centre + Strands mental model — A visual framework where a tonic sits at the centre and independent harmonic "strands" (diatonic chords, secondary dominants, modal interchange, etc.) radiate from it. Use it to organize harmonic options and draw from multiple systems simultaneously while staying anchored to one key.

Secondary Dominants — The V chord of any diatonic chord except the tonic. Use them by inserting a dominant seventh chord a fifth above your target chord to create a strong pull toward it. Keithson thinks of these as "five of two," "five of three," etc.

Modal Interchange (Borrowed Chords) — Borrowing chords from any parallel mode or scale that shares the same tonic, then returning to the original key. Keithson limits himself to borrowing from the three parallel minor modes: Dorian, Phrygian, and Aeolian. He memorizes these as chord types over scale degrees (e.g., flat-2 = major 7, flat-3 = major 7, flat-6 = major 7, flat-7 = dominant 7).

Two-Fives (II-Vs) — Preceding any dominant chord with its corresponding II chord. For major destinations, use a minor 7 II chord. For minor destinations, use a half-diminished II chord and an altered dominant V chord. Also includes "backdoor two-fives" and unnamed alternatives that resolve to the IV chord.

Tritone Substitutions — Replacing any dominant seventh chord with a dominant seventh a tritone away, because they share the same third and seventh (inverted). Two applications: (1) swap the diatonic V7 for its tritone sub; (2) swap any secondary dominant for its tritone sub. A practical shortcut: "whatever chord I'm heading to, I can put in a dominant chord a half step above."

Diminished Seventh Chords as Dominant Substitutes — Using fully diminished seventh chords to lead to target chords, exploiting their close relationship to dominant chords (lower any note of a dim7 by a semitone and you get a dominant 7). Practical shortcut: play a diminished chord a half-step below any target chord.

Five Chord Alternatives — A collection of non-diatonic chords that can replace the V7 and still resolve to the tonic, all derived from manipulating a diminished chord built on the raised root of the V chord. Includes: four diminished chords, the backdoor dominant (♭VII7), the tritone sub (♭II7), and several half-diminished/minor 6 options. He rejects any alternative that loses the critical F→E (scale degree 4→3) movement.

Unanswered questions and omissions

How do you choose which strand to use in a given moment? The talk presents a menu of options but offers no decision-making framework. When improvising, how does he decide between a secondary dominant, a tritone sub, or a diminished passing chord to reach the same target?

Voice leading is almost entirely absent. The harmonic options are presented as block chord choices. There's no discussion of how upper voices move, how to voice these chords on piano, or how smooth voice leading might constrain or guide which substitutions work best in practice.

Melody and harmony interaction is never addressed. How do these harmonic substitutions support or clash with a melody? If you're composing a song rather than noodling chord progressions, the melody would heavily constrain which borrowed chords are viable.

The modal interchange section is oddly restricted. He says he "only really borrow[s] chords from my three minor modes" (Dorian, Phrygian, Aeolian), explicitly excluding Lydian, Mixolydian, harmonic minor, and melodic minor. No justification is given for why these are excluded or what "desired effect" the others lack.

Rhythm and harmonic rhythm are ignored. Chord progressions don't exist in rhythmic isolation. How long you sit on a borrowed chord, where it falls in the bar, and how it interacts with meter dramatically affects whether it sounds "interesting" or chaotic.

The "five chord alternatives" derivation is dense and under-explained. The method of raising and lowering individual notes of a diminished chord to generate alternative dominants is presented rapidly. The logic of why some alterations work and others fail (the F→E pull) is mentioned but could easily lose someone without a strong theory background.

No discussion of when not to use these techniques. Beyond the closing caveat about simple progressions being valid, there's no practical guidance on over-harmonization, genre appropriateness, or how to develop taste in applying these tools sparingly.
