Adam Conover argues that the death of the sitcom is not a cultural phenomenon — it's not "cancel culture" or shifting audience taste — but a deliberate, structural consequence of the streaming business model: by cutting season orders to 8–10 episodes, separating writers from production, and outsourcing shoots overseas, Hollywood dismantled the decades-old "comedy factory" that made shows like Cheers and Parks and Rec funny, and it did so to cut costs even though sitcoms still outperform their catalog share in subscriber retention. This matters because, in Conover's view, the sitcom is a uniquely valuable format — it models how very different people come to love each other — and its absence is a self-inflicted industry wound, not an act of God.

Key Points

The drought is real and measurable. Sitcoms are only 1.7% of new TV shows this decade, yet The Big Bang Theory (ended 2019) is still top-10 streamed in May 2026, and sitcoms make up 13% of Hulu's subscriber retention despite being 7% of its catalog. Demand exists; supply doesn't.

"Comedies" today are dramas in disguise.The Bear and Hacks are written as serialized, high-stakes dramas with arcs and cliffhangers. In a true sitcom, "the comedy is what drives the action" — same characters, new situation each episode, no growth, no arc, just "goofy fun."

Change #1: Short season orders kill time, and time is the essential ingredient. Eight episodes force a season-long dramatic arc because there's no room to just hang with characters. First seasons of sitcoms are awkward because the writers don't know the characters yet — Ron Swanson only becomes "your jerk" by season four. Bingeing compresses what used to be a years-long weekly friendship into a weekend.

Change #2: Separating writing from production destroyed the apprenticeship. The Cheers creators (Les Charles, Glen Charles, James Burrows) made thousands of episodes across Taxi, MTM, MASH, Friends, Frasier — expertise that's now impossible to accumulate when writers get eight episodes, then wait years, "if their show doesn't get canceled by yet another goddamn media merger."

Change #3: Overseas production severed the writer-audience feedback loop. Writers used to walk next door from the writers' room to rehearsal and taping, punching up jokes in real time against a live studio audience. Now entire seasons are written before a single frame is shot. "Comedy is not just a monologue. It's a conversation between the writer and the audience" — and laughter is a visceral reaction you can't fake from afar.

The process is the product. Shows weren't funny because writers willed it; they were funny because of how they were made. The factory is gone, so streamers are "incapable of making a new Friends or Seinfeld" and instead trade classic sitcom rights "like medieval nobles trading Roman statues that no artist alive can sculpt."

The stakes are social, not just entertainment. Sitcoms force characters who'd be in different high school cliques to find reasons to love each other — Sam the jock with the mailman, the drunk accountant, and the feminist nerd. In a lonely, polarized era, that's the format we need most.

Tools, Practices, and Methodologies

Live-audience feedback loop: Writers and actors performing in front of a real studio audience, using real-time laughter (or its absence) to judge and punch up jokes. Conover says this is why he toured his stand-up hour for 18 months before taping it, and why Conan O'Brien tests Oscar jokes at open mics.

Writers' room adjacent to production: Physically locating the writers' room next to the soundstage so writers can watch rehearsals and tapings and revise jokes on the spot — the practice that made multi-cam sitcoms consistently funny.

Long season orders as character development: The broadcast model of 20+ weekly episodes across fall/spring seasons, which gives writers time to learn their characters and audiences time to form "genuine social bonds" (or at least parasocial ones) with them.

Writing-while-airing: Overlapping production and broadcast so writers can incorporate ratings, critical response, and fan reaction into later episodes — how The Office, 30 Rock, and New Girl stayed funny without live audiences.

Unanswered Questions and Omissions

The economics are asserted, not proven. Conover claims outsourcing and short orders save companies money, but never quantifies how much, or addresses whether the old factory model was actually profitable under streaming economics (or whether it only worked under ad-supported broadcast).

No counterargument engaged. He dismisses Rainn Wilson's cancel-culture claim in one joke but never seriously addresses whether audience taste, prestige-TV prestige economics, or the collapse of the mid-budget comedy might share blame.

"What it would take to get them back" is promised but barely delivered. The video's stated goal — explaining what would restore sitcoms — gets only a vague "eventually this sitcom will rise from the dead," with no concrete policy, business, or creative prescription.

The ad-supported streaming question goes unasked. If sitcoms retain subscribers so well, why haven't streamers noticed? The incentive-mismatch puzzle he sets up is never resolved.
