Retailers don't lose $1.73 trillion a year because they can't see their inventory — they lose it because visibility never converts into timely action. The speaker's falsifiable claim is that dashboards are structurally incapable of preventing stockouts and dead stock, because they leave four gaps: no alert, no owner, no recommended next step, and no follow-through ("The task quietly dies at the end of the shift"). The competitive edge in retail has therefore shifted from seeing stock to how fast a signal becomes an owned, completed action — and AI-driven systems that classify products nightly, recommend reorder levels, and draft purchase orders for human approval can cut inventory holding costs by at least 10–15%, which matters because roughly 80% of a retailer's capital is tied up in stock.

Key points

The dashboard paradox. Every retailer in the $1.73 trillion loss figure had dashboards, reports, and real-time data. More dashboards have not fixed bestsellers running out, slow movers piling up, delayed transfers, or purchasers waiting for manual checks. "A number on the screen is not a decision" — someone must notice it, own it, and act fast enough for it to matter.

Four gaps between seeing and acting. (1) No alert — nobody watches every product in every store every hour, so problems surface only when someone happens to look. (2) No owner — unowned problems always wait. (3) No recommended next step — the dashboard shows a number but not how much to order, from where, by when. (4) No follow-through — tasks die at shift's end with no record or confirmation.

Two failure stories. The fast mover slipping away: a bestseller quietly runs low across three stores, each drop looks small, and by the weekly report the customer has moved on. The slow mover that never leaves: it never triggers an out-of-stock alert, ties up cash and space for months, and is discovered only when discounts eat the margin. In both, "nobody lacked data."

The action chain replaces the red number. When stock runs low, iVendNext detects the problem continuously, converts it into an alert with an owner, checks whether other locations can fulfill before buying, and prepares a draft purchase order for a person to approve. "There's always a human in the loop."

What AI actually does here — not a crystal ball. It watches every product daily, classifies items as fast, slow, non-moving, or critical (an FSN analysis run nightly around midnight), recommends reorder level, quantity, and best supplier, and attaches a confidence score so teams approve a good decision in one click instead of investigating from scratch. Under the hood: ML algorithms like ARIMA reading historical patterns.

Slow movers need more than markdowns. Clearance sales are "the easy part." The AI Gateway module runs affinity analysis on historical data to suggest promotion combinations — what sells with what — so slow stock moves without pure margin destruction.

The industry is moving. Per Deloitte, AI use for supply chain visibility will rise from 30% to 41%, and 59% of leaders expect ROI on AI supply chain investment within 12 months. This is shifting from a differentiator to a baseline expectation for all retailers, not just enterprise players.

Before/after state. Before: separate systems for selling, buying, finance; data glued together in emails and spreadsheets; tribal knowledge in people's heads; every team sees a partial picture. After: one real-time view, one shared list of decisions with owners and statuses, notifications (in-app or email) fired the moment an item turns critical.

Tools, practices, and methodologies

iVendNext (City Access Technologies). Web-based, AI-baked retail management platform: POS, mobile POS, buying/procurement, inventory/warehouse, finance, loyalty, CRM — one application on one data pool, integrating with online stores, marketplaces, and ERPs like SAP Business One. Use it as "one platform, one version of truth" rather than stitched-together tools.

iVendNext AI Foresight (forecasting module). Optional licensed module running nightly classifications (fast/slow/non-moving/critical) using ML such as ARIMA. Use it to get reorder levels, suggested order quantities, lead times, and safety stock factors per item — e.g., reorder level 6, quantity 128, 14-day lead time, 1.2 safety stock factor — with a prediction confidence score (70% shown in demo).

Decision queue. A filtered workspace narrowing focus from ~2,500 items to only those needing action, eliminating spreadsheet reconciliation. Use it so the team works exceptions, not the whole catalog.

Automated draft purchase orders. On a reorder alert (e.g., stock of 2, forecast need of 6), the system recommends a preferred supplier and lead time and generates a PO in draft mode for approval. Keep humans in the approval loop.

iVendNext AI Gateway. Second AI module for affinity analysis on historical sales data — recommends which items to bundle into promotions to move slow stock meaningfully.

Workflows, triggers, and notifications. Configurable alerts (in-app, email) fired the moment an item moves from normal to critical, so "a view that gets actioned upon even before someone reaches the view."

FSN analysis + just-in-time stocking discipline. Continuously fine-tune reorder levels, quantities, buffers (10–30%), and lead times to keep inventory near just-in-time and release working capital.

Unanswered questions and omissions

No evidence for the headline numbers. The $1.73 trillion figure and the "10–15% minimum" holding-cost reduction are asserted, not sourced or demonstrated — no customer case study, no before/after data from a real deployment.

Data readiness is glossed over. ARIMA-based forecasting needs clean historical sales data; the talk never addresses retailers with messy, incomplete, or short histories, or how long until confidence scores become trustworthy.

The human-in-the-loop failure mode is unexamined. If the original problem is that nobody acts on dashboards, what guarantees a person approves the draft PO in time? Approval bottlenecks and alert fatigue go undiscussed.

Costs, implementation, and integration effort are absent. No pricing, no onboarding timeline, no discussion of migrating from legacy systems or ERP integration friction — despite SAP Business One being name-checked.

Forecasting accuracy limits. The speaker explicitly avoided "forecasting techniques, the different ML techniques" to keep the session non-technical — reasonable for a webinar, but it leaves the audience unable to probe how the system handles seasonality, promotions, new products, or demand shocks.

Competitive positioning is thin. No comparison against standalone demand-planning tools or what competitors' automation already does; the case rests on the single-platform argument alone.

The Q&A sidesteps depth. The field-services question gets a confident "yes" with no detail on how non-retail inventory (cabling, technician vans) maps to the retail data model.
