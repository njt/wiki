# Study uncovers lost 'golden age' of languages

By Mike Cummings, Yale News, July 23, 2026

A new study published July 23 in the journal *Science* reveals that a linguistic "golden age" flourished between 1,000 and 3,000 years ago, when tens of thousands of languages were spoken worldwide — far more than the roughly 7,600 languages spoken or signed today. The research, the product of a long international collaboration between experts in language, demography, and evolutionary biology, challenges the commonly held view that widespread language extinction began roughly 500 years ago with European colonial expansion.

The study was led by Damián Blasi, an ICREA research professor at the Center for Brain & Cognition at Pompeu Fabra University in Barcelona. Coauthors include Claire Bowern, professor of linguistics in Yale's Faculty of Arts and Sciences; Russell Gray, director of the Department of Linguistic and Cultural Evolution at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig, Germany; and Marcus Hamilton of the University of Texas at San Antonio and the Santa Fe Institute.

Because direct evidence of languages doesn't exist before the invention of writing roughly 6,000 years ago, the researchers combined ethnographic information, estimates of prehistoric population size, and statistical and social-computational modeling to estimate linguistic diversity throughout the Holocene, the epoch covering about the last 12,000 years of human history. They used ethnographic data from 171 hunter-gatherer and fisher societies, anthropological records describing social structures of present-day hunter-gatherer and small-scale agricultural societies over the past two centuries, and estimates that the global population 12,000 years ago was between 4.4 million and 7 million people. Their models estimate that 4,500 to 6,200 languages were spoken 12,000 years ago — fewer than are spoken today. Linguistic diversity then grew alongside the human population for thousands of years, peaking during the golden age, before the rise of large states and multinational empires such as the Roman Empire caused a rapid decline.

The languages of expanding states and empires were disproportionately likely to survive, while those of absorbed, displaced, or declining populations were far more likely to disappear. The roughly 7,600 languages spoken or signed today represent a small and historically biased sample of the languages that once existed — survivors of what Gray calls "a massive and highly selective historical bottleneck."

## Quotes

Claire Bowern: "In a way, language is ephemeral — a spoken sentence disappears into sound waves — and, yet, our work demonstrates that we can use patterns in language to reconstruct large demographic events over the course of millennia."

Bowern: "We show that language isn't isolated from political currents and that this dynamic has been a facet of human history for much longer than previously thought."

Bowern: "This finding is important for the many endangered languages of today because political currents don't have to move in one direction. It's not inevitable that endangered languages become extinct."

Damián Blasi: "The world immediately before agriculture was probably not exceptionally rich in languages. There were most likely fewer languages than there are today. Linguistic diversity then grew alongside the human population for thousands of years."

Russell Gray: "The languages we see today are the survivors of a massive and highly selective historical bottleneck."

Gray: "A linguistic feature may be common not because it is inherently efficient or better for communication, but because it happened to be carried by populations that expanded. Extinction may have shaped linguistic diversity much more profoundly than we previously appreciated."
