"In Praise of Normal Engineers" — Charity Majors, Craft 2025

(Note: the agenda title was "Build Boring Engineering Orgs," but she pivoted mid-preparation to "In Praise of Normal Engineers." The talk is really about org design, not boring technology — the "boring tech" thread only resurfaces in Q&A.)

1. Key Points

The team is the unit of ownership — so the team is the unit of leverage. Some engineers genuinely are dramatically more productive, and Majors doesn't dispute that ("my reaction is more: so what?"). But teams own software. Everyone shares the same delivery pipeline: "if it takes the slowest engineer at your company five hours to ship one line of code, it's going to take the fastest engineer five hours to ship a single line of code." Hence: "if you must 10x something, 10x this: build 10x engineering teams."

Superstar worship lets leaders off the hook. Obsessing over pedigrees (ex-Google, ex-FAANG, Ivy League) and "top 10% of global talent" hiring language (she calls out Netflix and Coinbase by name) is backwards. The real competitive advantage is building socio-technical systems "where less experienced engineers and normal people can convert most of their effort and energy and hard work into actual product and business momentum." That's harder for leaders than shoveling money at minted talent — and that's fine.

A great org is defined by its floor, not its ceiling. The best engineering orgs are ones "where you don't have to be a world class engineer just to move the product, the business forward day by day." If you need staff-plus engineers just to make daily progress, "there's something deeply wrong with your organization."

Design internal systems for normal people. Normal people have confirmation bias, recency bias, zone out, stop reading repeated text blocks, and are worse at 2am than mid-day. When systems are navigable by normal engineers, surplus brilliance gets "poured into the product itself" instead of "wasted on navigating the systems." She saw this at places with six-month deploy lag: world-class engineers whose brilliance went to "fighting with their own system."

Long feedback loops create a doom spiral. Slow deploys → longer diffs → slower reviews → people paging projects in and out → bundled, infrequent deploys that "break more often than not" → more waiting → more SREs, managers, JIRA tickets. "It's either feeding on each other going downhill or it's feeding on each other going uphill." Her evidence: companies with 800 engineers — "doing what? Probably waiting on each other to get shit done."

Diversity is operational resilience, not squishiness. "A monoculture can move faster than any other team" — until someone gets sick, pregnant, or you hire outside the culture, "and suddenly everything falls apart." Teams already used to operating with mixed genders, ages, backgrounds, geographies "can roll with it." Replace "culture fit" with: "what angles or axes of diversity do they bring to this team?"

Great orgs mint world-class engineers; they don't rent them. Honeycomb engineers who couldn't command FAANG salaries on arrival could "waltz right out the door tomorrow and make at least half a million dollars a year" a few years later. Excellence is learnable, situational, and specific — "excellence for a security consultancy is very different from excellence for a 12-person VC-backed startup doing payments." Companies that only hire pre-minted talent "reinforce and replicate all of the prejudices and inequities of the world at large."

The Fundamental Attribution Error applies to your company. We over-credit individual agency and ignore the systems that shape behavior. Flip it: "Your company is a system. What kind of engineers are minted by your system?" Investments in up-leveling are reusable — they pay off every time someone joins, changes teams, or learns a new skill.

Hire for the right people, not the best people. People excited about your problems, good teammates, good communicators. "Communication skills are a pretty good predictor of collaboration skills and success."

2. Pithy and Provocative Quotes

"Any jackass can build an org where the best engineers in the world get shit done."
Her core indictment of superstar-hiring as a leadership strategy — it's the easy path, not the impressive one.

"Zero babies were born good at algorithms. Great engineers are forged, they're made, they're not born."
Her rebuttal to the smart-kid identity politics of the software industry.

"People who self-identify as 10x engineers are almost all raging assholes."
On why the meme provokes such rage — and why the best engineers she knows are humble. The 10x obsession "comes from insecurity."

"Talent may be evenly distributed across populations. Opportunity is not."
Why over-indexing on already-minted talent replicates society's inequities.

"Platform engineering is about treating engineers like people."
Her one-line definition of the platform engineering movement — apply product development and design thinking even though "your customers are internal engineers."

"The fastest way to ship a line of code should also be the easiest."

"It's a dirty little secret in our industry that honestly a lot of engineering is not that hard."
Followed by the weightlifting analogy: "we do the reps, doing the easy stuff," which builds the judgment for when real technical challenges arrive.

"Excellence is a learnable skill."
The thesis in five words. People don't show up at your door "done baked."

"Talent is as common as dirt. The potential for excellence is everywhere."

"The interview is the conversation."
Honeycomb's take-home isn't the interview — the conversation about your decisions afterward is.

"The closer you get to laying bits down on disk, the more conservative you should be."
Her Q&A rule for the boring-tech-vs-growth tension: databases and operating systems are "ooh," but dev tools and frontend are "where you get to fuck around and find out."

"Be the kind of company that they don't want to leave."
The closing line — retention through fulfillment, not golden handcuffs.

3. Tools, Practices, and Methodologies

"Choose Boring Technology" (Dan McKinney's decade-old post) — the argument that boring means "the failure circumstances are well understood." Cited as the inspiration for the original talk title; recommended reading.

Short deploy intervals — "Deploys are the heartbeat of your company" (Intercom, quoted approvingly). Keep them constant and automatic; the shorter the interval, the lower the "cognitive carrying cost." This is the antidote to the doom spiral.

Platform engineering as internal product — self-service deploy, rollback, debugging for engineers. Make it easy to do the right thing, hard to do the wrong thing.

Feature flags — flip features on and off without a new deploy, so engineers can own their code in production.

Engineers run what they write — the dev/ops split "cut in half" the feedback loop; the people getting feedback must be "best positioned to do something about it." In Q&A: put engineers on call for their systems because tight feedback loops make things better — but it must be "compatible with actual life and family."

Visualize your systems (observability + traces) — poor sense-making is "the dark matter of software engineering." Concrete example: cut CI/CD time by tracing the pipeline to see where time goes — "suddenly it becomes just engineering." Trying to understand code by reading it in the IDE is a waste of world-class cycles.

Internal tools team with internal SLOs — "if it's everybody's job, it's nobody's job." Example SLO: "anytime it takes over an hour for us to run tests and deploy an artifact to production, we invest into paying that down." Engineering productivity cannot be outsourced.

The Golden Path model — an official, SRE-supported tech stack; veer off it and you support your choice yourself. Her answer for balancing stability and experimentation.

A conservatism gradient — databases/OS: maximally boring; dev tools/frontend: safe to experiment. A second tool for the same tension.

Honeycomb's interview process — take-home (extend a piece of code), then a conversation with future peers about your decisions: what did you do, why, what would you do with more time, how would it scale. Selects for communication because it predicts collaboration — a choice that "served us really well" when everyone went virtual.

"Who is thriving?" analysis — for hiring managers shifting from "who's the best" to "who will thrive here": start by studying who actually thrives in your org (while watching for the bias trap — see below).

Axes-of-diversity hiring question — replace "are they a culture fit" with "what angles or axes of diversity do they bring that we need and don't have?"

Mixed-level team composition — teams of all staff-plus engineers are bored and on autopilot; everyone should be learning, teaching, and "used to being a newbie." "That is some healthy shit."

The Fundamental Attribution Error (sociology) — use as a lens: stop attributing outcomes to intrinsic traits and start examining the context and systems that produced them.

4. Unanswered Questions and Omissions

How do you dig out of the doom spiral? She describes the descent vividly — long deploys, bundled diffs, breakage, headcount bloat — but the only prescription is "keep the deploy interval short." No migration path for the 800-engineer company already trapped in it.

How do you unwind hero dependency? Orgs "tightly wrapped around" one or a dozen wizard engineers are "a huge liability," and "these people don't belong to you" — but she offers no plan for de-risking an existing hero-centric org, or what to do when the wizard is a team player who keeps their ego in check but is still a single point of failure.

The compensation contradiction. She skewers top-10% hiring language with "are you ready, willing and able to pay them salaries in the top 10% or top 1%?" — but never states her own pay philosophy. She admits Honeycomb mints engineers worth $500k/year at FAANG and retains them on fulfillment alone. Is that sustainable? What happens when it isn't?

The bootstrapping problem. If great orgs mint world-class engineers, what's the recipe before you have any? What does the founding team look like, and how do you build a learning culture without senior mentors to learn from?

Measuring "business impact." She calls it "the only really meaningful measure of productivity" — and never says how to measure it. This is the same vagueness she criticizes in "culture fit."

Managing people out. She explicitly disclaims it ("I'm not saying you should not manage people out or have high standards") but never describes how to do so in an org built on psychological safety and "everyone should feel safe to... make mistakes." The mechanics of high standards in a no-shame culture are left blank.

The "who thrives here" bias trap — flagged, not solved. In Q&A she concedes the risk herself: "if you're like, oh, well, women engineers don't seem to thrive here, I guess we won't hire any of those — that's a bit of a problem." Then calls it "complicated" and moves on. Also: managers "can't do it without support from upper" — but she offers no playbook for getting that support, despite calling herself "not a top down person."

Scale. Honeycomb is ~250 people, 70–80 engineers. She name-checks the 50,000-person enterprise as having a different definition of excellence — but never says how the boring-org recipe changes at that scale.

Time horizons. "A six month survival horizon calls for a very different strategy than a one or two year horizon" — she never says what those different strategies are. She concedes individual ownership is correct for early startups, which softens the talk's universal claim more than she acknowledges.

The 10x concession is never reconciled. The research is "flimsy, shoddy," the defenses "hilarious" — yet "there is some truth to it" and "some engineers are dramatically more productive than others." How much individual variance matters, and when, is left hanging.

AI is the elephant in the room. A 2025 talk on engineer productivity, hiring, and the value of "doing the reps" never mentions AI — which is currently rewriting assumptions about individual productivity multipliers, what junior engineers learn from, and what "normal engineer" even means. The omission is glaring.

The boring-tech promise is mostly abandoned. Despite the title, the talk contains almost no technology-selection guidance — the two useful rules (bits-on-disk gradient, Golden Path) arrive only in a one-minute Q&A answer.
