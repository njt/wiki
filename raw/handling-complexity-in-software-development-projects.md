Handling Complexity in Software Development Projects — Summary

Key Points: Complexity Is Per Sub-Problem, Not Per Project

The central move of the talk is a deliberate "trick." At the system level, productizing automated parking looks trivially simple on the Stacey Matrix — "everybody knows what parking is... that hasn't changed in the last 50 years," the hardware is settled, and "we've been using C since 20 years." That would imply pure waterfall. But when you decompose the system architecturally, each sub-problem lands in a different Stacey quadrant: computer vision is complex, path planning is complicated, odometry is simple. The audience voted these placements live via Slido and agreed.

Therefore: one phase, many work modes. "If you take the agile hammer and you put everybody in a scrum team with a scrum master, with the product owner and everything, this might not be the ideal way of doing it." Teams working on simple sub-problems should work simply; agile and waterfall teams can coexist within the same phase and "it's not really a problem."

Architecture is the tool that connects organization to complexity. You use architectural design to identify the complex parts, then "organize your team according to that to tackle the problem with the organization as well." Teams are mapped onto the Stacey Matrix the same way their sub-problems are.

The three-phase lifecycle:pre-development (build a demo vehicle, show it to OEMs, iterate, win the award), productization (meet automotive standards, target hardware, hundreds of contracted requirements, deadlines), fine tuning (last mile with the customer: bugs, parameter tuning). Innovation happens in all three, both bottom-up (small team functions) and top-down (replacing the entire neural network architecture with low-level fusion; "radar vision parking" replacing ultrasonic sensors with radar).

Transitions between phases must be managed consciously. You carry assets out of pre-development (know-how, confidence from beating competing suppliers, an empowered agile mindset) and liabilities (technical debt from demo corner-cutting, implementation gaps — "what happens if a camera is broken? Or there is snow... or the park markers are covered by leaves?", overconfidence, unknown unknowns, and the collision with strict automotive process requirements: documentation, architecture, requirements, traceability).

Alignment between differently-working teams is a people problem, not a process problem. Balázs: "I don't really believe that we can have really nice tools or processes for this" — you need "empowered interfaces," key colleagues who connect teams. Standardized interactions, including the daily standup, are optional.

Fine tuning is politics. The base team (Europe) ships software to an application team colocated with the customer (e.g., Japan), which tunes parameters, changes the UI, and reports bugs. As deadlines near, requirement uncertainty rises while technology locks down — "aligning with your customer is always politics. Discussing the deadlines is politics." You negotiate schedule, content, and priorities; you triage (a car crash beats quality debts); you fly engineers to the application team; and you communicate priorities relentlessly, or "your firefighters don't know what to do."

Tools, Practices, and Methodologies

Stacey Matrix — a 2-axis chart (requirement uncertainty vs. technological uncertainty) classifying work as simple, complicated, complex, or chaotic. The speakers' key usage: apply it per architectural sub-problem, not per project, and use the placement to select each team's work mode.

Three-phase lifecycle (pre-development → productization → fine tuning) — Continental's project arc: demo-to-win, standards-compliant product build, then customer-integrated last mile. Each phase has distinct goals, work modes, and team structures.

Architectural decomposition as an organizational design tool — break the system into sub-problems (computer vision, path planning, odometry), rate each on the matrix, and shape teams to match. "You can use architectural design to identify complex problems... and organize your team according to that."

Mixed work modes within one phase — explicitly permit agile and waterfall teams side by side; align them through people, not standardized process.

"Empowered interfaces" — key individuals who personally synchronize teams. Árpád: "You have to find the right people with the right experience for the right job... You have to find the right individuals" — rather than top-down workshops or rigid designs.

Demo-first customer engagement — build the demo vehicle, show OEMs, collect feedback, rework, repeat. Continental leverages existing OEM relationships ("What is the first thing coming to your mind when I say Continental? Tires.").

Conscious transition inventory — when moving from pre-development to productization, explicitly list assets (know-how, confidence, agile mindset) and liabilities (technical debt, implementation gaps, unknown unknowns, process obligations).

Change control board (anti-pattern) — in pre-development, a board approving all changes "will slow down the whole development. You have to trust your teams."

Base team / application team split — base team develops the core solution; application team colocated with the customer tunes parameters, adapts the UI, and files bugs; base ships bug-fix releases. Code sharing via pull requests or clone-and-own branching (standard for per-OEM UIs).

Triage — in the endgame, rank incoming reports by severity: "If you crash your car... you focus on the car crash," not the quality debts.

Flight budget — a line item for flying base-team engineers to the application team so problems get resolved faster.

Timeboxing — how a waterfall team depends on a Scrum team: fixed milestones and deliverables at the boundaries, "freedom to act" inside the time slot, including make-or-buy decisions.

DevOps / "continuous everything" — Árpád's hard prerequisite: without full continuous integration/delivery, feedback-driven agile teams "will result back into this... waterfall workflow because they cannot do anything."

Colocation strategy — post-COVID, Continental streamlined its global footprint to concentrate each topic in one location (parking's largest headcount is in Budapest), enabling task-force mode in one room.

People rotation for know-how transfer — "Easiest way: people transfer between teams" — move someone across for a few months rather than documenting.

Quarterly reorganizations / quick reaction forces — the standing mechanism for complexity jumps mid-project.

Board-level independent consultants — for startups: recruit ex-senior automotive leaders as board members to inherit their OEM networks.

Unanswered Questions and Omissions

How is complexity actually rated? Árpád concedes the rating is "more or less done top down by the architects" — no rubric, no criteria, no reconciliation process when architects and teams disagree. Worse, he admits work-mode assignment is partly driven by preference ("there are some people who don't like agile"), which undercuts the claim that organization follows complexity rather than taste.

What happens when you misclassify? Odometry is rated "simple" and gets a simple work mode — but the risk of a "simple" sub-problem turning complex mid-project (and the cost of discovering that with a waterfall team attached) is never addressed.

The safety-standards elephant. They invoke "very strict processes," documentation, and traceability as a hard contradiction with agile — then never name a single standard (ISO 26262, ASPICE) or explain how agile teams produce certifiable artifacts. The contradiction is declared and abandoned. Likewise, AI/neural networks in safety-critical ADAS are mentioned as innovation (low-level fusion, new network architecture) with zero discussion of how AI components get validated or certified.

The "empowered interfaces" model has a bus factor of one. The entire cross-team alignment strategy rests on finding "key people" — succession, burnout, and what happens when they leave are never mentioned.

Colocation vs. the global footprint. The talk preaches same-location teams while Continental runs sites in India, Romania, and Mexico. The Q&A answer ("we checked our global footprint and tried to come up with some kind of streamlining") raises the obvious follow-up — what happens to the streamed-out locations and their utilization — which goes unanswered.

Technical debt is named, never measured. It's flagged as a pre-development liability, but there's no method offered for quantifying it or paying it down during productization.

Configuration management and variants vanish. Introduced early as a major complexity driver ("all the extra stuff on top... configuration management, variants and so on"), then never mentioned again.

Where are the actual failures? The talk opens with "we made a lot of mistakes, failures, errors. Please learn from these" — but no concrete failure is ever dissected. The mistakes stay abstract; every phase is presented as ultimately handled well.

The human cost of transitions. Moving an empowered, happy agile team into documentation-heavy waterfall mode is presented as a process checklist item. Morale, retention, and reskilling are never discussed — despite Balázs admitting team utilization swings "in each phase and also in each month" with only "find the right balance" as the answer.

Clone-and-own's long-term bill. App teams branch the base per OEM (every UI differs), and parking is "80 or 90%" base software — but the maintenance burden of perpetual per-OEM forks is never costed.

"Value to the customer" is the closing takeaway but never operationalized — no metrics, no examples of cutting work because it lacked customer value.

The Kent Beck "desert or forest" callback is used twice as an audience hand-vote ritual with zero explanation — incomprehensible to anyone who didn't attend the prior talk, which is everyone watching this one later.
