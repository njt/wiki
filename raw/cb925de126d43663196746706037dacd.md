Event Storming for Fun and Profit — Daniel Terhorst-North, Craft 2025

1. Key points — "Everybody knows what everyone knows": storming as story-building, systems thinking, and group therapy

The talk is the demo. North designed the talk by event-storming it — the slides are the wall of stickies ("once upon a time, Daniel decided to explain event storming… until finally the audience could try event storming for fun and profit"). The meta-move carries his core claim: event storming is narrative construction, built on the Disney formula every movie follows — initial status quo, "one day," "because of this," "until finally," new status quo.

The goal is shared knowledge, not documentation. Everyone arrives knowing one part of the elephant; the exercise ends when "everybody knows what everyone knows." The entire attendance heuristic: "people with questions, people with answers, and someone with stationery."

The mechanics are trivial — that's the point. Orange = domain events in past tense; stimuli (commands, external events, time events like "the first of the month happened"); pink = questions and puzzles, placed diagonally; plus view/read models. Start at "until finally" and fill left-to-right on a long wall with spare space on both sides, because "you're going to discover… you shouldn't have started here."

Storm a business process to strip it before automating. Automation does two things: makes a process deterministic, and "calcifies" it. Storm first, delete the vestigial steps ("we do it because we've always done it that way"), or you bake in work that "no one knows why. It's just always been there."

Storm a legacy system to get past the data model. People's first dump is suspiciously fast — and it's the data model, not behavior. "Clearing your throat" is a required phase before anyone can see how the system actually works. The end goal is discovering the "seams" (Mike Feathers' term).

Storm a new system to discover nobody knows anything. When pink stickies outnumber orange, "none of us knew" — and the real work is "let's pay down some of this uncertainty," not design. Skip this step and you get "the usual ball of mud."

Visualization alone is the intervention. The trading-firm server story: a 30-day lead time became 12 days (re-sequencing and parallelization spotted by the room), then two hours (pre-stocking three server types — "money makers," dev machines, "donkeys" — for a few thousand dollars of sunk cost). "We haven't done anything" — no new technology, just the whole process on a wall for the first time. The head of infrastructure's reaction: "I've never seen this process laid out end to end before."

The experience beats the output. The output is often "we don't know a bunch of stuff — let's do this again in two weeks." The durable value is putting people from the same value chain in one room — a leadership team told him, "this is probably the second time in a year we've all been in the same physical location."

Conflict is data. Goldratt's "universal harmony": there's one reality, so conflict means divergent views of it — find where the parties last agreed. The facilitation stance follows: "arguing to learn" over "arguing to be right"; "listening like you don't know the answer" over "listening, waiting to speak."

Disruptive people are operating on positive intent. Satir's principle: "everyone is trying to help" according to their own world model — the saboteur believes you are going to harm the organization. The empathy question: what must be true for them such that this behavior is the helpful thing to do? (Exception conceded: sociopaths exist, "often in leadership roles.")

Adoption is a safety problem, not a persuasion problem. "Trust Me Once": frame it as a cheap, reversible experiment — "all it cost you is a morning." For closed-minded bosses: Grace Hopper's forgiveness-over-permission. "JFDI."

3. Tools, practices, and methodologies — The whole kit: stationery, ground rules, and a tomato timer

The core technique

Event Storming (Alberto Brandolini) — A collaborative discovery/mapping exercise using stickies on a long wall. Bring everyone who knows the thing; the goal is "everybody knows what everyone knows." He calls it "one of the most self working techniques I've ever come across" and one of the easiest to facilitate.

Domain events (orange stickies) — Past-tense statements of things that happened; the atomic unit. He wishes Brandolini had chosen yellow, because orange is expensive and "you end up getting through a lot of orange stickies."

Stimulus stickies — Commands or external events that cause domain events: a person doing a thing, a system doing a thing, or a time-based event ("the first of the month happened").

Pink stickies — Questions and puzzles; place them diagonally "like a diamond shape because it makes them more obvious." Never run out — scarcity of questions kills the exercise.

View/read models — Someone getting information out of a system; the fourth sticky type.

The Disney story formula — "Once upon a time… one day… because of this… until finally." Use it as the narrative spine of every storm.

Start at the end — Model "until finally" first, then fill in time flowing left to right. Use a long wall with space on both sides, because you'll discover you started in the wrong place.

Clustering → aggregates/subdomains — Events that group by shared language reveal cohesive subsystems (DDD aggregates), candidates for their own storms. Q&A heuristic: bigger clusters attract more pink stickers — density signals complexity.

Applications

Vestigial process stripping — Before automating a process, ask what's just "we've always done it that way" and delete it; otherwise automation calcifies it permanently.

Clearing your throat — In legacy storms, let people dump their assumed model (usually the data model) first; only then ask how the system actually works.

Data flow diagram overlay — A diagnostic trick: overlay the DFD to check whether people are describing behavior or just reciting data.

Uncertainty paydown — For greenfield, when questions outnumber events, the deliverable is discovery work and a reconvene date — not a design.

Facilitation

Time cop — Deliberately time-box the workshop to create compression and urgency; sometimes you just move things along.

Explicit ground rules — Common decency plus scope control: off-pitch discussions become a pink sticky with a takeaway action ("figure out why we all disagree on pricing"), then back to the story.

Universal harmony (Eli Goldratt) — One reality; in conflict, find where the parties last agreed and work from the divergence. The answer is often "over here somewhere," reached by both parties teaching each other.

Arguing to learn / listening like you don't know the answer — The facilitator's stance, against the defaults of arguing to be right and listening-waiting-to-speak.

Principle of positive intent (Virginia Satir) — Everyone is trying to help per their own world model; ask "what must be true for them such that this behavior helps." Your hook for connecting with disruptors and saboteurs.

The archetype playbook — Disruptor/null ("please just be a participant"); wallflower (draw them in — often the deepest domain knowledge, "wisdom happens"); helper (usually a senior guy claiming status — make him a participant, not co-facilitator; occasionally a genuine ally who quietly influences colleagues); last-word person (let them have it — "life is too short"); surprise star (track their career).

Supporting practices

Pomodoro technique — 25-minute work chunks with 5-minute breaks; four makes a two-hour block, then a real break. The rarely-taught key: the notepad for capturing distractions so your brain "doesn't need to think about it." Necessary because storming is "very cognitively intense."

Photographs + OCR — Capture walls as photos, keystoned and color-corrected, then OCR them. "The main thing is having it searchable," not structured — he wants to find every session that mentioned "pricing."

Trust Me Once — Adoption model: frame any new practice as a cheap, reversible experiment to lower the safety barrier.

Forgiveness over permission (Grace Hopper) — For closed-minded bosses: do it, then report the unlocked value.

Six Thinking Hats (Edward de Bono) — Name-checked as a fellow "back-pocket tool" that still works exactly as written 30–40 years later.

Seams (Mike Feathers) — The vocabulary for what legacy storming is really hunting for.

Buxton's UX definition + affordances/signifiers (from The Design of Everyday Things) — UX is what the user feels, not the shape or color. His answer to integrating UX discovery: "I would do a UX discovery event storming session" — expect tons of pink stickers and research actions.

4. Unanswered questions and omissions — The pink stickies left on the wall

Distributed teams get a shrug. He dismisses virtual storming ("the energy is not nearly the same") and answers the online-tooling question with "just photographs" — no method for teams that can't get warm bodies in one room, despite calling himself "a massive fan of remote work" for inclusivity. The tension is named, never resolved.

No failure modes. "One of the most self working techniques I've ever come across" — but no counterexamples, no contexts where storming is the wrong tool, no mention of a storm that flopped.

No bridge from stickies to software. How clusters become aggregates, process managers, or code got "it depends" in Q&A. He also never distinguishes event storming from event sourcing — the confusion every DDD-curious audience brings through the door.

The sociopath loophole. Positive intent presumes a coherent world model; he concedes sociopaths "are among us… often in leadership roles" but offers no tactic for when the empathy question has no good answer.

Break up arguments vs. let them run. Both are advised; the judgment call — which one, when — is left entirely to instinct.

Who owns the pink stickies afterward? The output is often "we don't know a bunch of stuff — let's do this again in two weeks," but there's no method for tracking actions, owners, or momentum between sessions.

Scale logistics glossed. Forty people, a full day, a roll of butcher's paper — how you synthesize, validate, and document at that scale gets one sentence.

No path to go deeper. No mention of Brandolini's book or his big-picture/process-level/design-level taxonomy of storming; on facilitation psychology he deflects — "I'm an amateur at this. Go talk to Kat Hicks."

The politics of process surgery. The CIO sign-off quietly became "pre-approval" and a third of the lead time vanished — but the organizational politics of stripping steps (and status) from a process, possibly in front of the people whose power those steps encode, goes unexamined. Related: the head of infrastructure had never seen his own process end to end — a governance red flag played for laughs.

Sensitive domains. Patient record systems are name-checked as storming targets, but nothing is said about confidentiality when 40 people map a regulated process on a wall.

Evidence beyond anecdotes. The server story is one win; beyond "trust me once," there's no way offered to quantify the value for a skeptical CFO.
