I spent fifty days on campus over the course of two years at my low-res MFA, soaking up as much writing wisdom as possible from incredible teachers. It’s now been nearly two years since I graduated, so for fun I revisited the notebook I brought to every residency, typed all the advice into a spreadsheet and sorted it by category for you.

*A few notes: Since this was already so long, I decided to only include notes on fiction. Much of the advice is the personal opinion of a specific teacher and not intended to be universal, so don’t yell at me in the comments because you disagree. And for legal reasons, these are my original thoughts/interpretation and not literal class content.*

## Characters

1. If your character wants to do something strange, let them.

2. Characters need to do things, not just think, decide, etc.

3. Don’t worry about motivation/why people are the way they are, who cares?

4. Simplify the action and complicate the motivation.1

5. Characterization is action, the choices they make.

6. “Joe remembered” no, the whole story is joe remembering.

7. “Joe decided” no, what did Joe *do*?

8. “Joe worried” no, give him something to worry about. “Beth could come home at any time.”

9. Abstractions/broad terms are unhelpful “she’s conservative” “he’s traumatized” make yourself get specific.

10. You need to release the right number of rabbits, a.k.a. you need enough threads to make characters’ lives meaningful and interesting.

11. When a character isn’t interesting enough, she usually doesn’t have enough to do. Give her something to do well or poorly.

12. Common problem with speculative stories: the characters need to be interesting enough without the gimmick.

13. Inhabit the character rather than explaining the character.

14. Resist the tendency to write about souls in jars, from the neck up; only aware of other characters when the POV character looks at them.

15. Do you characters only see other people’s heads? Avoid “eyebrow work”: squinting, blinking, eye contact, grinning, frowning. These aren’t real actions.

16. It’s a mistake to only describe autonomic body functions - heart pounding, etc.

17. The body is the apparatus of the plot. If you’re just thinking, you’re frozen.

18. Too much action = a dog on an agility course. Too much emotion without action = fetal position, staring through a rainy window.

19. Physicality ≠ appearance.

20. The body is a setting. Clothing is a setting.

21. Try pulling yourself into the body of a character and just feeling it.

22. Aim for saturated characters.

23. Give a minor character agency.

24. Consider what someone says they want vs. what their actions show.

25. Characters who don’t know themselves clearly think through metaphors/proxies. They want something forbidden so they instead pretend they want something else or describe the landscape.

26. How does repulsion relate to desire within a character?

27. When you have two characters talking and they’re not really connecting, have one of them reach out and touch the other in an unusual way, and they’ll begin reacting to each other.

28. You shouldn’t see the POV character from the outside much — she tucked her hair behind her ear, revealing her sharp jaw. Revealing to who? You can’t see your own jaw.

29. Desperate people are emotionally potent.

## Dialogue

30. Save dialogue for when we really want to hear them talk, when their words matter.

31. What is dialogue doing beyond the literal meaning?

32. Dialogue shouldn’t verbalize subtext.

## Endings

33. The ending isn’t an ending, it’s part of the whole.

34. Good endings respect the reader’s intellect, bad endings treat them like a fool.

35. The most emotional endings are not what the reader wants, but are true to what needs to happen.

36. Ending with an image lets the reader interpret it, but you have to set it up earlier in the story.

37. Bad endings: ambiguity/lack of confidence; an image looking out onto a landscape feels cheap.

38. Bad endings: epiphany — lacks direction.

39. Bad endings: characters isolated in rooms, thinking.

40. Ambiguous ending ≠ ambiguous action. What literally happened should be clear.

41. Ambiguous vs. vague endings: the ending can pose a question, but the reader should at least know what the question is.

42. Write or summarize the ending you’re most afraid of, the one that horrifies or disgusts you.

43. Ending of a short story often makes you reconsider the events of the story.

44. Endings should lead to complexity of afterthought.

45. The end of the story should not feel like the end of time.

## General Craft Advice

46. If you are fighting to not see the dark things in your mind, you will write really boring stories.

47. Don’t list context, sprinkle it in.

48. There’s the thing that happened and then there’s the writerly description of it.

49. When it’s difficult and risky, you’re onto something.

50. You don’t need to say how people feel when it’s obvious, when a baby dies you don’t have to say everyone was sad.

51. Journalism makes arguments, fiction stages arguments.

52. Don’t keep secrets that people in the room would know.

53. May be tempting to describe directional details or days of the week — don’t.

54. Common error: reiterating your point too much.

55. When you insist on something, it’s weaker. “She’s beautiful” vs. description of her beauty.

56. Details should be something you wouldn’t necessarily think of. E.g. fumbling with a wallet containing both dollars and euros.

57. If you’ve lost interest in a story, or if it’s difficult to decide what to work on next and doesn’t get easier, let it go.

58. Don’t choose realism when realism is boring.

59. Murder cliches at every level from the word to the theme.

60. When writing big emotions, you may be tempted to use big grand words. Ground it in small, concrete detail.

61. Think about images from your own life to avoid cliches.

62. Important moments = change life in a concrete way. Resonant moments = smaller, quieter, yet bigger emotional weight.

63. Resonant moments: on the shoulders of an important moment. The day before the wedding.

64. Important moments are socially constructed. Resonant moments are highly specific, probably wouldn’t appear on an outline.

65. Your job as a writer is not to meet readers’ expectations, it’s to write a story and bring the reader where you want them.

66. Don’t worry about the imaginary asshole who will “um, actually” your story.

67. “It’s a sin to be boring” – Elizabeth McCracken

68. RV Castle: writers can only do six things - narrative, interiority, exposition, direct dialogue, indirect dialogue, description. When something isn’t working, look at these six things.

69. A scene is a moment in time and has subtext that can be interpreted multiple ways, which is why it’s superior to summary.

70. Summary should be used sparingly. You can use it to skip the boring parts.

71. A life without desire is without purpose.

72. Interrogate your nefarious desires.

73. What does desire look like on the page? Where’s the attention, POV/what they focus on, what they say vs what they really want, do they move with purpose toward their desire?

74. Lots of desire = events move slowly and more words used.

75. “Excess of clarity” is boring.

76. Every narrator is unreliable to some degree.

77. What you resist is what you need to write.

78. The more complicated the novel is, the more clues you need to give the reader

79. Your first thought is probably the least original. You may have to go down a long road to get to originality.

80. The most logical events, dialogue, and characteristics are all boring.

81. Sometimes there’s not enough going on, you may have to go back to the ideas store.

82. Are your elements changing over time? An Italian restaurant: is there a new manager, is it falling apart, is it catching on fire? Nothing is static.

83. Making a mess is imperative.

84. The longer you take off from working on the project the harder it is to get back in. Don’t take too long a break.

85. Making an outline is like making New Year’s Resolutions.

86. Get the first draft out as fast as possible.

87. When writing, it’s okay to focus on clarity and sensory details. The deeper meaning may be subconscious, and once you see it, you can turn up the volume on it.

88. Go deeper, into realms beyond the literal.

89. Melville wrote Moby Dick far from the ocean — a palpable sense of longing. Sometimes you have to be at a remove from the thing you’re writing about.

90. Saying what isn’t can sometimes be more powerful than saying what is. “There are no stars tonight” vs. “the stars are out.”

91. Introducing a negative can sometimes make the meaning stronger/more interesting. When someone says they hate their ex, they really love their ex.

92. If someone says something obvious or does something logical/expected, make they do the opposite.

93. Louise Gluck originally wrote “I love them [the flowers]. I love them like I love sex.” Changed it to: “I hate them [the flowers]. I hate them like I hate sex.”

94. Work on conventional narrative first, before you get experimental.

95. When you break the model, is it intentional?

## Plot

96. If you’re looking for a narrative engine, make sure everything goes wrong.

97. Plot is when characters do the things they said they’d never do.

98. Plot set to a clock: a hurricane is coming.

99. Erect a barrier between what they want and where they are.

100. Simplicity in plot: someone wants something, they can’t get it, what do they have to do to try to get it? Allows you to get weirder.

101. Lob out questions that will take a couple hundred pages to answer.

## Reading

102. Better to read 10 excellent books over and over than 50 books coming out this month that will be forgotten immediately. Re-reading is good.

103. “You have to find your ancestors” - Alice Mattison

104. Read best short stories of the current year, see where your favorites were published and read those journals.

## Revision

105. Biggest obstacle to revision is that people don’t want to do it. But the art is in the revision, it’s not punishment.

106. Second drafts are often worse than first.

107. Remember it’s fun and you can be weird and you can bring your whole emotional self.

108. Tolstoy wrote War and Peace eight times by hand.

109. Pick an easy or fun change first.

110. Plan the next day’s work (something easy or fun again).

111. Pull tiny story strings apart — look only at place, only at voice/music of the prose, only color/texture, only tension in the in-between moments, minor characters, make objects interesting.

112. Do the scary thing: save it as “big scary draft.” Try it for 10-15 pages. Pay attention to your body and feelings.

113. Consider the clock: actual time to get to the hospital, a war, etc. If your novel takes place over a year, why? Can it be consolidated? Is there a sense of time? Where is the pressure coming from?

114. Consider patterns of all kinds: family tree, key objects, what’s bubbling up in this piece and what can you research more?

115. Your story in images: write in a few images each, a few pervasive images. Capture the feeling, clarify what you’re working toward. E.g. girls sitting on a pile of bones drinking stolen vodka, missing mall air conditioning. Unlike the outline, this is the heartbeat.

116. Imagine the scene, then add in all five senses.

117. Move something from page 3 to the first line and cut it in half.

118. Pare it down. Ruthlessly cut a third and the meaning will stay.

119. First draft is often scaffolding, and future draft is the art. You have to take the scaffolding away at some point.

120. Rewrite it from scratch.

121. Have three trusted readers and if two of them agree, concede.

122. You only get the virgin eyes of a trusted reader once. Make the most of it, don’t show it too early.

123. When you’re not ready to start rewriting, read intentionally.

124. It’s okay (necessary) to change things late in the game.

125. You have to be willing to destroy the thing you’ve built and put it back together to make it better.

## Sentences/Words

126. Rhythm of sentences — try musicality. A quote about being on a boat rocks like a boat.

127. Make sure there’s a sentence you love on every page.

128. Cultivate reverence for the sentence. The words you choose are crucial to the meaning.

129. Deep love of language is not necessary.

130. Chimneys “threw out smoke” vs. “smoke drifted.” Which sets up a violent tone?

131. Word choice: walked vs waddled vs meandered.

## Setting

132. World building isn’t just for sci fi and fantasy.

133. When building a place, start with what you can see.

134. As a writer you don’t have a movie budget, you have unlimited budget. Don’t hold back.

135. If you set your breakup at a restaurant the actions are pretty limited “drinking wine” if you set it at a zoo more interesting things can happen and outside forces can take the conversation in unexpected directions.

136. Setting: an office. Obvious(boring) signifiers - copy machine, cubicles. Original (specific) - desk calendar from their uncle’s auto dealership, a small bowl of cough drops.

## Structure

137. Short stories can push against the edges and become more novel-ish, or they can push toward the center and begin to collapse into a poem

138. When working with two timelines, the reader should know which timeline you’re in within the first four words.

139. Your separate projects may just be one project.

140. Fragments shift time and place, creating a dreamscape.

141. You can write towards a whole without knowing what it is. Constructing a dinosaur from the bones.

142. Don’t flashback to interrupt a scene, plant something for the flashback and wait for the end of the scene.

143. Reflections go at the end of a scene or mini scene

144. Backstory traditionally takes place in the second chapter.

145. Obsession drives the novel.

146. When you write a story with a twist, you’re writing two stories: the one you see on first reading, and the one you see the second time.

147. Create a set of expectations so you can thwart them.

148. The opening of Moby Dick immediately gives motivation = I have to go see or I’ll kill myself. To put someone on an adventure, you have to give them no other choice. Super specific and also a grand, universal gesture.

149. Every scene must say something new; not just characterization.

150. The length of a scene usually corresponds to its importance in the work.

151. Flashbacks aren’t memories. They shouldn’t be staring out a window, hit in the head with a coconut. Enter enter is extremely effective punctuation

152. Flashbacks inherently feel less important than things happening in the present.

## Writing Wisdom

153. “The writing never gets easier, we do it for the feeling of something exceeding our grasp, and the pleasure of getting it right.” - Parul Sehgal

154. The most important skill: write badly and tolerate your own bad writing enough to keep going.

155. It can take years to step into something and surrender to it.

156. Don’t try to solve the great mysteries.

157. Always be failing; always be trying to do something you cannot do.

158. Audacity is key.

159. The book you can’t talk yourself out of is the one you should write.

160. You can’t people please with a work of art.

161. Self-trust is the #1 thing to develop for writing a novel.

162. Write 1,000 words first thing in the morning because then you’re thinking about the story all day.

163. No phone/internet until after you’re done writing.

164. Social media will wreck your brain and make you think only of the critics. You need to be discerning with what you ingest.

165. No writing is wasted.

166. Nobody has ever written a novel without being a control freak

167. Self-doubt can’t be defeated through external validation.

168. Don’t ever tell your brain to shut up.

169. The time immediately after the MFA can be the most fruitful2.

170. Writing requires some level of arrogance.

171. It’s your book, it’s your tombstone. You’re the only one in there.

172. “Heed your obsessions” – Claire Vaye Watkins

173. Envision writing just for your one best writing friend.

174. Don’t write toward the haters. Are you disturbed or uncomfortable? Welcome to my art.

175. Point of the MFA is to claim your ambition and being an artist.

176. “The task of the writer is to get close to the feeling of being alive”- Carole Maso

177. There’s enough guilt and shame attached to writing, you don’t need to feel guilty about how often you write.

178. When you get home, forget all your notes.
