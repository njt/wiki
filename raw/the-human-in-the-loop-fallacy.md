But this approach creates a shrinking role for human workers: As AI improves, the need for human validation declines, while...moreLeer en españolLer em portuguêsPostPostShareSaveGet PDFBuy CopiesPrint“There will still be a human in the loop.” That may be the most repeated sentence in enterprise discussions around AI adoption—the phrase that executives reach for to explain why adopting systems is responsible, and why their people will be fine. Yet this supposedly reassuring phrase often amounts to arranging a company’s most valuable people around the very tasks that a machine is about to take from them. Thought leaders regularly declare that the role for human workers in the future will be to occupy the gaps that machines can’t fill. A leading venture-capital firm has argued, for example, that in the knowledge economy there will soon be four main types of human workers: those who stay accountable when AI cannot be trusted, those who design the workflows that machines will run, those who manage relationships that AI agents cannot, and those who validate outputs that machines produce. Visions of that future now appear in consulting frameworks, academic projections, and the deployment guides of AI vendors. Regulators have even written it into law: The EU AI Act mandates human oversight of high-risk systems, and “human-in-the-loop” has become the near-universal shorthand for responsible AI adoption. Predictions of this sort all involve some version of the same logic: Identify what AI can’t yet do and then define the work that people can do to fill those gaps. People in this way of thinking represent a safety layer that’s wrapped around imperfect automation. On Fiverr, the fastest-growing request in writing is “humanize AI content” (searches up 641% in six months). Proofreading AI-generated copy has been a service category of its own since 2023. In translation, a job category particularly impacted by AI, the task of correcting machine output now accounts for roughly a quarter of European language companies’ revenue, and conventional translation shrinks year over year. Even the job titles that survive and are designed around correcting machine output are shrinking in content, demanding fewer skills and almost no new ones. The idea of having people fill the gaps sounds prudent and promises continuity when many are concerned whether their jobs will survive. The problem, though, is that it starts from the existing workflow, asks which tasks agents can absorb, and assigns the leftovers to people. The future organization in this view is simply today’s organization minus the automated tasks plus a layer of supervision. People appear nowhere as a source of new productive capacity. This view has problems. Decades of research on automation show that better automation systems lead to worse human safety layers; as automation grows more reliable, the people monitoring it grow complacent, their skills atrophy, and they become less attentive. Four decades ago, Lisanne Bainbridge named this an “irony of automation”: The more you automate, the more you depend on exactly the human capabilities that automation takes away. AI compounds the problem. Its failures sound as confident, fluent, and well-structured as its successes. Reviewers who merely grade coherence fail to grade correctness. Even when validation works and people catch errors, every correction is captured as training data. Each exception a person handles teaches the system to handle that exception; the better the data, the smaller the scope of human validation needed in the future. xAI, for instance, dismissed roughly 500 of its generalist “AI tutors” in September 2025, while announcing plans to expand its specialist tiers tenfold. Of all the roles one could nominate for the future of human work, these surely have the shortest half-life. The larger problem with the human-in-the-loop organization, though, is that the entire blueprint reads the transformation backwards. It organizes the company around the shrinking frontier of what machines can’t yet do. But there’s a larger opportunity here. Because AI removes constraints, it enables new combinations of work, new decision rights, and new forms of value. Organizations, accordingly, have an opportunity to reorganize and “rebundle” around those new decisions rather than the shrinking residue of the old work. That’s what this article is about. Two Transformations Whenever a general-purpose technology enters an industry, firms are confronted with similar organizational choices. As machines become more capable, they remove constraints that once limited how work could be performed. That allows people to move into higher-value roles that were previously out of reach. Removing an execution constraint expands the range of choices an organization can afford to make. Consider what’s happened recently in the world of movie-making. In 2019, when The Mandalorian demonstrated how giant LED screens could display computer-generated environments in real time, allowing filmmakers to capture complex visual effects directly on camera. What appeared to be an innovation in visual effects was also the beginning of a fundamental change in how filmmaking could be organized. In fact, though, it represented a bigger change: Decisions that had previously been deferred to post-production moved onto the set. The look of the world, the lighting, the composition, and much of the final image could now be created and validated while the scene was being performed. Filmmakers could now move between richly constructed environments without building new sets or waiting months for effects work, making feature-scale world-building possible at television budgets. Visual-effects artists were able to move from repairing images after production to helping decide what the camera would capture in the first place. With the constraint removed, the range and location of meaningful choices changed, and the moviemaking organization rebuilt itself around the people now responsible for making them. New roles have now emerged in the industry to recombine cinematography, production design, visual effects, and computing, among them virtual production supervisor, volume operator, and virtual camera operator. That sort of “rebundling” is also on display in the changing nature of the newsroom. The traditional newsroom bundled three activities—gathering facts, writing stories, and distributing stories. As generative AI makes drafting articles, headlines, and summaries easier, many newsrooms have reacted by having journalists spend more time reviewing AI-generated copy, but the newsroom remains otherwise unchanged. The real opportunity for newsrooms, however, is to move upstream to identifying claims worth investigating, establishing their provenance, and producing evidence that others can trust. The BBC’s most prominent AI-era editorial innovation is tellingly not a faster writing desk with humans-in-the-loop but BBC Verify. In response to AI-generated misinformation and disinformation, the newsroom launched a dedicated team of journalists who use open-source intelligence, satellite imagery, forensic analysis, and data science to establish the truth of contested claims rather than simply report them. Reuters has expanded in the same direction, building specialist verification and fact-checking teams, and positioning verified, fact-based reporting as a commercial product in its own right. Managing Transformation The way out of the human-in-the-loop model is not to wait for improvements in AI to eliminate the need for verification. Instead of asking which tasks the machine can take over, leading organizations should ask which new roles the technology makes possible, and then redesign the organization around them. To that end, there are several steps you can take. Map fallen constraints and newly available capabilities, not automatable tasks. The standard AI transformation roadmap starts with a task audit: Which activities can the machine absorb, and how many hours would that save? That opening question needs to change. For each function, ask which constraint has just fallen and what new opportunities that opens up. What was previously too expensive to execute, too slow a process to iterate, too small a market to serve? When IKEA’s customer-service chatbot began handling routine inquiries, in 2021, it freed up employees to do more valuable work. Between 2021 and 2023, the chatbot resolved 3.2 million inquiries, delivering nearly €13 million in savings. But Ingka Group—IKEA’s largest retailer—went further, retraining 8,500 call-center employees in remote interior design. By redirecting human capacity toward personalized advice, IKEA could expand its sales and support capabilities rather than simply reduce customer-service costs. By 2022, Ingka’s remote-selling channel was generating €1.3 billion in annual sales. Rather than using automation only to cut costs in today’s work, IKEA used it to expand the value its employees could create. Fund frontier cases, not just efficiency cases. Efficiency gains often seem much more practical and achievable, but they keep the organization fixated in the automation frame and delay the more thoughtful work of redesign. For every AI business case, require the team to identify not only the hours it will save but also a problem the technology makes newly solvable. In the UK, Garfield Law—authorized by the UK’s solicitors’ regulator in May 2025 as the first “AI-driven law firm”—pursues small debts that were uneconomic for conventional firms to take on, with fees starting at £2, and named solicitors accountable for every step; within a year it had initiated hundreds of claims and won its first contested trial. This is not an efficiency gain within an existing market. It’s a new market created by changed economics. Invest in the continuous assessment of human capabilities alongside evolving AI capabilities. Static role definitions cannot survive contact with a capability curve that moves every few months. Johnson & Johnson uses machine learning to infer the skills of thousands of its technologists from the data generated by their work, scores them against the capabilities its strategy will need next, and refreshes the assessment in successive rounds. Rather than treating workforce capabilities as static, the company is building a system for continuously assessing what its people can do, what they need to learn, and how their contributions can evolve as technology advances. Rebundle governance into the work itself rather than bolting it on at the end. If validation checkpoints are both unreliable and self-liquidating, the alternative is not less governance but governance of a different kind: rules embedded in the work, owned by accountable people, and verified continuously. Consider the work Morgan Stanley did before and after rolling its GPT-4 assistant out across its wealth-management organization. During the pilot, the firm’s experts defined what good answers looked like and assembled roughly 400 “golden questions” with known correct answers against which the system could be tested. Morgan Stanley then embedded evaluation into production. A regression suite of sample questions now runs daily to identify weaknesses and test whether changes degrade the quality or compliance of its answers. This is an example of continuous governance inside the deployment rather than through occasional, after-the-fact reviews of what advisers were told, in one of the most heavily regulated areas of knowledge work. Test whether you are really enabling transformation at all. Genuine reorganization differs from mere task reallocation in three important ways. First, the firm boundaries are redrawn, as the firm moves to internalize the learning cycle and the critical AI capabilities that power its transformation, rather than renting them from external vendors. Second, the unit of organization changes as teams rebuild around newly solvable problems, not around the stages of the old pipeline. And finally, the center of gravity moves. The functions that play the new game sit closer to the CEO. A reorganization that adds new titles but fails to account for these three shifts is merely a case of task reallocation—merely another version of the human-in-the-loop organization. John Deere’s transformation illustrates these three shifts in practice. First, Deere redrew the boundaries of the firm to bring critical learning capabilities in-house through its acquisition of computer-vision startup Blue River Technology in 2017. Second, in 2020, it changed the unit of organization and reorganized its equipment businesses around production systems (the interconnected jobs and decisions involved in growing crops and completing other customer work) rather than around individual product lines. Third, it created the first chief technology officer role in its 183-year history. The move shifted the center of gravity, by placing responsibility for a unified technology stack under an executive reporting directly to the CEO. Together, these decisions changed what Deere owned, how its businesses were organized, and where technological decisions were made. Rather than fitting AI into its existing organizational structure, the company redesigned the structure around the opportunities that these new technological capabilities opened up. . . . As AI removes execution constraints, the gains will go to companies that decide how to use their new freedom: what to make, whom to serve, which problems to take on. Companies that relegate their people to managing residual tasks will be doing more than missing the opportunity to transform. They’ll be mistaking unbundling as the destination rather than a trigger for new thinking.
