content: 'Press enter or click to view image in full sizeAgentic AiSoftware DevelopmentData GovernanceEconomicsThe Economic Paradox of AI-Generated SoftwareYassien ShaalanFollow7 min read·8 hours agoListenShare“The best code is no code at all.” Jeff AtwoodImagine a small retailer who needs a better way to manage orders, inventory and customer enquiries. Shopify, spreadsheets and existing business platforms already provide much of this functionality, but perhaps the retailer dislikes the subscriptions, finds the workflows restrictive or simply wants something tailored to the business. Previously, building an alternative required considerable development effort, making the existing products the economically sensible choice. Now an AI coding agent can generate a working application within hours, changing the calculation almost overnight. Research on vibe-coding identifies speed, accessibility, learning and creative satisfaction among the motivations, which makes sense because people aren’t necessarily trying to reinvent software, they’re trying to escape the compromises of using someone else’s. However,The declining cost of creating software can make building attractive even when the economic value of owning another application remains questionable.Why I see this important now to understand is because solving a problem and creating a new software product are not necessarily constitute the same outcome. Every additional application introduces complexity, dependencies and ongoing responsibilities for testing, security and maintenance. AI is making software dramatically easier and cheaper to create, but it does not automatically eliminate the obligations that come with owning it.The Build-or-Buy ReversalTransaction costs influence whether economic activity is performed internally or purchased from the market. Consider a company needing a simple approval workflow, where using an existing enterprise platform involves procurement, licensing, integration and adapting the business process, while asking an AI agent to build something similar appears easier. AI reduces the transaction costs of producing a custom solution, shifting the traditional build-versus-buy decision towards internal development. But the comparison can be misleading because the cost of generating the application is visible immediately, whereas the cost of running it accumulates over years. A commercial platform distributes development, security and maintenance costs across many customers, while a custom application places those obligations on its owner. Replacing subscriptions isn’t automatically a saving if the organisation must recreate the capabilities it previously purchased.AI may have changed the economics of building software without changing the economics of operating it, which means a cheaper implementation does not necessarily produce a cheaper solution.When Someone Else PaysConsider an employee who creates a dashboard that saves their department several hours each week, using an AI agent instead of waiting for the central engineering team. The immediate return looks excellent, especially when the employee doesn’t directly pay for infrastructure or security reviews. But the application may eventually require database administration, identity integration, monitoring, backups and someone to investigate failures after its creator moves to another role. Economists describe the underlying problem as an externality, where the decision-maker does not bear all the resulting costs. Suppose an organisation accumulates 1,000 independently deployed applications, each costing only $15 per month in infrastructure and two hours of maintenance per quarter. At an illustrative engineering cost of $100 per hour, the combined annual bill approaches $1 million, before incidents and integration costs. These are hypothetical assumptions rather than typical application costs, but the arithmetic illustrates how small obligations accumulate.The individual decision to build can be rational while the collective outcome becomes inefficient, particularly when the benefits are local and the costs are distributed across the organisation.The Rebound of Cheap CodeThink about how many applications would never have existed when development required weeks of engineering effort, a personal expense tracker, another team dashboard, a custom reporting interface or an AI agent handling a task already supported by existing tools. AI makes each of these increasingly affordable, but lowering the cost of production can also increase consumption, a phenomenon related to the rebound effect. Even if the incremental infrastructure cost per application falls, total consumption may rise when enough additional applications are created. The physical consequences depend on their architecture and usage, because a static webpage and a continuously running multi-agent service have very different resource requirements. Still, independent databases, repeated model calls, duplicated storage and persistent services ultimately consume computing capacity, electricity and hardware. The International Energy Agency (IEA) projects global data-centre electricity consumption roughly doubling between 2025 and 2030, driven by AI and broader digital demand, not specifically by vibe coding.The economic question is whether cheaper software creation produces proportionately more useful outcomes, or simply more systems consuming resources that could have been shared.The Physical Cost of DuplicationThe problem becomes more tangible when software demand meets the physical limits of computing. Consider three scenarios, a thousand teams independently deploying dashboards, each with its own always-running service, instead of using a shared platform, hundreds of AI agents repeatedly retrieving the same information and invoking models, rather than sharing retrieval results and reusable services, or departments maintaining separate copies of essentially the same datasets instead of accessing governed common data.Get Yassien Shaalan’s stories in your inboxJoin Medium for free to get updates from this writer.SubscribeSubscribeRemember me for faster sign inIn the first scenario, even an illustrative 10 watts of average additional infrastructure demand per application would amount to roughly 88 MWh annually, while a thousand duplicated 10 GB datasets would require 10 TB of logical storage before replication and backups. These are order-of-magnitude examples, not measured averages, and sharing infrastructure introduces its own trade-offs in isolation, reliability and contention. But the economic direction is clear shared, appropriately scaled services, caching, reusable components and data access can reduce unnecessary resource consumption, while dedicated infrastructure remains justified for workloads requiring isolation or predictable performance. And behind every unnecessary computation are physical resources like electricity, cooling, semiconductors, networking equipment and grid capacity, none of which becomes unlimited because AI makes code generation cheaper.The real efficiency gain is not creating more software with less human effort, but delivering more useful work from the same finite physical resources.Moving the BottleneckImagine an AI agent generating ten internal applications in the time a developer previously needed to build one, with each application appearing functional during a demonstration. The development team has increased its apparent output, but the organisation still needs to verify permissions, test integrations, assess security, understand dependencies and determine who will maintain the applications. This resembles a production bottleneck in economics which is improving one stage of production produces diminishing benefits when another stage cannot keep pace.Research from Google’s DORA programme suggests that AI amplifies existing engineering capabilities rather than automatically improving the entire delivery system.Using another AI model to review the generated code may increase evaluation throughput, but agreement between models cannot establish correctness, especially when they share assumptions or fail to test the same conditions. Security, verification and operational expertise therefore become increasingly important complementary resources.We may make code generation dramatically cheaper while increasing the total demand for engineering judgement, because every additional system introduces behaviour that somebody must verify and eventually support.Experiments Are Not ProductsA student rebuilding a familiar application to understand databases, authentication and user interfaces is making a very different economic decision from a company deploying that application to thousands of customers. The student may gain valuable knowledge even if the software is abandoned, while the company takes on continuing obligations and potential liability. Real-options theory helps explain why cheap experimentation is valuable while an inexpensive prototype allows us to acquire information before making a larger, harder-to-reverse investment.AI increases the number of ideas we can test, which should be encouraged, but it doesn’t mean every successful prototype deserves to become a production system.Research on AI-assisted programming also raises questions about whether completing an implementation produces equivalent understanding, particularly when the user delegates the reasoning rather than learning from it. The distinction I want to make here is between the value of experimentation and the value of ownership.A prototype can be economically successful because it teaches us something, including that the idea is not worth pursuing, while a production application must justify the resources and responsibilities required to keep it alive.The Value of SharingConsider authentication, payment processing or database infrastructure, where building a basic implementation is relatively straightforward but maintaining a secure, reliable service requires years of accumulated engineering knowledge. Products such as Stripe and established identity platforms allow many applications to share that investment rather than reproduce it independently, creating economies of scale and reducing duplicated effort. Open-source software operates through a similar economic arrangement, where thousands of organisations benefit from common libraries maintained by comparatively small communities. Recent economic research has raised the possibility that AI-generated software could weaken those communities if more users consume their work indirectly without contributing engagement or financial support, although this remains a conditional research finding rather than an established outcome. Reuse isn’t always preferable, because vendor lock-in, monopoly pricing, privacy requirements and concentrated failures can justify alternatives. But there is a difference between creating a competing implementation that delivers genuine value and rebuilding mature functionality simply because an AI agent makes it easy.The economic benefit of shared software is not just less code to write, but the ability to distribute the cost of reliability across everyone who depends on it.ConclusionImagine two organisations adopting AI coding tools. One allows every department to independently build applications, databases and integrations, while the other encourages the same experimentation but prioritises reusable capabilities, shared infrastructure and clear ownership before deployment. Both benefit from cheaper software creation, but the second is more likely to capture economies of scale while avoiding duplicated maintenance, security and infrastructure costs. The same question applies to individuals, who can now build their own expense trackers, personal assistants or productivity tools instead of using existing products. While the freedom to customise is valuable, every independently maintained application introduces its own costs, dependencies and responsibilities, even when the initial development costs almost nothing.The objective isn’t to discourage innovation, personal experimentation or force everyone onto centralised platforms, but to recognise that the economic value of software comes from what it delivers over its lifetime, not how cheaply it was created. Whether an organisation replaces a commercial platform or an individual builds a personal alternative, the decision should account for genuine benefits, opportunity costs, existing alternatives, infrastructure, security and ongoing maintenance. Not every application needs enterprise-grade governance, but even a personal tool should justify the time and resources required to keep it useful.For decades, the cost of implementation naturally constrained how much software we built. AI is removing that constraint, but not the economic consequences of unnecessary duplication. We should make software creation accessible to everyone, without assuming every problem deserves its own independently maintained application. When code becomes abundant, the real advantage may lie not in building more, but in knowing what to build, what to reuse and what doesn’t need to exist.#ArtificialIntelligence #VibeCoding #SoftwareEngineering #Economics #AIEngineering'
