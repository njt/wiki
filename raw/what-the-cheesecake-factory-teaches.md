These days everyone is talking about “software factories.”

If you’re not up on this hot new term, don’t worry. It’s basically what “AI agents” was a year ago, rapidly shifting from “no one really knows what it means” to “we all disagree on what it means.”

However, before we speak of AI, let us begin with another kind of factory, one which holds a distinctly different (but no less consequential) place in society: *The Cheesecake Factory*.

Have you ever been to one? If not, let me tell you about one of its celebrated virtues, an utterly enormous menu. Over 20 pages. 250 items.

This phenomenon isn’t limited to The Factory (for example, it’s also endemic to the classic American diner), and it always struck me as kind of nuts. Why is this a good business model and/or customer experience?

Even amongst the most decisive of people, choosing an item from a dinner menu is a classic social challenge. Every group dinner has at least one person who is still staring at the menu with a look of confusion and mild panic when the server comes back.

Social science research holds that human beings struggle more to make decisions as the number of choices increases. (Psychologists call this “choice overload.”) Yet for some reason The Factory chooses to hand each customer that walks in the door the hospitality industry’s equivalent of *War and Peace*.

Why do they do this?

## It matters who picks up the tab

It’s actually quite rational to have a massive menu if you stop to think about (1) how much it really costs, (2) who pays those costs, and (3) what the perceived value is.

Let’s consider the costs. It’s certainly not the breadth of ingredients; look closely at the menu, and you’ll see that there’s a huge amount of overlap in the dishes. It’s the same playbook run by many takeout places (Chinese, Mexican, Italian): a small number of common ingredients remixed into menu items of nearly infinite variety. Combinatorics is doing the heavy lifting here.

Another cost is complexity, and admittedly, cooking and serving off a 250-item menu is more challenging than a 25-item one. However, a kitchen (especially one that fancies itself a “factory”) can get very good at this with practice. Chain restaurants are built on exactly this kind of process discipline.

On the other side of the ledger from these (fairly modest) costs is the value. First off, a huge menu just *looks* valuable. I mean, look at all the choices!

In addition, there’s a lot of market benefit to being the “default option.” Choosing a restaurant for a group is a brutal and thankless endeavor. Being the safe choice that won’t impress anyone but also won’t upset anyone is a completely sensible way to try to position yourself in the market.

So it would seem the value outweighs the costs. But, wait! We’ve forgotten one: *the cost of choosing off a 250-item menu*.

This cost is real, and more importantly, it’s not The Factory that shoulders it. It’s the customer, and often we don’t even realize we’re being forced to pay it.

## Why do we let them do this to us?

Clearly this is an unfair arrangement, but I can think of a few reasons we allow it to continue.

First, people often view more choices as universally beneficial. That is the way *Homo economicus* (the ruthlessly rational model of human decision making that traditional economics is based on) thinks. If you have more options, that increases the space of possible outcomes, which can only lead to a more optimal optimum.

Modern behavioral economics points out that real people don’t actually work like this. Instead, we are often overwhelmed by too many choices and struggle to make optimal decisions. These limitations cause us to employ decision-making “heuristics,” which is a fancy way of saying that we cheat.

If you ever find yourself in a state of analysis paralysis when confronting The Factory’s encyclopedic menu, here are the typical moves: *shrink the menu* (“I’m only looking at the burgers”), *choose a default* (“I’ll have the usual”), or *follow the crowd* (“I’ll have what she’s having”).

But perhaps the biggest reason we haven’t taken up arms against The Factory is that ultimately it’s not that big of a deal. It’s just dinner.

There’s not some optimal choice on The Factory menu that will unlock the secrets of success. It’s unlikely that an order of fried macaroni and cheese balls will radically alter the trajectory of my life (except perhaps to make it marginally shorter).

## Back to software factories

If you think The Cheesecake Factory has a huge menu, it’s nothing compared to the one that AI coding agents have handed to software.

It’s the same playbook, too. Coding agents have taken a small set of primitives (generating text + writing code + search + computer use in place of pasta + bread + cheese + fryer) and combined them in nearly limitless ways to address an incredible breadth of use cases.

And although all those combinations lead to massive complexity, the “kitchen” has gotten very good at handling it. That’s what a software factory really is: coding agents, set up with the right environments and development process to cook just about anything off an enormous menu, reliably and at scale.

Which brings us to the AI world’s version of “look at all the choices!” Spend any time on X or at an AI conference and you’ll hear the same rallying cry: *be more ambitious*. You can build anything now, so we should all think bigger.

That’s not wrong. But remember who picks up the tab. A software factory can make the cooking cheap, but the cost of choosing still lands on the customer, and in this case the customer is your organization.

## The prix fixe trap

Actually, the problem is tougher than it sounds, because organizations are even worse at making choices than restaurant-goers. Dealing with an abundance of options isn’t a muscle your typical business gets to flex very often, because it generally doesn’t have a lot to choose from.

Think about how a software company builds its roadmap. It isn’t choosing from an infinite menu. It’s choosing from a very short one, curated by its current customers, its perceived TAM, its tech stack, its budget, the people it hired and what they know how to do, and (perhaps most importantly) an intensely habituated sense of purpose, a.k.a. “this is what we do here.”

In other words, most organizations have spent their whole lives ordering off a prix fixe menu. There are a few courses with a couple of options each, all chosen in advance by someone else (in this case, their constraints).

For most companies that’s been a good thing, because a short menu is what makes focus possible. (The entire story of disruption is a new entrant taking advantage of the blind spots this habituation creates.)

Now the constraints are coming off. Small teams can take on work that used to need big ones, and expertise you never hired for is suddenly within reach. Someone just swapped the prix fixe one-pager for The Cheesecake Factory tome, and most companies have never had to practice ordering off one before.

So they cope exactly the way the diners do: *shrink the menu* (use AI for the things we already do), *choose a default* (the same roadmap, but faster!), and *follow the crowd* (e.g. another in-app chatbot).

From the outside, all of this looks remarkably like a “lack of ambition.” It’s really an organization that’s never had to learn how to choose from an infinite menu.

## Learning to love the big menu

At The Factory, the stakes are low. It’s just dinner, and if you order wrong, you can always come back next week.

For a company, it’s not just dinner. Every dish you don’t order is an opportunity left on the table. Someone else in the market will order it, quite possibly a new entrant who never had the baggage of living in a prix fixe mindset.

*Be more ambitious* is only half an answer. The real skill is choosing ambitiously *and* decisively. Ambitious means reading the whole menu instead of defaulting to the usual. Decisive means committing once you have. The new failure mode isn’t just ordering something boring, it’s also ordering one of everything and never finishing anything.

There’s a catch, though. Unlike at The Factory, nobody has actually handed you a menu. The space of things your company could build now exists, but it hasn’t been written down anywhere. Just figuring out what’s on it (let alone what’s worth ordering) is real work, and that work has to be somebody’s job. I’m willing to bet that at your organization, it isn’t anyone’s.

Here’s a manageable place to start. As you make your plans for 2027, go back to when you made your 2026 plan and look at some of the things you “took off the menu”: features you shelved, bets you never staffed, opportunities you decided were too big a reach for you. Then re-price each one at today’s costs, and I expect many of them will look like very different options than they did a year ago.

Everyone’s talking about building software factories, and they should be. But a software factory is just an efficient kitchen, and pretty soon everyone will have a great one. The big winners will be the ones who know what to order.
