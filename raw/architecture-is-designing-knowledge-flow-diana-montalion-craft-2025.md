Architecture Is Designing Knowledge Flow — Diana Montalion, Craft 2025

1. Key Points: Your Microservices Are as Coupled as Your Minds

Software encodes its builders' mental models. Three months after her team's first monolith-to-microservices migration: "our microservices are pretty tightly coupled, but our brains were very tightly coupled." The ideas about what mattered lived in their heads, "and that's what we encoded into the software."

Pirsig's factory is the transformation trap. Tear down the factory but leave the rationality standing, and it builds another factory. Real transformation means changing "the mindset out of which the systems, goals, power structures, rules and culture arise" — all of which are finely tuned for the old world of monthly releases and no personalization.

The iceberg model explains why adding control fails. A production bug is a visible event arising from patterns and structures (test coverage, hiring) arising from mental models ("run fast and hot is the way"). Organizations respond to the event with "more predictability and more control" — reinforcing the very model that produced it.

Two universal failure modes, from 50 years of research. Every human system facing complexity (1) blames — the Beer Game, product blames tech, everyone blames the architects — and (2) acts counterintuitively: Forrester found "the people who know a system are often pushing the change in the wrong direction." Brooks's Law (adding manpower to a late project makes it later) is the canonical tech example.

Blame the system, not the people — except Steve. Entrenched resisters are "a social problem, not an architectural problem." Empathy is necessary but insufficient.

Relationships produce effect. Meadows: you must understand "and." Ackoff: a system "is never the sum of its parts, it's the product of their interaction." The bugs prove it — eventual consistency, caching layers, one service tuned faster until its neighbor can't keep up. And it's social too: "if product hates tech and tech hates product, that architects the system."

No shared purpose, no decisions. Ask every VP "what's our fast package delivery?" (the DDD core domain) and you get a different answer from each — so engineers get asked to build a car boat, "and everybody hates it because nobody wanted a car boat."

Knowledge flow beats knowledge stock. Stock (whiteboard-testable expertise, "five years of JavaScript") makes you efficient; flow — "your ability to transfer knowledge between people and technology in ways that change and shift the system" — makes you effective. With roughly 30% of the room on a different tech stack than a year ago, storage depreciates fast.

Trade opinion for knowledge. "What we're usually asked for is our opinion." Knowledge instead means synthesizing "knowledge, experience and sound judgment into recommendations based on valid reasoning."

Capabilities are architected, not engineered. "Transformation doesn't scale through procedures and pipelines or CI/CD. It scales through capabilities." Capabilities — what a system must do, adapt to, or make sense of — persist across implementations; "implement the new payment system in six months" is a factory request, "reduce cost per financial transaction by 20%" is an architecture problem.

Leverage points are the real work — and nobody will believe you found them. Small change, big system-wide effect. Meadows: "hardly anybody will believe you." Montalion's 18-month rule: first "you're too abstract, Diana," then "yeah, we always knew that."

Source of truth is a verb. "The source of truth is asynchronous. The source of truth is in motion, not in one particular space, one particular moment in time."

3. Tools, Practices, and Methodologies

The iceberg model — systems-thinking diagnostic mapping visible events → patterns/structures → mental models. Use it when a bug hits production: work down the layers instead of applying more predictability and control, which only reinforces the mental model that caused the event.

The Beer Game — Jay Forrester's MIT supply-chain simulation (retailer → distributor → hipster brewery) that collapses into chaos when demand spikes. She says "it's worth googling"; play it to internalize blame as the universal first response to complexity.

The "fast package delivery" question — a core-domain alignment exercise: gather the CEOs and VPs and ask what your equivalent of FedEx's fast package delivery is. If everyone answers differently, "it's impossible to make decisions in the software" — you're headed for a car boat.

Capability reframing — rewrite implementation requests as outcome problems: "implement the new payment system in six months" → "reduce cost per financial transaction by 20%"; "migrate Jenkins to CircleCI" → "reduce developer toil on pipeline maintenance by 50%"; "decouple the front end" → "distribute content." Capabilities (processing transactions, deploying updates, distributing content) are stable; implementations churn.

Leverage point hunting (Donella Meadows) — finding "a place where you can intervene in a system where a small change will actually make a big difference in a wider scope." Her examples: reconcile transactions across contexts (sales software and shipping software not talking) instead of buying a new payment system; make pipelines observable and self-healing instead of migrating CI; structure content for distribution instead of dumping MySQL through an API.

Designing data in motion — treat source of truth as asynchronous and in motion, not "the vault in which information is in." Why: "until we're able to grapple with that, we're going to likely continue to have the same problems that caused us to make the change."

Knowledge vs. opinion response pattern — when asked "what do you think of this?", answer with experiences, information, and learnings rather than verdicts: "here's how I can use what I know to help improve your ability to be effective."

Swarming on decisions — per the doctors study she cites, groups that swarmed on a diagnosis made "significantly better" decisions than analytical solo diagnosis. Use: "let's get together and whiteboard this and see what's going on."

Knowledge stock vs. knowledge flow audit — evaluate choices by whether they improve efficiency (your monorepo fluency) or effectiveness (choices that improve the system as a whole because "you've synthesized information beyond your one context, beyond what is your cat bed").

The 18-month rule — a patience practice: expect rejection ("you're too abstract"), then retroactive consensus ("we always knew that") roughly 18 months later. Don't expect credit — the point is the system generating the outcome "rather than me personally and my personal knowledge stock."

Growth mindset, extended — move from strengthening what you know to "helping other people develop new capacities." Necessary because as complexity increases, "you can't possibly know everything" — you need others' knowledge to decide well in your context.

"Fire Steve" — treat entrenched blockers as a social problem, not an architectural one. She claims there are "lots of ways" to keep change from being blocked by Steve; none named on stage.

Prior art for the mindset — Ted Nelson's hypermedia and "intertwingled" (1960s): the Internet's founders intended information to be shared across boundaries. Useful conceptual grounding for flow rather than vaults.

Architecture repository — teased as her concrete example of building knowledge flow; cut for time: "we will have to hold that for next time."

4. Unanswered Questions and Omissions

No method for finding leverage points. The talk's centerpiece activity has no technique attached. Her Q&A answer: "you need experience... you got to put a system out in the wild and see what happens." That's a prerequisite, not a process — and it offers no way to distinguish a real leverage point from a plausible wrong one before the 18 months are up.

The only concrete build got cut. The architecture repository example — the one worked demonstration of constructing knowledge flow rather than diagnosing its absence — was deferred. The talk critiques brilliantly but never shows a construction.

"Fire Steve" vs. "blame the system, not the people" is unresolved. When is a person the problem and when the system? She says there are "lots of ways" to unblock Steve without firing him and names zero of them.

How do you resolve the fast-package-delivery standoff? She describes the failure — every VP a different arrow, none hitting the target — but not the process for converging, especially when the conflicting VPs outrank the architect asking the question.

Power is mostly absent. Mental-model change threatens whoever's status depends on the old model. Beyond the Steve joke, the talk never addresses what to do when leadership itself is the factory — the same leadership that "wants me to make a Gantt chart."

Knowledge flow has no measurement. Stock has crude proxies (whiteboard tests, years-of-experience listings); flow gets a definition but no metric, leaving the talk's central concept unverifiable in practice.

The swarming study is uncited and vague. "This particular doctor whose name I can't pronounce" — no citation, no detail on what swarming looks like for an engineering team rather than a medical diagnosis.

The counterargument to capability reframing is skipped. Sometimes "implement the new payment system in six months" is the right ask — deadlines and constraints exist. And "reduce cost per financial transaction by 20%" still requires someone to deliver; the reframing could read as architects dodging accountability, a reading she never engages.

The 18-month survival problem. How does the disbelieved architect stay credible, funded, and employed while everyone thinks she's wrong? Her answer is "that's okay" — but organizations frequently don't keep the heretic around that long, and she offers no tactics for weathering it.

No AI anywhere. A 2025 talk about knowledge moving between people and technology that never mentions generative AI — currently the loudest force reshaping exactly that — is a conspicuous silence.

"It depends" leaves dispositions, not process. She's upfront that every system has different blockers, so the deliverable is mindsets (context, relationships, growth) plus one controversial heuristic — not a repeatable method for the person in the doorway who still has to produce something Monday.

SAFe dismissed in one line ("this is not what I mean today by transformation") without engaging why organizations keep reaching for it — the Gantt-chart stakeholders don't vanish because the doorway person disapproves.

Kubernetes and serverless get quips, not criteria. "We need Kubernetes because we don't have Kubernetes" diagnoses fashion, but she never says when a platform is the right call — and admits she picks snark targets partly because "somebody likes them and I have to change."

Note: the talk functions partly as a trailer — upcoming book, a panel the next day, the repository example deferred — so some of this thinness is by design. Whether the book delivers the missing method is the open question the talk leaves behind.
