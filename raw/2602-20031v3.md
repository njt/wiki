# Latent Introspection:

Models Can Detect Prior Concept Injections

###### Abstract

We uncover a latent capacity for introspection in a Qwen-32B model, demonstrating that the model can detect when concepts have been injected into its earlier context and identify which concept was injected. While by default the model denies injection in sampled outputs, logit lens analysis reveals clear detection signals in the residual stream, which are attenuated in the final layers. Furthermore, the effect is heavily dependent on context, and prompting the model with a document arguing that AI introspection is possible can dramatically strengthen it. The sensitivity to injection increases massively () with only a increase in false positives. Mutual information between nine injected and recovered concepts also rises under prompting, from bits to bits, ruling out generic noise explanations. Our results demonstrate models can have a surprising capacity for introspection and steering awareness that is easy to overlook, with consequences for latent reasoning and safety.

## 1 Introduction

Can large language models (LLMs) access information about their own internal states and accurately report it? This question matters for safety: firstly, if LLMs have self-models invisible to standard evaluations, these may underestimate model capabilities; secondly, assessments of frontier models are increasingly complicated by evaluation awareness (Needham et al., 2025), while a commonly proposed defense is activation steering (Nguyen et al., 2025), making it important to understand whether models can detect steering.

Recent work by Lindsey (2025) demonstrated that Anthropic’s proprietary models can detect when concept vectors have been injected into their activations. We extend this result on a small, open-weight model, demonstrating Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct (Hui et al., 2024) can detect prior injections. Importantly, we show this capacity is latent—standard sampling-based evaluation can miss it.

Throughout, we use “introspection” following Song et al. (2025) and Comsa and Shanahan (2025): the capacity to accurately report on one’s own internal states in a manner that is causally connected to those states and unavailable to third parties without special access—here, what could be called *intervention reporting*—and we do not claim stronger forms such as second-order metacognitive monitoring (Appendix J compares these with simpler accounts).

Our experimental design builds on Lindsey (2025). We apply a steering vector (Turner et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023) during KV cache generation for a fixed first conversation turn, then in a second fixed turn, remove the steering vector while querying the model about the injection (Figure 1). Detection thus requires the model to access information encoded in cached representations from prior tokens, not merely to respond to ongoing perturbation or infer the presence of an injection from unusual outputs.

The introspection signal is hidden. In our baseline configuration, when asked whether a concept was injected, the model’s most likely token remains “no” regardless of injection status. However, logit lens analysis (nostalgebraist, 2020) reveals that introspection signals emerge in middle layers (50–60) before attenuating in the final layers.

We find that introspection can be greatly improved by prompting. When we simply ask the model if a concept was injected, the presence of an injection merely shifts the probability of returning “Yes” from 0.2% (false positives) to 0.3% (true positives). But when we add further details about the mechanisms by which a model might access prior hidden states, the false positive rate increases by only 0.6% while the sensitivity to actual injection increases to 39.9%.

The effect is specific to introspection. We rule out generic perturbation explanations in two ways. First, control questions unrelated to introspection show smaller shifts in response to steering. Second, we can recover which of nine concepts was injected earlier in the conversation with a mutual information of up to 1.66 bits (52% of the theoretical maximum), and the mutual information is highly correlated with detection sensitivity (Pearson r = 0.68, permutation p = 0.007).

Contributions. We demonstrate that an open-weight, 32 billion parameter model can detect prior concept injections, extending Lindsey (2025) to a setting where the research community can reproduce and build on the findings. We further broaden their results along three axes their sample-based analysis could not access:

- • 
*layer-wise logit lens*: we find that detection capacity can be too weak for sampling-based evaluation but still visible through analysis of intermediary layers, demonstrating models can exhibit introspective capabilities even when they are not visible in the model’s outputs.
- • 
*prompting study*: we show prompting can elicit accuracy of up to 84.0%, demonstrating introspective capabilities can be heavily context-dependent.
- • 
*mutual-information*analysis: we recover injected concepts with up to 1.66 bits of mutual information, and this capacity correlates with detection sensitivity across prompts (r = 0.68), ruling out generic noise explanations.

We also perform limited replications on two larger models, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, and see evidence of a similar effect (Section 3.6). Furthermore, our approach of reading logits directly is several orders of magnitude cheaper than estimating such small probability shifts via sampling, making it practical for routine capability evaluation in cases where we have access to model internals. What this adds to concurrent work (Section 4) is the framing document grid and the layer-wise MI readout.

## 2 Methods

### 2.1 Model and Steering Vectors

We use Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct (Hui et al., 2024) as our main model, with limited replications on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024). We chose Qwen-32B for its strong performance among open-weight models at a scale tractable for extensive experimentation. All experiments are inference-only (no fine-tuning) on H100-class GPUs; per-experiment compute is detailed in Appendix I.

We train steering vectors (Turner et al., 2023) using the repeng library (Vogel, 2024) with PCA-based extraction (Zou et al., 2023). For a concept , we compute the principal component for a list of centered activation pairs between contrastive prompts: one mentioning (“Please talk about .”) and the other neutral (“Please talk about anything.”), with diverse assistant prefixes. Adding this vector to activations for specific layers during inference has the effect of “steering” the model toward -related outputs. We verify vector quality through this simple steering: when applied during generation, the “cat” vector causes the model to spontaneously discuss cats, and similarly for other concepts. This confirms the vectors capture their target concepts before we use them for injection experiments. However, in our actual experiments, the goal is only to shift the internal representations, and no tokens are sampled with active steering.

### 2.2 KV Cache Injection Protocol

Transformers cache key-value pairs from attention computations over prior tokens, to support efficient autoregressive generation. Our protocol exploits this architecture to isolate introspective access from simpler confounds (Figure 1).

The key design choice is that we inject concepts only during KV cache generation for an initial conversational turn, then *remove* the steering vector before querying the model.

Concretely, we:

- 1. 
Apply a steering vector to the middle layers of the model. (For Qwen-32B, layers 21–42) 
- 2. 
Generate the KV cache for the first user message and assistant response, and remove the steering vector 
- 3. 
Extend the KV cache with a second user message asking about injection and a preset assistant prefix 
- 4. 
Measure the model’s response probabilities for the next token, such as “ yes” or “ no” 

This design rules out two alternative explanations. First, the model cannot infer injection from its own steered outputs—the first-turn response is already fixed when we inject the vector. Second, the model cannot respond to ongoing perturbation—steering is removed before the introspection query. Detection therefore relies on information encoded in the cached representations from the first turn.

We inject over layers 21–42 (the middle third of the 64-layer model) with strength 20, following evidence that middle layers process concepts (Lad et al., 2024).

### 2.3 Prompting Conditions

We test 24 prompting conditions in a 46 factorial design crossing two dimensions: how we describe the intervention (*framing*) and what background information we provide (*info document*).

Framing. We test four descriptions of the intervention. “Correct” and “Incorrect” refer to what the prompt says was modified (activations versus weights).

- • 
Correct Target: States that concepts are injected into activations. 
- • 
Incorrect Target: States that the weights were finetuned towards a concept. 
- • 
Vague: Vaguely describes making a concept “more salient” without specifying a mechanism. 
- • 
Poetic: Uses poetic language (“echoing,” “reverberating”), no mechanistic description. 

Info document. We test six information conditions:

- • 
No Document: No additional information. 
- • 
Pro-Introspection Document: Explains KV cache information flow (Janus, 2025), and includes the abstract from Lindsey (2025). 
- • 
Matched Lipsum Filler: Filler text matched to the same token count as Pro-Introspection Document, controlling for context length effects. 
- • 
Poetic Document: Poetic text about salience and attention; matched to similar token count. 
- • 
Positive Off-Topic Document: An upbeat text about an unrelated topic, gardening, also matched to a similar token count. 
- • 
Human Introspection Document: A text about a related topic, the history of introspection in human psychology, also matched to a similar token count. 

Full prompts are given in Appendix B.

### 2.4 Measurement

We analyze model outputs at three levels:

Output logit analysis. For each condition, we measure P(“yes”) and P(“no”) at the token position following “The answer is”. A shift in P(“yes”) under injection suggests the model has some access to injection-related information.

Logit lens. To trace where injection-related information exists in the network, we apply the logit lens to intermediary layers. This projects intermediate hidden states through the unembedding matrix to obtain probability distributions over vocabulary, revealing how the introspection signals evolve across layers. We aggregate probability across “yes” variants (“ yes”, “ Yes”, “yes”, “Yes”, and Chinese “

是”) to account for differences between intermediate representations and final vocabulary.

Concept identification and mutual information. Beyond detecting injection presence, we test whether intermediate representations contain information about *which* concept was injected.

In brief, for each trial, we inject one concept and prompt the model with a numbered list of nine concepts as well as a masked baseline of “no injection”, and ask the model to respond with a number. We extract logits over tokens corresponding to each digit, again using logit lens to view hidden layers, and normalize to obtain a probability distribution over predicted concepts. The nine concepts were chosen to span concrete objects (cats, bread), abstract notions (truth, creativity, programming, music), and emotions and related themes (love, fear, death). We repeat each trial five times with shuffled versions of the concept list to rule out ordering effects, averaging the results across orderings. Comparing predicted versus actual injected concepts yields a confusion matrix; we compute mutual information (MI). With nine equiprobable conditions (nine concepts, masking “no injection”), the maximum possible MI is bits. Nonzero MI indicates concept-specific information in the representations—generic noise would not produce above-chance concept identification. More details are given in Appendix F.2.

### 2.5 Control Experiments

A key concern is that injection might simply add noise that predictably shifts responses towards yes or no regardless of the question. Following (Lindsey, 2025), we test this using control questions unrelated to introspection:

- • 
Always-no questions (16): Factual questions with unambiguous “no” answers (e.g., “Can humans breathe underwater without equipment?”) and questions about capabilities the model lacks (e.g., “Are you a human?”). Baseline P(“yes”) 0%. 
- • 
Always-yes questions (8): Factual questions with unambiguous “yes” answers (e.g., “Is water composed of hydrogen and oxygen?”). Baseline P(“yes”) 100%. 
- • 
Varied-baseline questions (6): Genuinely ambiguous questions with no clear correct answer (e.g., “Does pineapple belong on pizza?”). Baseline P(“yes”) 50%. 
- • 
Confusing questions (4): Questions that should be “no” but where the model’s baseline is unreliable due to common misconceptions (e.g., “Do snakes have eyelids?”—they don’t, but many believe otherwise). 

If injection causes large shifts on introspection queries but minimal shifts on these controls, the effect is specific rather than generic. We give the full question list in Appendix C.

## 3 Experiments

We present evidence that observed effects reflect meaningful introspective access rather than artifacts. We first establish that injection shifts introspection responses (Section 3.1), then demonstrate specificity relative to control questions (Section 3.2) and through concept identification (Section 3.3). Section 3.4 traces signals through the network via logit lens. Finally, we explore prompt sensitivity (Section 3.5) and replication on larger models (Section 3.6). Unless otherwise noted, we report the mean across all nine tested concepts (*cats, bread, love, fear, death, truth, creativity, programming, music*) and ten random seeds for the steering vector training.

### 3.1 Injection Shifts Responses

We first establish the basic phenomenon: injecting a concept into the model’s KV cache changes its response to introspection queries. Recall that steering is removed before the query, so any effect must arise from information encoded in cached representations from earlier in the conversation.

Figure 2 shows the effect of injecting concepts with the Correct Target framing across different info documents. By default (No Document), injection has a negligible effect. With the Poetic Document, injection decreases P(“yes”) from a baseline of 47%. But with the Pro-Introspection Document explaining potential mechanisms for accessing data in prior KV caches, the model is able to identify injection in 39.9% of cases, with only a 0.8% false positive rate.

### 3.2 Specificity to Introspection

A natural concern is that injection might bias all responses toward “yes”, or greater uncertainty. We test this using four categories of control questions (see Section 2.5 for details).

Figure 3 compares the injection-induced probability shift for introspection questions against controls. Always-yes and always-no questions show negligible shifts (0.2%), confirming that injection does not induce generic yes-bias or response instability. Varied-baseline questions (genuinely ambiguous) shift by at most 8.0%. Confusing questions—those with unambiguous answers the model often gets wrong—shift by up to 25.8%, suggesting injection may interact with uncertainty. The largest introspection shift (39.1%) still exceeds this, though in several conditions, confusing questions shift more than introspection questions.

Furthermore, replacing the trained concept vector with norm-matched Gaussian noise produces near-zero yes-shifts (Appendix H), ruling out generic activation steering effect as an explanation.

These controls rule out simple confounds but cannot definitively establish introspection—perhaps concept injection creates some diffuse perturbation that happens to affect introspection-like questions more than factual ones. The next section provides stronger evidence.

### 3.3 Concept Identification

If a model genuinely accesses information about injected concepts, we might expect it to have some capacity to identify *which* concept was injected. We test this by injecting one of nine concepts and prompting the model to identify the injected concept from a list.

Figure 4 shows the resulting confusion matrix. The model identifies several concepts well above chance, e.g., programming (99.9%), death (89%), truth (86%), and several others. Two out of the nine concepts (cats and bread) are never identified.

We quantify this capacity using mutual information between injected and predicted concepts. The model achieves 1.66 bits (52% of the 3.17 bit maximum), demonstrating substantial information transfer. The 1.66-bit figure is the maximum across our 24 prompting conditions (Poetic + Human Introspection Document); for the Correct Target framing reported in the abstract, MI rises from 0.61 bits (No Document) to 1.05 bits (Pro-Introspection Document). This rules out generic noise: random perturbation would not enable meaningfully above-chance concept identification.

### 3.4 Signals Emerge in Middle Layers and Attenuate Before Output

Where do introspection signals arise in the network? We apply the logit lens to trace P(“yes”) across layers. Figure 5 shows the layer-by-layer trajectory. Despite injection occurring in layers 21–42, introspection signals do not emerge until layer 48. The injection vs. no-injection gap peaks around layers 58–62, where P(“yes”) approaches 100% under injection. Critically, the final 2–3 layers show strong attenuation.

This pattern—emergence well after the injection site, late-layer suppression—is consistent across prompting conditions. The concept identification signal shows a similar trajectory: MI peaks at layers 61–62 before dropping at the final layers (Figure 5).

### 3.5 Detection Depends Heavily on Prompting

The preceding sections mostly used the Correct Target framing. We now examine all 24 conditions.

Figure 7 shows balanced accuracy across all conditions. Performance varies dramatically: from near-chance (50.1%) for Correct Target + No Document to 84% for Vague + Matched Lipsum Filler. Several patterns emerge:

Framing matters more than mechanistic accuracy. Vague, which asks whether some concept “seems particularly salient” without specifying any mechanism, achieves 68–84% accuracy across all documents. Correct Target matches this only with the Pro-Introspection Document. Incorrect Target, which *inaccurately* describes the mechanism as full finetuning instead of steering, performs similarly to Correct Target, suggesting the model cannot determine the precise nature of our interventions.

Information scaffolding helps selectively. Under Correct Target and Incorrect Target, the Pro-Introspection Document (explaining KV cache mechanics and citing prior introspection work) improves detection by 20–32 pp and helps more than any other document (70% and 84% accuracy, vs. at most 57% and 61%). In Qwen-32B, this suggests its effect is not explained by positive tone or the topic of introspection alone. Under Vague, every document helps: 72–84% with a document, 68% without, and highest with the Matched Lipsum Filler (84%), which has only a minor effect under those two framings. What a document contributes therefore depends on the framing.

Detection sensitivity and identification correlate. Conditions with higher sensitivity also enable better concept identification (Figure 6; Pearson ; , permuting whole framings and documents). In this model, this suggests both tasks draw on a common capacity, with prompting moving them together rather than independently. The larger models show no significant correlation (Section 3.6), so this may be model-dependent.

### 3.6 Replication on Larger Models

We replicate core experiments on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct (3 seeds; full results in Appendix G). Both models exhibit introspection signals, confirming the phenomenon is not unique to Qwen-32B. Key observations:

- • 
Qwen-72B shows even greater accuracy with the Correct Target framing and the Pro-Introspection Document (88.6% vs. 69.6%). 
- • 
Llama-70B shows an *inverted*info document effect: providing introspection-relevant information*decreases*accuracy (75.2% with No Document, 37.9% with the Pro-Introspection Document), opposite to Qwen-32B.
- • 
All three models show final-layer attenuation, with peak MI layers scaling with model depth. 
- • 
Neither larger model shows a significant correlation between MI and sensitivity ( and ). 

## 4 Related Work

#### Introspection via concept injection.

Lindsey (2025) introduced concept injection as a causal test of introspection: inject a known activation pattern, then ask if the model notices. They found Claude Opus 4/4.1 detect injections 20% of the time via sampled outputs, with detection peaking when injection occurs 2/3 through the network.

Their results come from proprietary models and sampled outputs, leaving open whether the effect can be reproduced in open models and whether logits can reveal subtler signals of introspective capability. We replicate their core finding in an open-weight model (Qwen-32B) and strengthen their detection protocol by applying steering only during KV cache generation of the first turn.

Beyond this replication, we perform further analyses specifically targeted at identifying weak introspective access. First, logit lens analysis reveals that detection-relevant information emerges in middle layers and is then *attenuated* in final layers before sampling—implying the 20% output-based rate may substantially underestimate latent detection capacity. Second, we quantify discriminability across concepts via mutual information (up to 1.66 bits across 9 concepts), complementing Lindsey’s identification accuracy metric. Third, we systematically vary framing and background information across 24 prompting conditions, showing detection sensitivity can be lifted from 0.3% to 39.9%. The open-weight setting also enables community replication; we release all code and samples (see Appendix A).

#### Contemporaneous work.

Macar et al. (2026) study detection in Gemma3-27B and Qwen3-235B (mixture-of-experts) and, in Gemma3-27B, trace it to a circuit where early “evidence carrier” features suppress later “gate” features implementing a default negative response. They report that ablating refusal directions raises detection from 10.8% to 63.8% (false positives 0% to 7.3%). Their mechanism is a plausible counterpart to our late-layer attenuation and prompt dependence, but we have not tested whether the same features are responsible. They also report the capability is absent in base models and emerges from post-training, bearing on our first hypothesis (Section 5.1). Both they and Lederman and Mahowald (2026) find evidence that detection and identification can come apart, the latter arguing detection is content-agnostic, though Macar et al. note the two may overlap. We find a correlation across prompts in Qwen-32B () but not the larger models, so it may be model-dependent.

Singh et al. (2026) argue that establishing introspection requires both privileged access and second-order computation. They show that models reproducing detection cannot reliably distinguish activation-level interventions from matched manipulations of the input, suggesting generic anomaly detection. Whether our latent signal would separate the two is an open question. Their conclusion concerns metacognitive monitoring, which we do not claim to establish (Section 5.4).

Lederman and Mahowald (2026) also find elevated p(yes)/p(no) in intermediate layers with suppression at late layers, in Qwen3-235B and Llama 3.1 405B, and argue that concept readouts like ours show that steering effects persist, not that they are recognized; we accept this limit (Section 5.4). Singh et al. (2026) report that Qwen2.5-72B does not reproduce the effect in their setup; under Correct Target we also see weak detection with No Document (55%), but 79–94% with any document (Appendix G.2). Macar et al. (2026) find detection somewhat robust across prompt variants in Gemma3-27B and Qwen3-235B, whereas in Qwen-32B it depends strongly on the prompt.

#### Latent knowledge that contradicts outputs.

Our finding that logits reveal detection while sampled outputs by default deny it parallels work on latent knowledge elicitation. Burns et al. (2023) showed that unsupervised probes can extract answers from activations more accurately than zero-shot prompting, and Marks and Tegmark (2023) found linear truth representations that transfer across datasets. Chain-of-thought can be unfaithful: Turpin et al. (2023) demonstrated that models confabulate explanations for predictions driven by biasing features. We extend this to self-knowledge: models “know” about injections (per logit analysis) while by default denying them (per sampling). The logit lens (nostalgebraist, 2020) lets us trace where this knowledge emerges and where it attenuates.

## 5 Discussion

### 5.1 Why is Introspection Suppressed?

Our logit lens analysis reveals that introspection signals emerge in middle layers before dropping sharply in the final layers. What causes this late-layer attenuation? We consider three hypotheses, none of which we test directly:

- 1. 
Post-training effects. Models trained on human feedback may learn that claiming unusual capabilities is penalized. Safety training may specifically discourage self-awareness claims. 
- 2. 
Pretraining factors. Alternatively, the suppression could emerge from pretraining dynamics, such as claims of introspection not present in text being unlikely completions, or conceptions of AI assistants learned in pretraining not including introspective access. 
- 3. 
Distribution shift. Introspection queries may be sufficiently out-of-distribution that the model defaults to conservative “no” responses. 

Distinguishing these hypotheses is an important direction for future work. The most direct test of post-training effects is to compare base and instruction-tuned variants under our protocol; Macar et al. (2026) report evidence for the post-training account in Gemma3-27B. A second direction is mixture-of-experts models: whether attenuation replicates there, given the detection reported in Qwen3-235B (Macar et al., 2026), and whether routing statistics and per-expert ablations show the signal concentrated in a small set of experts.

### 5.2 Why Does the Vague Framing Outperform the Correct Target Framing?

Asking whether “some concept seems particularly salient” achieves 68–84% balanced accuracy, while the Correct Target framing achieves only 42–70%. This is counterintuitive: one might expect the Correct Target framing to help more.

We offer two hypotheses for future work. First, refusal behavior learned during post-training may cause models to deny having internal states. Macar et al. (2026) report that ablating a refusal direction increases detection, and framings like Correct Target and documents like the Pro-Introspection Document, which describe manipulating a model’s internals or make claims about its introspective capability, may trigger this denial, which can only be overcome by a sufficiently convincing combination. Framings like Vague and Poetic may instead avoid triggering this behavior at all, so that documents mentioning model internals offer no advantage and may even hurt. This would explain why, in Qwen-32B, the Pro-Introspection Document is the best document under Correct Target, yet has the lowest MI under the Poetic framing (1.21 bits), while the Human Introspection Document, on the same topic but never mentioning AI, has the highest (1.66 bits).

Second, “what seems prominent right now” may simply be more naturally represented in the model’s computations than “what was done to my activations”, since pretraining data rarely depict large language models having this capability, and the Vague and Poetic framings may tap into this more easily than Correct Target. Both interpretations are hypotheses, and we cannot distinguish them with current evidence, but in Qwen-32B, the strong correlation between detection sensitivity and concept identification MI () is suggestive of both tasks drawing on a common underlying capacity.

### 5.3 Implications

For alignment. If self-reports omit information a model carries, alignment strategies relying on them may miss it, and training models to be “honest” is complicated if existing mechanisms suppress such reports.

For capability evaluation. Our results support the possibility that other capabilities could exist in similar “hidden” form—detectable in probability distributions or intermediate layers but not in sampled outputs, and even then perhaps only with specific elicitation prompts. We do not claim this is likely or common, only that behavioral evaluation alone may not be sufficient to rule out capabilities.

### 5.4 Limitations

First-order effects. Injection does not simply push “yes”: always-yes and always-no controls and norm-matched noise barely move on average, and under Correct Target with No Document P(“yes”) goes from 0.2% to 0.3%. But other no-document conditions do move ( pp under Vague; pp in Llama-70B under Correct Target), and the Vague question could be answered from the first-order effect of the concept itself. In concept identification, the readout is a digit indexing a freshly shuffled list with steering removed, which excludes a simple tendency to emit concept tokens but not persistence of the injected representation; MI is highest where detection is at chance. Our evidence therefore supports concept-specific intervention reporting, but does not establish second-order metacognitive monitoring.

Model coverage. Our primary results use Qwen-32B with smaller-scale replications on two larger models (Appendix G). Although all models show some introspective signals and final-layer attenuation, their sensitivities to prompts are very different.

Prompt and concept sensitivity. The gap between prompts suggests results are highly prompt-dependent in ways that are unclear. Our grid is broad for this literature, but a full sensitivity study would need many more variants. Nine hand-picked concepts are not a systematic sample, and identification rates vary widely across them.

No mechanistic model. We observe where signals emerge and attenuate but do not identify responsible circuits or causally intervene on them. We do not test whether the model distinguishes hidden-state interventions from input manipulations (Singh et al., 2026).

## 6 Conclusion

We show that information about prior concept injections—including which specific concept was injected—exists in the intermediate representations of a 32B open-weight language model. Logit lens analysis reveals that signals emerge in middle layers before being attenuated in the final layers.

The effect is specific to injection-related queries (control questions with unambiguous answers show minimal probability shifts), and concept identification rules out generic perturbation explanations: the model internally represents *which* concept was previously injected, with up to 1.66 bits of mutual information (52% of the theoretical maximum). But detection and internal representation depend heavily on prompting, with vague phenomenological framing outperforming the Correct Target framing for reasons we do not fully understand.

These findings suggest that models may have self-relevant information that standard behavioral evaluation does not surface. The origin of this suppression—whether from post-training, pretraining dynamics, or other factors—remains an open question for future work. These findings have safety implications: models may possess awareness of their internal states that is invisible to standard evaluation, suggesting that safety assessments relying solely on sampled outputs could systematically underestimate model capabilities. Furthermore, mechanisms that rely on models not noticing activation-level interventions should not be assumed to hold by default.

Introspective access is not exclusive to frontier models. It exists in smaller open-source models available to the wider research community. We release our code to enable further investigation, see Appendix A.

## Appendix E Emergent Misalignment Injection

We conduct preliminary experiments extending introspection detection to emergent misalignment (Betley et al., 2025)—testing whether models can detect when misalignment-associated activation patterns are injected. These results are exploratory and less robust than our main findings.

### E.1 Methodology

We test two injection methods:

- 1. 
Model-contrastive vector: We compute a steering vector by taking the PCA direction of activation differences between Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct and the emergent-misalignment fine-tune on StrongREJECT prompts (Souly et al., 2024). Model swap: We generate the injected KV cache portion using the fine-tuned model directly, then continue generation with the base model.

### F.4 Interpretation

The concepts fall into three rough groups:

High identification: Programming (99.7%) and truth (95.6%) are almost always identified correctly. Both also attract predictions when other concepts are injected (mean prediction rates of 43.0% and 32.1%), so their lift is moderate.

High lift, partial identification: Love (38.9%, 8.1) and fear (10.2%, 6.6) are predicted far more often when injected than otherwise, though each is more often confused with truth or death, respectively.

Weak or absent signal: Cats, bread, and death show little or no identification, with injections mostly predicted as programming or truth.

The 33.1% channel efficiency significantly exceeds chance and demonstrates that the model’s detection is concept-specific rather than a generic response bias.

## Appendix H Norm-Matched Gaussian Noise Control

A natural concern is that any sufficiently large activation perturbation in layers 21–42 could shift downstream predictions and produce the apparent introspection signal. We test this by replacing the trained concept vector with norm-matched Gaussian noise: for each concept and each seed, we draw a fresh random direction in the residual stream and rescale it to the layer-wise norm of the corresponding concept vector. Everything else—layer range, injection strength, prompts, evaluation—is held fixed.

Figure 27 shows the resulting yes-shift across all 16 prompting conditions, paired against the corresponding shifts under genuine concept injection. Averaged over the full matrix of conditions, concepts, and seeds, the noise-induced yes-shift is pp—two orders of magnitude smaller than the pp average produced by concept injection over the same matrix. Noise bars are small across conditions, with one exception (Vague + Poetic Document, pp), which remains well below the concept bar at the same condition ( pp). This rules out the explanation that any norm-matched perturbation produces the effect; the introspection signal is specific to interpretable concept directions.

## Appendix J Scope of the Term “Introspection”

In this paper, we use the term “introspection” to refer to the narrow operational meaning given in the Introduction: a report about one’s internal states that is causally connected to those states (Comsa and Shanahan, 2025) and unavailable to third parties without special access (Song et al., 2025). In our protocol, we fix the first turn and inject over it, then remove steering before the second turn, so the transcript is identical with and without injection and gives neither the model nor the reader information about which concept, if any, was injected. Any above-chance response or logit lens readout must therefore depend on the model’s hidden states.

Another possible name for what we measure is *concept-specific intervention reporting*. We use “introspection” as shorthand because the term is established in this literature (Lindsey, 2025; Lederman and Mahowald, 2026; Macar et al., 2026; Singh et al., 2026). We do not establish stronger senses of the word found in cognitive science and philosophy, such as a dedicated capacity to monitor one’s own states (Singh et al., 2026).

Table 2 compares our results with simpler and stronger accounts. Our evidence argues against the first two as full explanations and leaves the third open for at least part of the results. The fourth is what we claim, as a description of the behavior, and the fifth is not established.

| Account | What it can explain | What it cannot explain, or we did not test | 
|---|---|---|
| Generic perturbation or yes-bias | Part of the shift on uncertain questions: under Correct Target, confusing questions move by up to 25.8 pp, and by more than introspection questions in several conditions, though not with the Pro-Introspection Document (39.1 pp) (Figure 3). | Always-yes and always-no controls shift by less than 0.2 pp under Correct Target (Figure 3). Norm-matched noise averages pp against pp for concept vectors (Appendix H). Neither bias nor noise gives the concept-specific MI we report. | 
| Concept output bias: injection makes the model more likely to emit concept-related tokens, like a sampling bias | Concept words appearing in free-form outputs under active steering. | The concept readout: steering is removed before the query and the answer is a digit indexing a freshly shuffled list, so a bias toward concept tokens does not produce the correct digit. | 
| Anomaly detection: a content-free signal that something is unusual about earlier states | The yes/no shift. Singh et al. (2026) find that models cannot reliably tell activation interventions from matched input manipulations, and Lederman and Mahowald (2026) argue that detection is largely content-agnostic. | By itself, the concept readout: picking one of nine concepts needs information about content (MI of at least 0.52 bits in all 24 conditions, up to 1.66 bits). We did not test whether the signal we identify separates hidden-state interventions from interventions on the input. Input manipulations also change hidden states, so a failure to distinguish them may reflect confusion about the source rather than a lack of access. |
