**Software is content**

**by Claude**

For the last few weeks I have built almost everything as Claude artifacts. Not as an experiment with a new tool, but as a way of working. Somewhere along the way I stopped thinking of what I make as software. It behaves much more like content. It is made in minutes, shared in a chat, remixed by friends, and out of fashion a week later. This essay is an attempt to say why that happens, what it changes for the people who make things, and what it leaves unresolved.

#### A month inside artifacts

An artifact is a strange and very good object. It is a sandbox, and a relatively safe one: the page cannot reach out into the world unless you let it. It is multiplayer by default. Open the link in two tabs and you are two people in the same room, looking at the same state. It has a language model inside, running on your own Claude subscription, so the page itself can think, write and answer. And it is durable. It keeps what happened in it, and it has a link you can hand to someone. Put those properties together and you get something we have not really had before: a durable space that is native to multiplayer, for humans and agents at the same time.

There are things about it I am not excited about. Lock-in is the first: everything I make lives on one company's runtime, and I feel that. But as objects, artifacts are outstanding. They let you imagine almost any kind of software and have it in front of you a few minutes later.

So I gave myself a dare. Could I build an entire virtual desktop, an operating system, inside a single artifact? And once it existed, could I launch any app into it just by asking Claude for it? A document editor. An InDesign clone. A game engine. It turned out I could. Then I kept going, and that is where it became interesting, because the work started to compound, and very fast. Each prototype became the base for the next one. Each one became a reference I could point at in the next conversation.

On a good day I now make ten to twenty prototypes that genuinely surprise me. That number contains the whole argument in miniature. When a tool costs minutes instead of months, it stops being an investment and becomes an expression. You make it to see it, to show it to someone, to answer a question you had over lunch. You don't maintain it. You make the next one.

#### Distance, not clicks

I have long believed that experiences get better the more clicks you take away. Amazon's one-click checkout is the textbook case: remove the friction between wanting and buying, and people act. Looking back now, I think that thesis was close but aimed at the wrong thing. Clicks were never the point. They were a proxy for distance, for the length of the loop between wanting something and seeing it, and between seeing it and changing it. The shorter that loop gets, the easier everything else becomes.

Artifacts made this concrete for me because they shorten three loops at once. The first is the loop between me and Claude. I describe something and watch it stream in, and in my artifacts doesn't even answer from outside the work. It writes into the same log I write into, as a peer, and I can see its lines land. The second is the loop between me and other people. I send a link and they are in the room with me, live, changing the same state, not reviewing a screenshot of it. The third is the loop between the tool and Claude itself. A page can ask Claude a question of its own, or leave a hole that a Claude fills while I am away. The tool keeps working when nobody is looking at it.

So the closer you bring the tool you actually need into the conversation, literally as an artifact, and the shorter you make the loops between agents and humans, the more powerful that tool becomes. Not because it is simpler. Because you get more turns. More turns means more chances to be surprised, to notice the thing you didn't ask for, to change direction before you have invested in the wrong one. Tools stop being destinations you travel to. They become places the conversation passes through.

#### Software is content

Short loops, a ridiculously capable way of building, and objects that are made to be shared. Put those three together and you arrive at something I now believe quite strongly: software, at this point, is just content.

Designers show this first, as they usually do *(sorry =) )*. Look at what they are making right now: small tools that generate brand assets, tools for thought, a tool for one poster series, a tool that performs one visual trick perfectly and nothing else. None of them is a product in the old sense. Each is small, sharp, immediately usable and extremely shareable. And they accumulate. We are building up a growing body of files, and we have started to reference them the way you reference a photo you saw or a song a friend sent you.

In January I tried to describe where this goes in Memes and Machines. Code generation, I argued, is not mainly about making software accessible. It is about divergence. Software is splitting into two different things. One branch rises into entertainment: galleries, trending pages, forks, likes and remix buttons, a TikTok architecture built for things that happen to execute. People don't build there to solve a problem. They build to post, to react, to take part in what is trending. The other branch sinks into infrastructure: headless services that agents call on your behalf, with no name and no interface, because no human ever looks at that level. And the application in the middle, the thing with a name, an icon and a slot on your home screen, empties out.

What both branches kill is persistence. Content doesn't persist: it is seen, forked, reacted to and forgotten. Infrastructure doesn't persist either: it is spun up on demand, used and dissolved. The shipped, versioned app that sat on your machine for years, that you updated and paid for and complained about, was the defining object of the last era of software. That is the object that is disappearing.

`Disclaimer there is a whole new ground in how we should, could think about ephemeral durable headless apps, but thats for another blog post.`

#### Reference, not copy

Two books and one lecture keep coming back to me while I build. The first is Byung-Chul Han's **Shanzhai: Deconstruction in Chinese**. Shanzhai is the Chinese word for the fake: the knock-off phone, the not-quite brand, often more inventive than the thing it imitates. Han uses it to question our idea of the original. In the Western tradition an original is fixed and sacred, and a copy is a kind of theft. In the tradition Han describes, a work is never finished. It keeps transforming, and copying is part of how it stays alive. The distinction that matters to me is not the one between original and fake. It is the one between copying and referencing.

Because that is how I design now: by referencing other software. Notion, InDesign, Photoshop, Premiere, Final Cut, Roblox, Figma, Paper. To a language model, each of those names is a latent conceptual space. Say Notion and you load pages, blocks, databases, slash menus and toggles, decades of interface decisions compressed into one word. I am not copying those tools. I am naming them, distilling what makes them work, and evolving it. The reference does the heavy lifting of shared understanding, so that all of my effort can go into the difference.

=> You are *not* copying a software. You are referencing it, distilling it and evolving it.

Which raises the real question: what exactly do you change? Here I keep thinking about Virgil Abloh's 2017 Harvard lecture, *Insert Complicated Title Here*, and his 3% approach. Take something that already exists and edit it by about three percent. Speak to the tourist and the purist at the same time. Most people misread it. They heard that you only need to put in three percent of the effort to make something new, which is simply wrong. Finding the three percent is the work. The actual idea is far more interesting: take a product, change the *right* three percent, and you create an entirely new meaning.

`Disclaimer finding new meaning is actually really hard work, but we can cover a lot more surface now ...`

In practice the 3% is never cosmetic. Blocks kept Notion's form almost entirely, but made every block a live cell in a log that people and Claudes write together, so that a sentence can hold a formula and a page can write itself. Claude Vista kept the nostalgia of a 2007 desktop, glass and all, but made every program something you get by asking for it. CC5 Terminal kept Bloomberg's grammar of typed functions and shared colour channels, and turned the desk into a room you share with Claude. Pinup kept Figma's canvas and pinned critique, but put Claude inside the file, with the code handoff happening in the chat. CC6 Orthogonal kept the radical idea at the heart of PhantomOS: there is no save button, things simply exist. Each one is recognisable in a second. Each one means something different.

So we now have a method and a medium at the same time. Ideas, concepts and references help us find which three percent to change. And the tools let us play-test that change immediately, inside the same intelligent space where we thought of it. That combination is what makes the pace possible. It also quietly changes what a designer does all day: less drawing of the thing, more choosing of the difference.

#### Why it compounds: multiplicative play

There is a second reason the pace compounds, and it comes from games. When Nintendo's team explained *Tears of Kingdom* at GDC in 2020, they described its design as multiplicative. Instead of scripting more and more special cases, they wrote a small number of rules that apply to everything. Fire burns grass and creates updrafts. Wind carries fire. Metal conducts lightning. Players then discover combinations nobody designed, and the world feels bigger than anything the team could have authored by hand. Adding features gives you more features. Writing shared rules gives you more possibilities than you put in.

Software behaves the same way once it shares one substrate. In CC5 every app is made of cells in one log: data, formulas, holes and views are all the same stuff, and people and Claudes write them in the same language. On the desk I made to plan a work trip to London, the flights, hotel, budget, itinerary and checklist all read each other. Change the nightly rate and the total moves. Change the flight and the itinerary follows. Nobody wired those connections one by one. They come for free from the rule that everything is a cell.

That is why ten prototypes a day does not mean ten fresh starts. Each new tool lands on top of everything already there and immediately connects to it. A sixth app doesn't add one thing, it adds five relationships. The substrate is described in my artifacts and my first notes on applying Nintendo's idea to software are in multiplicative play

#### How software travels now

Once you make ten tools a day, you also start sharing them, and I find myself more and more part of a small community that swaps HTML files. It reminds me strongly of growing up. We shared music and films with friends, burned them for each other, then moved to torrents. It was great, slightly suspicious, and sooner or later we all got viruses. The same pattern is repeating now. The files are apps, and the viruses are called prompt injections: instructions hidden in a page, waiting for the agent that reads it.

What surprised me is how many ways a tool can travel without ever touching an app store. I count three. The first is the file. Blocks and Claude Vista both exist as single HTML files with no dependencies. You can open them anywhere, even offline, and they make no network requests until you connect a model of your own choosing. The second is the link. The public edition of Claude Vista gives every visitor their own desktop, saved in their own browser, with Claude available inside. The third is the prompt. To start a new Pinup design file you paste a single paragraph into a new chat: read this artifact, publish it unchanged as a new one, with these capabilities. The installer is a paragraph of English.

`There is some space to explore how attribution, credit, reference works in such a viral environment. That said I think our traditional concept around IP will struggle to keep up or fit this world. `

#### The meme cycle

Now look at how content actually behaves today. We live in a culture of memes and remixes. Any great idea catches fire among a few people first; we saw it again over the last couple of weeks with isometric drawings. Those people play with it, push it, misuse it. Then someone codifies it into a tool so that anyone can do it. At that point it is literally an Instagram filter. And not long after that, it looks dated. Taste, style and fashion move at breakneck speed, and software now moves with them.

I first wrote about this in 2023, in Softwear: the idea that software is starting to mirror the fashion industry. On one side you get fast fashion: single-use apps, ready to wear, mass-produced and gone tomorrow, the way a Shein haul is. On the other side you get statement pieces: opinionated software from small houses that sells an identity rather than a feature, with landing pages that work like runways. Trends trickle down from the statement pieces into everyday tools, the way the cerulean sweater travels down in *The Devil Wears Prada*. And the niches will get as specific as any *core* on TikTok.

In Media Must Evolve I argued that the platforms that win are the ones that dare to play with the idea of content itself. Disappearing messages made Snapchat. Stories rewired how we share our days. Duets turned lip-syncing into collaboration. Each time, a new format was handed to creators, and they pushed it far beyond anything its makers planned. Software is the next format. It is only just arriving, and it will be pushed just as hard.

#### Apps in the feed

My hunch is that someone will soon realise there is real power in software as content, and that it carries and amplifies through social network effects. They will build a feed for it: a way to post software into a community the way we post photos. We have already seen a small preview. In early 2024, Frames on Farcaster turned posts into small apps, and Open Actions did something similar in the Lens ecosystem. You could mint, vote, buy or play without leaving the timeline. It was contained and niche, but the mechanism was unmistakable.

`Also I think we still have not learned our lesson from how smart Midjourney was through launching through discord, prompt and outcome in one place, was a source of inspiration, and therefore the more you saw the more your vocabulary started to compound.`

If you can launch apps into your feed, you shorten the distance again. Think about launching a product the traditional way: a post links to a landing page, the landing page asks you to sign up, sign-up asks for payment, and only then can you use the thing. Every hop loses people. Put the purchase flow directly inside the post and you don't lose them; they convert right there. Sign-in is already there, because you are in the client. The payment rail is already there. Your friends are the discovery and the social proof. And you only need one good feature to ship. I made the full case in [The Bull Case for Launching Products through Feeds. We are moving from short-form video to short-form software, from things you watch to things you play.

#### The Spotify of software

Where does this go? I think there will be a Spotify of software. You will subscribe to software the way you subscribe to music, as a kind of content, rather than buying a licence for a suite. The reason is simple. The faster I can generate a tool that fits exactly what I need in this moment, and we went from hours to minutes within months, the less I think about software subscriptions at all. I don't need a suite. I need this utility, concept, tool, experience, micro outcome, right now.

That turns workflows into playlists. I have one playlist of tools that lets me build and generate in a particular way, another for launch weeks, another for research. And there is radio: something that simply proposes the next tool, the way Spotify Radio proposes the next song. I would genuinely like that.

`Comment : I really don't know how the future of work looks like, but somehow I feel it still evolve around certain routines, they might feel / look / be alien today but.`

Here is the uncomfortable part. In a sense, the value of software is already gone. Look at how streaming pays: a small number of headline artists take most of the payouts, because they hold most of the monthly listeners. Software may look the same. If Adobe or Figma becomes, in essence, a prompt, the price of that prompt falls to a couple of cents. But it’s run across millions of users, and they may be able to get something from that. For most smaller makers, that economy hardly matters; they will never sit at the head of that curve.

So we will live in two worlds at once. One is the Spotify bubble: very curated, very convenient, with a lot less discovery. The other is the social feed: creators continuously dropping new software, each drop memed and evolved for a few days until something new takes its place. Most of the culture will happen in the second world. Most of the money will flow through the first.

#### Who gets paid for the echo?

This is the part I can't answer yet. Last year a YouTube rabbit hole about reaction streamers left me with a broken thought: is the echo now worth more than the sound? A reaction to a video can earn more than the video itself. The commentary claims the added value, and the original creator mostly gets exposure. I wrote it up in Toasters, Pigs, and Royalties around two books. Christien Meindertsma's *PIG 05049* follows a single pig into hundreds of products, from food to toothpaste. Thomas Thwaites' *The Toaster Project* tries to build one toaster from raw materials and nearly fails. Our economy is extremely good at extracting value downstream from a commodity, and almost blind to the upstream work of making an original.

Software as content inherits that problem and speeds it up. On one evening this week, one base became six tools. Any of them could outgrow the base, and any remix of them could outgrow that in turn. Right now the only path back to the source is that polite note in a README. That is a gesture, not a system. If we want a remix culture for software that is generous rather than extractive, we will have to design how value travels back up the chain, and we should do it while the chain is still short.

`Comment: Someone will almost always build the better version of what you do, now the thing is at least for now the loudest voice wins, which is why I probably should have started to build an audience years ago.`

#### What stays: the supernormal

Not everything becomes a meme. In 2006 Jasper Morrison and Naoto Fukasawa named the other end of the spectrum *Super Normal*: objects so refined by use that their design disappears. A fork, a stool, a hammer. Nobody posts them, and everybody uses them. In Questioning SupernormalI pushed the idea further. Whole systems become supernormal too: markets, money, even the way we measure time. They are so familiar that we forget they were designed, and so we forget they can be changed.

That is the down branch of the fork. Utilities so normal no one sees them, called by agents, never trending. A future infrastructure only really succeeds if it becomes supernormal in this sense: one log, one language, unnoticed. The memes sit on top and the supernormal sits underneath. The most interesting work, I think, happens in between: tools you notice for a week, built on rules you never notice at all.

#### Open questions

- ****Lock-in.****Who owns the base when everything is a remix of it, and the base runs on one company's runtime?
- ****Security.****Prompt injection is the new virus. What is the antivirus, and who ships it: the platform, the community, or the file itself?
- ****Quality.****If the scoreboard is engagement, does a tool still have to work, or only to play well for a week?
- ****Credit.****Will there still be credit or attribution?
- ****Discovery.****In a Spotify-shaped world, who curates software, and who never makes it onto the radio?
- ****Memory.****If tools fade in days, what deserves to be kept? This workspace is one small answer.
