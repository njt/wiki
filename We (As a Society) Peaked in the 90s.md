# We (As a Society) Peaked in the 90s

Chris's blog post arguing that the late 1990s struck a balance between technology and humanity that has since been lost — a short, earnest personal essay that ignited a 125-comment HN thread spanning nostalgia, privilege, generational perspective, and the question of whether things are genuinely worse or we're all just getting older. The post itself is lightweight, but the discussion it catalyzed is a rich accidental focus group on what people miss and what they're glad is gone.

---

## Key Quotes

> "We used tech without letting it own us."

The thesis of the piece, stated plainly. The 90s had digital tools — CDs, early internet, consoles — but they were discrete objects you used and walked away from. Contrast with the subscription-and-notification regime of the 2020s. This maps cleanly onto the [[Smart Models Dumb Pipes]] framing: tools should serve, not demand service.

> "It wasn't your whole life."

On the early internet. You logged on, explored, got what you needed, and stepped away. The browser was a place you visited, not a layer over reality. This is the pre-history of every modern conversation about attention and agency — see [[Cognitive Debt]] for what happens when consumption velocity exceeds comprehension.

> "People didn't know that much about each other — which was fine."

Privacy as a default, not a setting. You could disappear, change, be half-formed without documenting it. The right to be incomplete is one of the quiet casualties of the social internet. [[Introduction to Obsidian]] touches on this tangentially — the file-over-app philosophy is also a privacy-over-platform philosophy.

> "Hard work produced something you could point to — not just emails and meetings and vibes."

The tangible/intangible work distinction. [[The Mundanity of Excellence]] makes a related point from the other direction: excellence is about the quality of choices, not the visible artifact, but both authors are reaching for the same unease — that work has become unmoored from physical consequence.

## Key Themes

- **#concept** **Technological balance** — The 90s as a Goldilocks zone: enough tech to enhance life, not enough to dominate it. This is the central framework of the piece.
- **#concept** **Nostalgia as diagnostic** — Whether the 90s were objectively better or this is just what getting older feels like. The HN thread spends half its energy on this meta-question.
- **#pattern** **Scarcity creates value** — Limited photos per roll, slow album releases, waiting for a movie to come out. Artificial scarcity in the 2020s (drops, queues, limited editions) is an attempt to recreate what was once just reality.
- **#concept** **The pre/post internet boundary** — SilverElfin's HN comment nails it: the 90s cohort got to experience both worlds. That dual citizenship is closing as the last pre-internet adults age out.
- **#tool** **The internet as place vs. layer** — Early internet was a destination; now it's the substrate. Chris's framing echoes early web nostalgia but the architectural point is deeper than nostalgia.
- **#person** **Chris (@chris)** — Independent blogger on pagecord.com. The post is personal, not researched — it's a vibe piece that happened to land.

## Critical Analysis

**The post is weak as an argument but strong as a prompt.** Chris doesn't attempt to prove anything — he lists ten feelings and calls it a day. But the HN response proves there's something here. The comment section functions as an accidental deliberative poll: half the respondents agree with the diagnosis but disagree about the mechanism, a quarter reject the premise on privilege grounds, and the remainder are debating whether the feeling is real or just aging.

**The strongest counterargument is the privilege critique.** ThrowawayTestr's "Yeah it was great if you weren't gay" is a four-word essay. The 90s had AIDS, Section 28, rampant school bullying with no recourse, and the war on drugs at full throttle. Chris acknowledges "plenty of stuff was grim" but doesn't engage with it — and that's the difference between a blog post and an argument.

**The Douglas Adams rule is a dodge, not a rebuttal.** Multiple HN commenters cite Adams's three phases of technology reaction ("anything invented after you're 35 is against the natural order"), but sebastos correctly identifies this as a thought-terminating cliche. It assumes all technological change is neutral and all negative reactions are age-bias. That's an empirical claim, not a logical one — and arguments about social media, attention economics, and surveillance capitalism suggest some changes genuinely are worse.

**The 90s were a local maximum for a specific demographic.** White, straight, middle-class, Western, male — the set of people for whom the 90s were a balance of technology and freedom is narrow. Chris doesn't claim universality, but the piece would be stronger for acknowledging its perspective. The HN thread supplies this critique from multiple angles (sexuality, race, class, geography).

**The "what now?" question is the interesting one.** Ghusto's comment — "How can we have a constructive conversation about this, come to a consensus that things are not okay now, and move forward?" — is where the real work lives. The piece diagnoses; it doesn't prescribe. The [[Creative Firewall]] concept (Sundar's framework for the boundary between authentic human prompting and AI-optimized output) is one attempt at an answer. [[Things You're Allowed to Do]] is another — most constraints are self-imposed, and opting out of the attention economy is harder but possible, as zer00eyz notes in the thread.

**The piece matters because the feeling is widespread, not because the argument is airtight.** The fact that Gen Z — people who weren't alive in the 90s — also romanticize the decade suggests something structural, not generational. The 90s weren't a golden age; they were the last decade before the internet became a totalizing force. That's what people are responding to, even if they can't articulate it.
