# The Education of the Broligarchy

Blake Smith's essay in Colossus argues that the Silicon Valley canon — *Atlas Shrugged*, *The Sovereign Individual*, *Snow Crash*, biographies of Jobs/Musk/Rockefeller, and a dozen other books passed between founders like Patrick Collison and Marc Andreessen — doesn't teach business or technology. It teaches a worldview. The canon balances tales of world-bending titans against books that humble that ambition with impersonal systemic forces, and Smith's sharpest observation is that these are books "first encountered mostly in adolescence — and particularly by teenage boys," diagnosing an arrested development at the core of tech's ruling class.

---

## Key Quotes

> "The same sources that inspired tech moguls to bend matter, minds, and markets to their will may also help explain their foray into other forms of power."

Smith's framing quote on the Colossus author page. The move from markets to politics isn't a pivot — it's the same canon, same worldview, applied to a new domain.

> The canon's books are "first encountered mostly in adolescence — and particularly by teenage boys."

The essay's most damning line. It's not that the books are bad — it's that they're frozen at the moment of first encounter. A 45-year-old billionaire reasoning from *Atlas Shrugged* is working with an intellectual toolkit assembled at 15.

## Critical Analysis

Smith's essay is valuable because it reads the canon *as a canon* — not as a list of favorite books but as a coherent belief system with tensions built in. The ambition/humility dialectic he identifies is real: for every Jobs bio that says "you can change the world," there's a *Sovereign Individual* or *Godel, Escher, Bach* that says "you're a tiny node in an incomprehensibly large system." That tension produces a specific kind of person: someone who believes both that they're special and that they're helpless before large forces. It explains a lot about the oscillation between grandiosity and victimhood in tech leadership.

The "teenage boys" observation is the kill shot, but it's also the essay's limitation. Smith doesn't fully explore *why* the canon arrests development rather than maturing with its readers. Part of the answer is structural: when you're rich enough that no one tells you no, you never have to update your priors. The books aren't the problem — the insulation is. A canon read inside a feedback-proof bubble becomes dogma.

The essay also underplays class. This isn't just any teenage boys — it's teenage boys who went to Stanford and Harvard, who had the social capital to treat *Atlas Shrugged* as aspirational rather than ridiculous. The canon is a class marker as much as an intellectual formation.
