# ytx How to Write Interesting Chord Progressions

Michael Keithson's radial mental model for harmony: a key centre with seven independent "strands" of harmonic options radiating outward. A 25-minute YouTube video, distilled by njt into a reference gist that prioritizes function over theory and practical shortcuts over memorization.

---

## The Radial Model

Keithson visualizes a tonic at the centre with harmonic systems radiating outward like spokes. You draw from multiple strands simultaneously when improvising or composing — they're not sequential levels but parallel options.

His seven strands, accumulated over years of practice:

1. **Diatonic chords** — the seven chords native to the key
2. **Secondary dominants** — V of each diatonic chord (except I)
3. **Modal interchange** — chords borrowed from parallel minor modes (Dorian, Phrygian, Aeolian)
4. **Two-fives (II-Vs)** — preceding any dominant with its II
5. **Tritone substitutions** — swapping a dominant for one a tritone away
6. **Diminished chords** — dominant substitutes, half-step below any target
7. **Five chord alternatives** — non-diatonic V7 replacements that preserve the 4→3 voice leading

## Key Quotes

> "For a while I was using some of these options in my playing, but without any real sort of understanding"

This is the core arc of the video: from stumbling into sounds to building a systematic mental model. Keithson positions himself as someone who learned backward — playing first, understanding later — which gives his framework a practical, non-academic texture.

> "This visual, the key centre with the different strands coming off is quite a good representation"

The radial model is the video's real contribution. It's not novel music theory — secondary dominants and tritone subs are Jazz 101 — but the *organization* as parallel, independently attachable systems is genuinely useful.

> "having complex and interesting or atypical harmony might actually hinder trying to get the result that you want"

The anti-jazz-snob caveat. Refreshing self-awareness for a theory video. He explicitly names different goals (lyrical storytelling, dance hypnotism) where harmonic complexity is counterproductive.

## Practical Shortcuts

Keithson teaches operationalizable heuristics, not just concepts:

- **Tritone sub shortcut:** play a dominant 7 a half-step *above* your target chord (the tritone of that is a half-step above the target — no need to calculate)
- **Diminished shortcut:** play a diminished chord a half-step *below* your target
- **Modal interchange as scale degrees:** memorize chord *types* per scale degree (♭II = maj7, ♭III = maj7, ♭VI = maj7, ♭VII = dom7) so the knowledge is key-independent

These shortcuts are what make the video worth 25 minutes. Most theory instruction gives you the concept and leaves you to derive the operational version. Keithson gives you both.

## Critical Analysis

**What's good:** The radial model is sticky precisely because it's loose. It doesn't try to unify everything into one grand theory of harmony — it acknowledges that these systems were discovered independently and can be bolted on independently. The function-over-origin framing ("this chord works because of where it leads, not where it came from") is the right lens for a working musician. And the practical shortcuts are genuinely useful for improvisers who need to think in real time.

**What's missing:** The summary itself catalogues the gaps well. No decision-making framework exists for *choosing* which strand to use when — you get the full toolbox but no workflow. Voice leading gets almost no attention despite being what separates competent chord changes from graceful ones. The modal interchange restriction to Dorian/Phrygian/Aeolian is never justified (what about Lydian? Mixolydian? Harmonic minor?). And rhythm — the dimension that determines whether a progression sounds like jazz or metal or folk — is entirely absent.
