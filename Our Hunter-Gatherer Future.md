# Our Hunter-Gatherer Future

An academic paper by John Gowdy (Professor of Economics Emeritus, Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute) arguing that agriculture was enabled by the uniquely stable climate of the Holocene, and that extreme climate change projections would make agriculture impossible -- potentially returning humanity to hunter-gatherer subsistence. Published in *Futures* (January 2020), open access.

---

## Key Quotes

> "For most of human history, about 300,000 years, we lived as hunter gatherers in sustainable, egalitarian communities of a few dozen people."

> "Climate models indicate that the Earth could warm by 3C-4C by the year 2100 and eventually by as much as 8C or more. This would return the planet to the unstable climate conditions of the Pleistocene when agriculture was impossible."

> "Policies could be enacted to make the transition away from industrial civilization less devastating and improve the prospects of our hunter-gatherer descendants. These include aggressive policies to reduce the long-run extremes of climate change, aggressive population reduction policies, rewilding, and protecting the world's remaining indigenous cultures."

The paper makes two arguments that are individually well-established but rarely combined.

First: the Neolithic Revolution was not an unambiguous advance. For 300,000 years humans lived as hunter-gatherers in sustainable, egalitarian communities. The Holocene -- a geological epoch beginning about 12,000 years ago -- provided an unprecedented combination of climate stability and warm temperatures that made dependence on wild grains possible, which over several thousand years led to agriculture and large-scale state societies. These agricultural societies show a common pattern of expansion and collapse, with climate change frequently driving the collapses. Archaeological evidence consistently shows that early farmers were shorter, sicker, more malnourished, and worked longer hours than contemporary hunter-gatherers. This is mainstream archaeology, not a fringe claim.

Second: business-as-usual climate trajectories (3-4C by 2100, potentially 8C or more beyond) would destabilize the climate to the point where large-scale agriculture becomes impossible. The stable Holocene climate that enabled agriculture in the first place was a historical anomaly. Return to Pleistocene-like instability means return to Pleistocene-like subsistence patterns.

The implicit conclusion is darkly ironic: the thing that made us "civilized" (agriculture) may have been a temporary blip enabled by an unusually stable climate, and we're about to end that stability ourselves.

The paper's policy proposals are notably radical for an academic paper: aggressive population reduction, rewilding, and protecting remaining indigenous cultures -- framed not as moral imperatives but as practical preparation for post-agricultural society. The title's word "uncivilization" comes from Paul Kingsnorth and Dougald Hine's "Dark Mountain Manifesto."

## Critical Analysis

The first argument (agriculture was initially bad for individuals) is well-supported but incomplete -- agriculture eventually enabled medicine, literacy, science, and everything else that makes modern life possible. The quality-of-life comparison only holds for the first several millennia. Gowdy's point about expansion-and-collapse cycles in agricultural societies is stronger and harder to dismiss.

The second argument (extreme warming kills agriculture) is physically plausible but depends entirely on the emissions trajectory. The 8C+ scenario is worst-case, not inevitable. And the paper doesn't adequately address human adaptation -- greenhouse agriculture, genetic engineering, and other technologies that didn't exist in the Pleistocene. The Holocene may have been necessary for the *origin* of agriculture, but it's not clear it's necessary for its *continuation* given modern technology.

The policy proposals (especially "aggressive population reduction") raise obvious ethical questions that the abstract doesn't address. The framing of post-agricultural life as a return to "sustainable, egalitarian communities" romanticizes hunter-gatherer existence -- survival at carrying capacity with high infant mortality and short life expectancy is "sustainable" in the ecological sense but hardly in the humanitarian one.

Still, the core reframing is valuable: we take stable climate and functioning agriculture as permanent fixtures of human life, and they're neither. The 12,000-year Holocene is a blip in the 300,000-year history of our species. The historical perspective is a useful corrective to presentism.
