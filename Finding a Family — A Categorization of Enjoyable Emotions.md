# Finding a Family — A Categorization of Enjoyable Emotions

Graham, Thomson, Nakamura, Brandt, and Siegel (2017, Claremont Graduate University, Journal of Positive Psychology) propose the first systematic taxonomy of enjoyable emotions: 28 discrete emotions classified into eight families. The classification emerged from a visual-mapping-then-debate process, starting from 49 candidate terms and filtering down using two criteria (defined triggers/appraisals/outcomes, and pleasurable to experience).

The paper is a review and synthesis, not a empirical study — it surveys existing scholarship, groups by shared features (triggers, appraisals, or phenomenology), and identifies research gaps for every emotion and family. Many of the eight families are proposed for the first time.

The core move: treat positive emotions as *discrete* rather than as undifferentiated "positive affect." This is a corrective to a field that had accumulated names for individual emotions without any structure relating them.

---

## The Eight Families

| Family | Unifier | Emotions |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-praising** | Positive appraisal of self | authentic pride, fiero, naches, feeling respected |
| **Other-praising** | Response to exemplary other | admiration, elevation, gratitude, inspiration |
| **Past-oriented** | Focus on past event | forgiveness, nostalgia, relief |
| **Future-oriented** | Focus on desirable future outcome | anticipatory enthusiasm, courage, determination, hope |
| **Hazardous** | Self-gratification at cost to others | lust, schadenfreude, hubristic pride |
| **Affectionate** | Other worthy of care (no praise required) | love, attachment love, tenderness, positive empathy |
| **Arousal-defined** | Activation level | euphoria, serenity |
| **Violation-elicited** | Novel/unexpected stimulus | amusement, awe, curiosity, positive surprise |

The families form three contrast pairs: self ↔ other praising, past ↔ future oriented, affectionate ↔ hazardous. The arousal-defined and violation-elicited families are unpaired.

---

## The Method — Why This Isn't Just Another List

The authors started with 49 candidate emotion terms from a literature search (March 2015), then applied two criteria: (1) the term must name something with defined triggers, appraisals, and behavioral patterns in the psychological literature, and (2) it must be enjoyable to experience.

This eliminated optimism (a trait, not an emotion — no specific triggers) and, most controversially, **happiness and joy**. The authors couldn't find scholarly consensus on what specifically triggers joy, or whether "happiness" refers to an emotion or a life evaluation. They concluded it was "premature" to classify either as a discrete emotion.

> "Even among articles that define joy and happiness as discrete emotions, there seems to be little consensus regarding the triggers of these emotions."

They suggest joy might be a midpoint on the arousal dimension between serenity and euphoria — an idea the classification system itself generated. This is the kind of hypothesis a taxonomy is supposed to produce.

The classification process: emotions were "visually mapped into groups based on the degree to which their definitions, eliciting events, and associated outcomes shared key features." Then the authors collectively debated each placement. It was qualitative, iterative, and self-consciously provisional.

## What the Full Paper Adds Beyond the Abstract

### The definitions matter

Each of the 28 emotions gets a specific definition with academic provenance. The culturally specific ones are the most vivid:

- **Fiero** (Italian): "The emotion felt when meeting a difficult challenge, either with others in the context of competition or alone" (Ekman, 2003). Parkinson (2005): "a triumphal feeling coming as a release after a period of intense struggle"
- **Naches** (Yiddish): "Pride in the achievement of others, with whom you have a relationship" (Platt et al., 2013). The social component is essential
- **Schadenfreude** (German): Pleasure in others' misfortune, intensified by self-evaluation threats

### The distinction between feeling respected and respecting

The paper draws a sharp line: *feeling* respected qualifies as an enjoyable emotion. *Respecting* another does not — it may be a form of admiration or a "socially constructed state," not a discrete emotion with defined triggers and outcomes.

### The relief subtypes

Relief splits into near-miss relief and task-completion relief, with different functions. Near-miss relief motivates counterfactual thinking about how to avoid similar situations. Task-completion relief reinforces endurance during difficult tasks. This is one of the few emotions with well-differentiated subtypes in the paper.

### The courage problem

Courage is primarily studied as a disposition, not an emotion. It barely satisfies the criteria. Hannah et al.'s (2007) model includes positive emotional components, but the authors acknowledge this is the weakest member of the future-oriented family — the research just isn't there yet.

### Elevation is empirically distinct

Experimental studies have shown elevation to be distinct from amusement, joy, enthusiasm, pleasure, and gratitude. It produces its own psychological and behavioral responses. This matters because it's easy to assume elevation is "just a flavor of joy." It's not.

### Inspiration has two directions

Thrash & Elliot (2004) distinguish "inspired by" (transcendence, imaginative recreation) from "inspired to" (approach motivation, the urge to move toward a stimulus). Both are empirically associated with positive outcomes, but through different mechanisms.

### The oxytocin connection

Both elevation and gratitude have been empirically linked to oxytocin — suggesting a shared biological substrate for the other-praising family. This is noted as a future research direction, not a finding. But it's the kind of cross-emotion connection the family framework enables.

## Key Quotes

> "The pursuit of enjoyment is a primary motivation in our lives… People organize their lives to maximize the experience of [enjoyable emotions]." (Ekman, 2003, p. 200)

This is the premise. The paper takes it as given that classifying what people pursue is worth doing.

> "Algoe and Haidt designated elevation, admiration, and gratitude as other-praising emotions and we add the emotion inspiration as it also has the unifying feature of being triggered by an exemplary other."

The quiet scholarly move of adding a fourth member to someone else's three-member family. The justification is structural, not empirical — if inspiration shares the trigger structure, it belongs.

> "Tenderness should produce altruistic motivation directed toward the ultimate goal of addressing the target's vulnerability." (Lishner, Batson, & Huss, 2011)

The clearest statement of the functional logic behind the affectionate family.

> "The current manuscript is the first attempt to put forth a comprehensive list and categorization of enjoyable emotions, and as such it may not be as ordered as it is poised to be in the future."

Honest about the provisional nature. Good.

## Critical Analysis

### The taxonomy is more useful as a corrective than as a map

The core contribution isn't that these eight families are *right* — it's that positive psychology needed *some* structure beyond the crude positive/negative valence split. The families themselves are uneven. "Arousal-defined" (euphoria and serenity) feels like a residual category. These two emotions share almost nothing except being defined partly by arousal level — it's the "miscellaneous" drawer with a fancy label.

### The culturally specific emotions expose a tension the paper doesn't resolve

Fiero (Italian), naches (Yiddish), and schadenfreude (German) are included alongside English-labeled emotions without interrogation of whether the taxonomy itself is culturally bounded. If the classification had been built from a different linguistic starting point, would the families look the same? Almost certainly not. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by including these terms but doesn't wrestle with what it means for the universality of the framework.

### "Hazardous emotions" is the category that justifies the whole enterprise

This is the paper's sharpest theoretical move and the one most likely to survive revision. If positive psychology only studied unambiguously good emotions, it would be propaganda. Including lust, schadenfreude, and hubristic pride in a taxonomy of "enjoyable" emotions forces the distinction between *feels good* and *is good*. The authors note the hazardous family is "unlikely to appear on other positive emotion lists." That's the point.

### The violation-elicited family is the most generative for future work

Grouping amusement, awe, curiosity, and positive surprise under "expectation violation" connects positive psychology to predictive processing and Bayesian brain theories in a way the other families don't. It suggests a mechanism: enjoyable emotions arise when the world violates expectations in safe, manageable ways. The authors note that all four emotions in this family involve a "shift of attention toward the stimuli in an effort to understand" — they're learning emotions. This is empirically richer than the temporal or arousal-based groupings.

### The real gap is function

The families are organized by trigger (self-praising, other-praising, violation-elicited) or time-orientation (past, future), not by what the emotions *do*. An alternative taxonomy organized around social function (bonding, status negotiation, learning, threat response) might cut the phenomena at different joints. Love (affectionate) and gratitude (other-praising) end up in different families here; both are fundamentally about social bonding. The authors acknowledge this implicitly — they note that "future classifications might consider" outcomes as a basis, but chose not to here.

### The ejection of joy and happiness is a feature, not a bug

Some readers will balk at a taxonomy of positive emotions that excludes joy. But the authors' reasoning is sound: if you can't specify what triggers an emotion, you can't distinguish it from general positive affect. The ejection is methodologically honest. It also generates a testable hypothesis: if joy is the midpoint between serenity and euphoria on an arousal dimension, then the arousal-defined family has three members, not two. The taxonomy earns its keep by producing that prediction.

### The paper's modesty is strategic

"We welcome future scholarship that critically considers these criteria, or proposes the inclusion or exclusion of other emotions to expand upon or refine the present list." This is how a first taxonomy should land — as an invitation to argue, not a declaration. The structure is less important than the conversation it enables.
