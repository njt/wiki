# Finding a Family — A Categorization of Enjoyable Emotions

Graham, Thomson, Nakamura, Brandt, and Siegel (2017) propose the first systematic taxonomy of enjoyable emotions, classifying 28 discrete positive emotions into eight families. The paper is a review and synthesis — it surveys existing scholarship on each emotion, then groups them by shared antecedents, phenomenology, and function. Many of the families are proposed for the first time. The framework's core move is to treat positive emotions as *discrete* rather than as an undifferentiated "positive affect" blob, which had been the default in psychology.

---

## The Eight Families

The 28 emotions, grouped:

| Family | Emotions |
|---|---|
| **Self-praising** | authentic pride, fiero, naches, feeling respected |
| **Other-praising** | admiration, elevation, gratitude, inspiration |
| **Past-oriented** | forgiveness, nostalgia, relief |
| **Future-oriented** | anticipatory enthusiasm, courage, determination, hope |
| **Hazardous** | lust, schadenfreude, hubristic pride |
| **Affectionate** | love, attachment love, tenderness, positive empathy |
| **Arousal-defined** | euphoria, serenity |
| **Violation-elicited** | amusement, awe, curiosity, positive surprise |

The temporal families (past-oriented, future-oriented) are the most intuitive. The "violation-elicited" family is the most interesting structural choice — grouping amusement, awe, curiosity, and positive surprise under the shared trigger of *expectation violation* is a genuinely novel synthesis. The "hazardous" family is the most provocative: enjoyable emotions that reliably produce negative outcomes.

---

## Key Quotes

> "Responding to burgeoning scholarship examining discrete positive emotions, the overarching goals of the current review are to provide a summary of 28 enjoyable emotions and to offer an initial classification of these emotions into families."

The paper is explicitly a *first pass* at taxonomy, not a final word. The modesty is appropriate — taxonomy in emotion research is notoriously contested, and the authors are self-aware about the provisional nature of the groupings.

> "The families of discrete enjoyable emotions, many proposed for the first time..."

The phrase "many proposed for the first time" is doing heavy lifting. Before this paper, positive psychology had no systematic classification of positive emotions at the discrete level. The field had names for individual emotions but no structure relating them to each other. That's a remarkable gap for a field called "positive psychology."

---

## Key Themes

- **#concept** Discrete emotions approach — Treating positive emotions as distinct states with different causes, subjective feels, and consequences, rather than collapsing them into valence.
- **#concept** Emotion families — Grouping by shared function or trigger rather than by surface similarity. The temporal families and violation-elicited family are the strongest cases for this approach.
- **#concept** Hazardous positive emotions — The recognition that "positive" and "good for you" are not the same thing. Schadenfreude and hubristic pride are enjoyable in the moment and corrosive over time.

---

## Critical Analysis

**The taxonomy is more useful as a corrective than as a map.** The core contribution isn't that these eight families are *right* — it's that positive psychology needed *some* structure beyond the crude positive/negative valence split. The families themselves are uneven. "Arousal-defined" (euphoria and serenity) feels like a residual category — these two emotions share almost nothing except being defined partly by arousal level. It's the "miscellaneous" drawer with a fancy label.

**The culturally specific emotions are the most valuable entries and the most underanalyzed.** Fiero (Italian — pride in overcoming a challenge), naches (Yiddish — pride in children's/students' achievements), and schadenfreude (German — joy in others' misfortune) are included alongside English-labeled emotions without much interrogation of whether the taxonomy itself is culturally bounded. If the classification had been built from a different linguistic starting point, would the families look the same? Probably not. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by including these terms but doesn't wrestle with what it means for the universality of the framework.

**"Hazardous emotions" is the category that justifies the whole enterprise.** If positive psychology only studied unambiguously good emotions, it would be propaganda. Including lust, schadenfreude, and hubristic pride in a taxonomy of "enjoyable" emotions forces the distinction between *feels good* and *is good*. This is the paper's sharpest theoretical move, and it's the one most likely to survive revision of the taxonomy.

**The violation-elicited family is the most generative for future work.** Grouping amusement, awe, curiosity, and positive surprise under "expectation violation" connects positive psychology to predictive processing and Bayesian brain theories in a way the other families don't. It suggests a mechanism: enjoyable emotions arise when the world violates expectations in safe, manageable ways. This is empirically richer than the temporal or arousal-based groupings.

**The real gap is function.** The families are organized by trigger (self-praising, other-praising, violation-elicited) or time-orientation (past, future), not by what the emotions *do*. An alternative taxonomy organized around social function (bonding, status negotiation, learning, threat response) might cut the phenomena at different joints and produce different groupings. Love and gratitude end up in different families here; both are fundamentally about social bonding.
