# The Pragmatic Summit

Gergely Orosz's inaugural one-day curated conference for senior engineers and engineering leaders, held February 11, 2026 in San Francisco. Application-based admission, ~400 attendees, $499. Speaker lineup drawn from CTOs and founders at OpenAI, Atlassian, GitHub, Linear, and Uber. An extension of *The Pragmatic Engineer* newsletter's brand: practical, high-signal, aimed at people who build and lead rather than people who manage slide decks.

---

## Format and Positioning

Single-track, 8am–6pm, deliberately small. Application-based admission is the key design choice — it's not a pay-to-attend free-for-all. The thesis: curate the audience as carefully as the speakers, and the hallway conversations become as valuable as the talks.

The price point ($499) is interesting. It's above the grassroots meetup tier but well below the $1,500–$3,000 corporate conference band where ticket buyers expense it without reading the agenda. This is a signal: you're paying real money, and you care enough about engineering practice to spend it.

## Speaker Thesis

The lineup reveals the editorial stance. No academics, no pure thought leaders, no vendor evangelists (sponsors aside). Every speaker is either currently building (CTOs of Atlassian and DX, Principal Engineer at Uber) or recently built (ex-GitHub CEO) or building tools for builders (Linear founder, Flint co-founder). The OpenAI slot (Vijaye Raji as CTO Applications) is the one outsider — an AI company speaking to an audience whose jobs AI is actively reshaping.

Notable absences: no FAANG staff engineers sharing war stories (the classic conference staple), nobody from the VC/"startup guru" circuit, no panel discussions (which are usually filler). Every slot is a named individual with a product or team they're accountable for.

## What It Is and Isn't

This is not a replacement for Strange Loop, QCon, or LeadDev. It's a curated salon masquerading as a conference. The single-track format eliminates the "which talk do I skip" problem but also means there's no room for niche topics — everything has to work for a CTO and a staff engineer simultaneously.

It's the conference equivalent of a well-edited newsletter: fewer pieces, higher bar, strong editorial voice. The risk is that it becomes a speaker circuit for the same dozen people who rotate through every engineering conference. The inaugural lineup suggests awareness of that trap — Dohmke is fresh off GitHub, Lim is from a lesser-known company, Tacho runs a developer-experience startup rather than the usual infrastructure SaaS.

## Key Themes

- **#concept** Curation over scale — application-based admission and single-track format prioritize signal density over reach
- **#concept** Practitioner-led — every speaker is accountable for real products and teams, not just ideas
- **#pattern** Newsletter-to-conference pipeline — *The Pragmatic Engineer* joins Stratechery, Lenny's Podcast, and others in extending a media brand into live events
- **#tool** The sponsor choice (Statsig + Linear) signals the audience: engineers who care about feature flags, experimentation, and project management tooling

---

## Critical Analysis

The curation play is smart but fragile. At 400 people and application-based, the inaugural year can be selective because the brand is fresh and the location is San Francisco — the densest concentration of the target audience on earth. Scaling this model to a second year, or (especially) to a second city, means either expanding capacity (diluting curation) or staying small and leaving money on the table. The newsletter's business model doesn't depend on the conference, though, so perhaps staying small is the point.

The speaker lineup is heavy on CTOs, which is a double-edged signal. CTO talks can be revealing (Atlassian scaling stories, Uber infrastructure decisions) or they can be polished PR. The application-based format means there's some pressure to deliver substance, since the audience didn't just expense a ticket and show up — they made a case for why they should be there.

The absence of recorded talks is the biggest missed opportunity. If the point is signal density, why gate it to 400 people? A single-track, curated conference with this caliber of speaker should be publishing videos. *The Pragmatic Engineer* newsletter reaches hundreds of thousands of readers — the conference content should amplify that, not sit behind a velvet rope. The newsletter-to-conference pipeline works best when the conference feeds the newsletter, not when they're separate products.

Good: the focus on engineering practice over tool evangelism. Bad: San Francisco-only, no recordings, and the format risks becoming a speaker circuit. Worth watching: whether year two maintains curation discipline or expands to capture demand.
