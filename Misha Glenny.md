# Misha Glenny

English journalist and author whose career traces a coherent arc through the hidden systems that shape the world: Balkan ethno-nationalism, global organized crime, cybercrime, and rare earth geopolitics. Best known for *McMafia* (2008) and for succeeding Melvyn Bragg as host of BBC Radio 4's *In Our Time* (announced December 2025).

---

## Key Quotes

> "International organized crime could account for 15% of global GDP."

The headline claim from *McMafia*. As economics it's soft — measuring hidden economies is definitionally impossible, and the number functions more as a provocation than a data point. But the provocation works: it reframes organized crime not as a marginal pathology but as a structural layer of the global economy, interleaved with legitimate capital in ways that make the distinction between "clean" and "dirty" money analytically useless.

> Investigating how competition for critical raw materials drives geopolitical tensions.

From his 2022 framing of *The Scramble for Rare Earths*. The thread connecting his Balkan war reporting, organized crime books, cybercrime work, and rare earths is the same: power flows through supply chains, not just borders. Glenny is fundamentally a supply-chain thinker who happens to work in narrative journalism.

## Key Themes

- **#person** — Journalist, author, broadcaster, professor, NGO operator
- **#concept** — Organized crime as systemic, not deviant; the criminal-underworld/legitimate-world membrane as the real object of study
- **#pattern** — Following power as it disperses from state actors to non-state networks (warlords → mafias → hackers → mineral traders)
- **#concept** — Narrative journalism as systems analysis; making complexity accessible without sanding off the edges

## Critical Analysis

Glenny's strength is pattern recognition across domains that specialists keep separate. The Yugoslav Wars correspondent who later writes about Brazilian favela drug lords and then rare earth supply chains isn't a dilettante — he's tracing the same question ("who actually has power, and how does it move?") through different instantiations. This is what makes him more interesting than a conventional war correspondent or crime reporter.

The weakness is the journalist's temptation to over-claim. "15% of global GDP" is a great lede and a terrible statistic. *McMafia* the book is richer than its headline number, but the headline number is what travels. The BBC drama adaptation compounds this: it's a fictionalized version of a journalistic thesis that already blurred the line between evidence and narrative. Entertaining, but the epistemic chain is three removes from the underlying facts before the viewer sees a frame.

His appointment to *In Our Time* is fascinating. Bragg made the show a monument to a certain kind of intellectual inquiry — mostly dead white men, mostly Western canon, rigorous but narrow. Glenny's global-systems lens could broaden it productively. Or it could turn out that the format is the format and the host doesn't matter much. The fact that the BBC chose a 67-year-old white male journalist rather than someone who'd signal a sharper break suggests institutional caution dressed as continuity.

The through-line of Glenny's career — from Balkan battlefields to rare earth mines — is that understanding power means following the material. Not ideology, not stated intentions, but physical supply chains, money flows, and the people who control them. That's a useful corrective to both the think-tank abstraction of geopolitics and the true-crime sensationalism of most organized crime coverage. He's a materialist in a field that prefers either moralism or technocracy.
