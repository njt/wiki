# One Year of Keeping a Tada List

Aditya Bhargava's field report on maintaining a daily "tada list" (a to-done list, the inverse of a to-do list) for a full year. Rather than tracking what remains undone, he recorded what he finished each day, one page per month, with a hand-drawn header illustration. The practice revealed hidden chains of effort behind finished work, added daily pressure to achieve, and produced a physical artifact he can revisit. After a year, he's undecided about continuing: the value is real, but the maintenance cost is non-trivial.

---

## Key Quotes

> "a tada list forces you to have an accomplishment each day so you can write it down"

Bhargava names the dark side of the practice directly. A to-done list sounds gentler than a to-do list, but it imposes its own tyranny: every day must produce something worth recording. This is the productivity trap with the serial numbers filed off.

> "part of the reason I like learning stuff is it lets me do things I couldn't do before"

The tada list doesn't just record outputs -- it makes visible the capability accumulation that enables those outputs. His watercolor cards didn't come from nowhere; they came from months of mixing greens and doing value studies. The list connects dots that would otherwise fade from memory.

**The chain of effort.** The article's strongest insight is that finished work obscures its own provenance. "I made some cards" collapses months of color mixing, value studies, and plein air painting into a single sentence. The tada list resists that collapse. This is the same pattern that [[engineering-notebook]] addresses for code: the reasoning behind decisions evaporates unless you capture it. The tada list is engineering-notebook for everything else.

**Accomplishment vs. pressure.** The practice has a genuine tension: celebrating what you did vs. feeling you must produce something daily to have anything to celebrate. Bhargava is honest that both are true. Compare with [[life-system]]'s morning/evening ritual, which adds intention-setting to the daily reflection -- the tada list is purely retrospective, which makes the pressure worse because you can't front-load your intention and then check it off.

**Artifact over habit.** By year's end, Bhargava values the physical notebook as an artifact more than he values the daily practice that produced it. This inverts the usual productivity framing (where the habit is the point and the output is secondary). Like [[AI Agents with Human-Like Collaborative Tools]] found with agent journals, the act of writing was more valuable than re-reading -- but the artifact has a different kind of value: forensic, nostalgic, worth having even if you never look at it.

## Critical Analysis

The essay's honesty about the practice's failure modes is what makes it useful. Most productivity writing is aspirational fiction -- here's the system, it worked for me, you should do it too. Bhargava shows the handwriting deteriorating in later months. He stopped doing the illustrations. He's not sure he'll do it again. That's a real field report, not a sales pitch.

The "tada list" framing is clever but ultimately just a rebranding of journaling. What distinguishes it is the constraint: only accomplishments, every day. That constraint creates the value (forced reflection on what actually happened) and the cost (forced production of something to record). Whether the tradeoff is worth it depends on whether you're naturally too hard on yourself (the list helps) or already prone to overwork (the list hurts).

The watercolor example is the most compelling evidence for the practice. Without the tada list, the cards would feel like a discrete project that took a few weeks. With it, they're visible as the culmination of a year-plus of skill-building. Most creative work looks like this -- long invisible preparation, short visible output -- and most project-tracking systems obscure it. The tada list is a corrective.
