# CO2 Overload and Human Blood Chemistry

Larcombe & Bierwirth (2026) present the strongest empirical evidence yet that rising atmospheric CO2 is already measurably altering human blood chemistry at the population level. Using 21 years of NHANES data (1999–2020, ~7,000 Americans per cycle), they show serum bicarbonate climbing in lockstep with atmospheric CO2 (+7%), while calcium and phosphorus decline. Linear extrapolation puts bicarbonate outside the healthy range by ~2076 — within 50 years. This isn't a model; it's a trend already in the data.

---

## Key Quotes

> "The increase in average blood HCO3− (~0.34% per year) is comparable to the increase in atmospheric CO2 levels (~0.5% per year)."

The core empirical claim. Not a correlation mined from noise — the effect size tracks the atmospheric increase remarkably closely across 11 NHANES cycles. The R² isn't reported explicitly but the visual fit in Figure 2A is tight. This is the kind of parallel-trend evidence that makes "causal link" hard to dismiss.

> "Trend analysis suggests that HCO3− levels will be at the currently accepted limit of the healthy range within 50 years, with calcium and phosphorus reaching the currently accepted minimum levels shortly after. Clearly these are estimations."

The paper's own hedge is worth preserving. "Clearly these are estimations" appears right after the most alarming sentence in the abstract. They know they're extrapolating linearly from 21 years of data. But the honesty about uncertainty doesn't make the data less concerning — it makes the authors more credible.

> "Given that the entire evolution period for humans has seen a stable and relatively low atmospheric CO2 level (< 300 ppm), it's possible that our physiology is finely tuned for a range of CO2 that will not be much greater than this level."

The evolutionary framing is the paper's strongest conceptual move. We didn't evolve for 420 ppm. We evolved for 280 ppm, and our CO2 transport system — carbonic anhydrase, the bicarbonate buffer, renal compensation — was calibrated to that range over millions of years. The authors don't overstate this (note the careful "it's possible that"), but it's the argument that makes the NHANES trends legible as a warning rather than a curiosity.

> "Unfortunately, these arguments ignore the mounting evidence that even small, short-term increases in atmospheric CO2 do indeed impact physiology in many species ranging from gastropods, to rodents and humans."

A direct shot at Malte & Wang (2024) and Saunders & Habgood (2023), who argued that acid-base compensatory mechanisms can handle further CO2 increases. The authors' counter is empirical, not theoretical: we're already seeing effects across species. The burden of proof has shifted from "prove CO2 hurts us" to "prove it doesn't."

> "Even a small permanent increase in global human anxiety could have a dangerous impact on societies being associated with greater fear, mental disturbance, conflicts, etc."

One of the paper's most understated but freighted claims. CO2 is an anxiogenic — it triggers the brain's alarm system directly. The distribution of CO2 sensitivity is continuous and normally distributed in the population. If ambient CO2 rises for everyone, the entire anxiety distribution shifts right. The societal implications (political instability, conflict, mental health crisis) are gestured at but not explored — the paper stays in its medical lane.

> "Realistically, to mitigate this approaching threat, the alarm needs to be raised immediately. Unfortunately, currently there is little awareness or inclination for action on this issue."

The paper's closing line. It's notable how restrained this is for a finding this alarming. No call for policy, no manifesto — just a statement that nobody is paying attention and that needs to change. The restraint reads as strategic: make the data unignorable and let others do the alarming.

## Key Themes

#concept **CO2 as a direct physiological toxin, not just a climate forcing agent.** The standard climate discourse treats CO2 as a problem because it warms the planet. This paper argues CO2 is also a direct toxin — it enters your bloodstream, alters your biochemistry, and accumulates in your tissues. The climate change framing made CO2 an environmental problem; the direct-toxicity framing makes it a public health emergency. These are different political categories with different urgency profiles.

#concept **Evolutionary miscalibration.** Our physiology was tuned to ~280 ppm CO2 over millions of years of hominin evolution. We're now at 420 ppm and adding ~2.5 ppm/year. The body's CO2 management system — carbonic anhydrase, bicarbonate buffering, renal compensation, bone mineral exchange — was calibrated for a range we're rapidly leaving. The word "finely tuned" in the paper is doing a lot of work: it implies the tuning is narrow, not robust.

#concept **Bicarbonate as a population-level biomarker.** The clever methodological move: you can't easily measure lifetime CO2 exposure in humans, but you can measure serum bicarbonate, which is mechanistically linked to CO2 load via carbonic anhydrase. NHANES has been measuring it for decades for unrelated reasons. The authors didn't need to run an experiment — they just needed to notice that an existing dataset was already recording the signal.

#concept **Bone as CO2 sink.** The declining calcium and phosphorus are the most counterintuitive finding. Rising CO2 → blood acidification → bone resorption (Ca and P released to buffer pH) → declining serum levels over time as minerals are sequestered in tissue calcification or excreted. Your skeleton is literally buffering the atmosphere. This is a form of slow-motion physiological debt that mimics osteoporosis but with a different mechanism.

#concept **Indoor CO2 amplification.** Americans spend 87% of time indoors where CO2 routinely hits 1,000–3,000 ppm — far above ambient. The paper notes this as a complicating factor: indoor exposure may be driving the NHANES trends more than ambient outdoor levels. Either way, the blood chemistry is changing. But if indoor CO2 is the primary driver, ventilation standards become a public health intervention, not just a comfort issue.

#pattern **Parallel trends as causal evidence.** The paper's entire argument rests on visual parallelism: bicarbonate goes up as CO2 goes up, bicarbonate goes down as... well, it doesn't go down, but calcium and phosphorus do. This isn't a randomized controlled trial and can't be. The NHANES data is observational, population-level, and confounded by everything that changed between 1999 and 2020 (diet, medication, diagnostic criteria, lab methods). The authors acknowledge this ("Clearly these are estimations") but the parallelism across three independent biomarkers is hard to dismiss as coincidence.

#tool **NHANES as accidental longitudinal study.** NHANES was designed for cross-sectional nutritional surveillance, not for tracking CO2 effects. But its consistent methodology, large sample sizes (~7,000 per cycle), and comprehensive blood panels made it an accidental time machine. A reminder that the most valuable dataset for a question is often one collected for a different purpose entirely.

## Critical Analysis

**The paper's empirical core is strong; its extrapolations are speculative but honest about it.** The NHANES trends are real — bicarbonate is rising, calcium and phosphorus are falling. The parallelism with atmospheric CO2 is striking. The 2076/2085/2099 thresholds are linear extrapolations from 21 years of data on a system that is almost certainly nonlinear. The authors know this and say so. The honesty about uncertainty is a feature, not a bug: they're drawing a line through data points and saying "if this continues, here's where we hit the wall." That's not a prediction; it's a warning with a timestamp.

**The causal chain has a gap that the paper doesn't fully close.** Rising atmospheric CO2 → increased CO2 inhalation → increased blood CO2 → increased bicarbonate sounds straightforward. But the paper doesn't show that the *same individuals* with higher CO2 exposure have higher bicarbonate. It shows population means moving together. Diet changes (increased processed food, changed macronutrient ratios), medication trends (proton pump inhibitors affect bicarbonate), and lab methodology shifts could all contribute. The paper's case is circumstantial but cumulative — three independent biomarkers, all moving in mechanistically consistent directions, paralleling atmospheric CO2. That's not proof, but it's the kind of pattern that demands an explanation, and "CO2 is doing this" fits the available evidence better than "three unrelated population trends coincidentally all point the same direction."

**The paper's biggest blind spot is diet.** Between 1999 and 2020, the American diet changed dramatically — more processed food, more protein, different preservatives, different mineral content in soil and water. The acid-base effects of the modern Western diet (high acid load from protein and phosphate additives) are well-documented and could independently drive bicarbonate, calcium, and phosphorus in the directions observed. The paper doesn't control for diet at all. This is a significant limitation that the authors don't adequately address.

**The evolutionary framing is rhetorically powerful but scientifically slippery.** Yes, we evolved at 280 ppm CO2. But we also evolved without antibiotics, eyeglasses, or central heating. "Evolved for X" doesn't mean "cannot survive outside X." Human physiology has substantial homeostatic range — that's what homeostasis *is*. The question is where the range ends, and the paper provides evidence that we may be approaching it, but the evolutionary argument is a framing device, not a mechanism.

**The anxiety claim is the paper's most explosive and least-developed argument.** CO2 is anxiogenic — this is well-established in the panic disorder literature (Battaglia, 2017, cited in the paper). A population-wide rightward shift in the anxiety distribution could have enormous societal consequences. But the paper mentions this in one paragraph and moves on. The dose-response relationship between ambient CO2 (as opposed to inhaled CO2 challenges in a lab) and anxiety has not been established. This deserves its own research program, and the paper is right to flag it — but it's flagging, not demonstrating.

**The complementary angle to [[Our Hunter-Gatherer Future]] is striking.** Gowdy argued from evolutionary mismatch, cognitive decline at elevated CO2, and the fragility of agricultural civilization. Larcombe & Bierwirth provide the blood-chemistry data that makes Gowdy's CO2-cognitive-decline argument biologically plausible. If ambient CO2 is literally changing our blood chemistry, the cognitive effects Gowdy cites (15% decline at 950 ppm) stop being speculation about poorly ventilated rooms and start being a prediction about outdoor air. These papers read like they were written to be read together.

**The real contribution isn't the 50-year prediction — it's the 21-year trend.** The paper will be cited for the alarming timeline, but its lasting value is the documentation that *it's already happening*. Human blood chemistry has measurably changed in the direction of CO2 overload. That's not a model output; it's a measurement. Everything else is extrapolation.
