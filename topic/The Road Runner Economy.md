# The Road Runner Economy

Noah Raford's case that the software industry is already in freefall — it just hasn't looked down yet. Using the Wile E. Coyote metaphor, he argues that Christmas 2025 was the inflection point where frontier AI models crossed the threshold of replicating most commercial SaaS. The essay matters because it's not a thinkpiece from an analyst: Raford had never written code before building nine functional apps in weeks. He's the canary, not the coal-mine inspector.

---

## Key Quotes

> "The Road Runner Economy is a phase transition where the vast majority of commercial software becomes instantly replicable by any competent team with access to frontier AI models."

The thesis in one sentence. "Instantly replicable" is the operative phrase — not "cheaper to build" or "faster to prototype." Copy-paste economics.

> "This meeting could have been a prompt."

The companion aphorism to "I just one-shat your company." Raford's point isn't that meetings are wasteful — it's that the entire corporate coordination apparatus (requirements gathering, sprint planning, integration discussions) collapses into a well-crafted prompt. The meeting *was* the coordination mechanism. Now the prompt is.

> "Enterprise software has always been a compromise — you buy Salesforce not because it fits, but because custom builds were prohibitively expensive."

This is the economic inversion that makes the Road Runner Economy different from prior disruption waves. Previous SaaS disruption was "better, faster, cheaper" within the same paradigm of buying software. This is the paradigm itself dissolving: when custom is cheaper than off-the-shelf, the entire procurement logic inverts.

> "I've got to be honest, I have basically never written a line of code in my life."

Raford drops this *after* listing nine projects he built, including a 135-dimensional ML audio fingerprinting suite. It's the essay's most devastating rhetorical move — not because he's bragging, but because he's *not* a prodigy. He's evidence that the barrier wasn't skill, it was the tooling.

> "Whether that world is better depends entirely on who's building it, and for whom."

The closing line. Raford refuses to be a utopian or a doomer. The technology is neutral; the politics aren't. This is where the essay earns its seriousness — it's not a prediction, it's a warning.

## Key Themes

- **#concept Road Runner Economy** — The phase transition where commercial software becomes trivially replicable. Companies appear solvent until the quarterly reports catch up to the churn metrics. Named for the cartoon moment when physics hasn't yet asserted itself.
- **#concept One-Shat** — Past tense of one-shotting a product, service, or company. "Casual, off-handed, but devastatingly effective." Not a boast but a unit of economic destruction.
- **#person Noah Raford** — Futurist and strategist. Formerly at Dubai Future Foundation. Built Make It So Camp to teach knowledge workers AI "captainship." The essay's power comes from him being a practitioner, not a pundit.
- **#person Harper Reed** — Named as catalyst for the community of builders. Runs the "Vibez (code code code)" WhatsApp group. Connected to [[Awesome Vibez]] and [[Immaculate Knowledge Graph]].
- **#pattern Christmas 2025 watershed** — Extra time + curious builders + Claude Code + New Year's resolution energy + network flywheel. Not a product launch but a collective realization spreading through networks.
- **#concept ACME Price Comparison** — Salesforce at $150-300/user/month vs. AI-generated CRM at $50-100 total/month. The price delta is so extreme it becomes qualitative, not quantitative.
- **#concept GAI (Generic Artificial Intelligence)** — Raford's preferred term over AGI. The distinction: we don't need *general* intelligence to transform the economy, just *generic* intelligence — intelligence as a commodity input, like electricity.

## Critical Analysis

**The essay's greatest strength is its author.** Raford is not a VC pitch-decking or an engineer gatekeeping. He's a futurist who woke up one day and discovered he could build. His credibility comes from being the data point, not analyzing the data. When he says "one-shat," you believe him because he's done it nine times.

**The Road Runner metaphor is better than he knows.** The cartoon isn't just about falling — it's about the *delay* between crossing the cliff and falling. Raford uses this for the SaaS industry (they haven't looked down), but it applies equally to employment, education, and regulation. The whole economy is Wile E. Coyote right now, and each sector has its own gravity schedule.

**The essay dodges its own strongest question.** Raford asks "what are we actually paying for?" and breaks down SaaS into its functional cores (Salesforce = database + forms + workflows), but he doesn't follow through on what this means for the *people* inside those companies. [[Cyborgs Will Kill the Corporation]] takes the same Coasean logic further — it's not just SaaS products that dissolve when transaction costs collapse, it's the firm itself.

**The "Make It So Camp" is the most underdeveloped idea.** Raford mentions it in one sentence, but it's the positive program the essay lacks. Teaching knowledge workers to be AI "captains" through articulation rather than programming is a genuinely novel pedagogy. The essay would be stronger with even a paragraph on what this looks like in practice.

**Compared to [[AI Killing B2B SaaS]], Raford's essay is better argued but less actionable.** [[AI Killing B2B SaaS]] gives SaaS companies a strategy (become a system of record). Raford gives them a tombstone. Both are right about the threat, but Raford's honesty — "we don't know" what comes next — is more intellectually satisfying and less useful to a CEO on Monday morning.

**The "meeting could have been a prompt" meme undersells what it actually means.** If coordination collapses into a prompt, the prompt becomes the coordination artifact. This connects directly to [[Specifications as the Product]] and [[The Plan Is the Program]] — the durable artifact shifts from code to intent. But Raford doesn't explore what happens when prompts are the new meeting minutes, the new contracts, the new source of truth. [[Long Live Systems of Record]]'s question — "where does the truth live?" — becomes urgent when the truth is a prompt someone wrote at 2am.

**The GAI vs. AGI framing is useful and likely to be ignored.** The AI discourse is addicted to the AGI question because it's dramatic. Raford's point — we don't need Skynet, we just need generic intelligence as cheap infrastructure — is more sober and more correct. But "GAI" won't catch on because it doesn't end the world or save it. It just changes it.

**This pairs well with** [[AI Killing B2B SaaS]] (same thesis from the SaaS side), [[Cyborgs Will Kill the Corporation]] (Coasean follow-through Raford doesn't complete), [[Vibe Coding and the Maker Movement]] (the taste problem Raford's builders will hit), [[Minions — Stripe's One-Shot Coding Agents]] (one-shotting as operational practice at scale), [[Opus 4.5 Changes Everything]] (same "everything changed" moment, different author), [[Specifications as the Product]] (what the durable artifact becomes when code is disposable), [[The Plan Is the Program]] (intent as atomic unit of work), [[Radical Accountability]] (taste as the last differentiator when execution is free), [[Two Kinds of User Are Emerging]] (Raford is the archetype of the non-technical power user), and [[Awesome Vibez]] (the community infrastructure that made this possible).
