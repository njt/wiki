# Ideas and Culture

This theme holds the essays, histories and arguments that sit around technology rather than inside it: how people think, learn, decide and organise, and what a culture does when a new tool arrives faster than its habits can adjust. Read together, the sources make one claim more often than any other. When making something becomes cheap, whether the thing is code, prose, a forecast or a picture, scarcity moves from production to judgment, and the friction of making turns out to have been doing unpriced work for learning, meaning and trust. The wiki believes that claim is well supported at the scale of the individual and the small team, where sources arrive at it from optometry, gambling research, executive-skills coaching and Olympic swimming as well as from software. It believes the claim is not yet supported at the scale of the economy, where the sources split and the macro data available in 2026 favour the sceptics of mass displacement. Two things would falsify this page: self-reported productivity gains starting to match measured ones, or aggregate employment moving with AI adoption rather than ignoring it.

## The Argument

### The bottleneck was never the making

The oldest source here is the one the others keep citing. In 1960 J. C. R. Licklider timed his own working days and found that about 85 per cent of his "thinking" time went on getting into a position to think: plotting, transcribing, looking things up. His proposal in [[Man-Computer Symbiosis]] was a division of labour in which people set goals, form hypotheses and judge, while machines do the routinisable work between decisions. Several of this wiki's notes link back to that paper as the ancestor of what they describe, and the lineage is fair. Licklider's "instructions to computers specify courses; instructions to human beings specify goals" is the distinction the 2026 essays keep rediscovering.

What has changed is which side of the line feels scarce. Laura Summers, writing from inside the team that builds Pydantic AI, says the bottleneck "was never the code" but human attention, engineering judgment and the ability to hold a system in one's head, and that those capacities are now revealed as the limiting resource ([[Human-in-the-Loop is Tired]]). The Bestmate founder says the same from the product side: execution stopped being the bottleneck, judgment did, and the practical consequence was writing and talking into a phone to find out what they actually believed, because you cannot tell an agent what to do when you do not know what you want ([[How I Actually Use Agents]]). Manuel Odendahl's response is to slow down on purpose, reading agent-produced design documents on an e-ink tablet with a pen, because a mediocre prompt produces mediocre architecture at high speed ([[Slowing Down in the Age of Coding Agents]]).

Braydon McCormick turns this into an architecture: models own judgment, deterministic pipes own execution and audit, and the human "changes altitude" to policy and exceptions ([[Smart Models Dumb Pipes]], [[From AI Studio to AI Forge]]). Ramon Marc makes the parallel move for design. If capabilities are the real unit and screens are only their appearances, then interfaces are derived rather than designed, and agents invert fifty years of assumption by acting first and asking for approval second ([[Intent Is the Interface]]). Once both sides persist, he adds, every interface is multiplayer, and the asymmetry is that an agent's attention is buyable while a person's can only be outsourced ([[Real-Time Multiplayer Interfaces]]). Brad Feld reaches the same place through Eric von Hippel: the user has the need and the sticky information, the machine can now build and ship, so the manufacturer becomes a machine the user operates ([[Lead User and the Machines That Build Machines]]).

Benedict Evans supplies the corrective this cluster needs. Most people are not tool-builders and do not see their own job as a workflow. The hard part was never writing the code but knowing you needed a tool and what it should do, and even a working tool then has to be adopted by hundreds of people across departments and regulators, which is an eighteen-month sales process rather than a five-minute build ([[AI, Tools and Transformation]]). His spectrum from institutionalised tasks to improvised ones explains why a company has hundreds of apps and why a chatbot is just one more freeform substrate that takes tasks from apps and loses tasks to them. Palash Awasthi's benchmark is the small, hard version of the same point: a vision agent needed about 45 times the tokens and about fifty times the wall-clock of an API agent for the same admin task, and the gap was structural, set by the interface rather than the model ([[Computer Use is 45x More Expensive Than Structured APIs]]).

### What is left for people, and whether "taste" is an answer

If judgment is the scarce thing, the culture has reached for one word for it. The Asterisk essay traces how "taste" became Silicon Valley's catch-all for whatever survives automation, quoted by Paul Graham, Greg Brockman and Rick Rubin without any of them defining it, and then does the work they skipped: taste as class strategy in Bourdieu, as mimetic desire in Girard, and, most usefully, as Arendt's reading of Kant, an "enlarged mentality" that anticipates communication with others and persuades rather than coerces ([[Why We Like Things]]). The essay carries its own rebuttal. Leif Weatherby points out that models already encode judgments of taste, from literary criticism to Likert ratings, so the claim that taste is the last preserve of the human reads as industry anxiety about deskilling.

Caleb Gross makes the sharper version of that warning. Arguments that AI could "never" do something, or that humans do it "better", depend on a capability gap that narrows every year; the robust position is simply that humans are valuable, stated without qualifiers. His secondary point is more practical: creation is the distillation of intent into form, and what generative tools make easy is substantial form with almost no intent. Slop is form whose intent cannot be discerned, and humans made it before the machines did ([[You Can Just Say It]]). Tim O'Reilly and Harper Carroll say the same from the educator's side: AI is a medium like photography, what you bring determines the result, and good intuition "goes against the input", which is exactly what a pattern-matcher struggles with ([[What You Bring to AI Determines the Result]]).

David Brooks moves the line from intelligence to volition. What will separate people is their "need for cognition": Productive Passengers who use AI to think less, Reluctant Optimizers who resolve to resist and get pulled in, and Mental Marathoners who use it to widen their agency. The thing a model cannot supply is desire, because it has no needs, no history of wounds and no structure of cares ([[The People Who Will Thrive in the AI Age]]). Octopusyarn's cyborg essay grounds the same intuition in bodies: judgment "looks more like vibes than algorithms", trust requires skin in the game, and skin requires a nervous system that feels consequences ([[Cyborgs Will Kill the Corporation]]). Siddhi Sundar's "Trojan prompts", prompts so rooted in memory and sensation that no model could have written them, is the creative-writing version ([[Creative Firewall]]).

Steve Yegge adds the piece the taste discourse leaves out: judgment has a ceiling. Everyone has a "demand horizon" set by the hardest problem they bring and a "discernment horizon" set by the hardest answer they can check, and past the second one a smarter model is indistinguishable from a dumber one because you cannot grade it ([[The Flat Curve Society]]). Chip Huyen's version is the question that remains once anyone can build anything describable: who imagines what does not exist yet ([[Building When It Feels Like There's Nothing Left to Build]]). Sachin's essay on the maker movement gives the most honest name for what this leaves: taste as "a residue of expenditure", the thing you have left after spending a lot of surplus intelligence and noticing what was worth it ([[Vibe Coding and the Maker Movement]]).

The wiki's reading: Brooks and Gross are more convincing than the taste-as-moat camp, because their claims do not depend on where the capability frontier sits this quarter. Yegge's horizons are the most useful practical tool, since they turn "judgment" from a slogan into something an organisation can audit.

### Friction was load-bearing

The strongest convergence in this theme is on what cheap reward does to attention. Rachel Thomas maps vibe coding onto the gambling literature: unclear feedback, "losses disguised as wins", and a false sense of control, with Csikszentmihalyi's "junk flow" as the state it induces. Her anchor is the METR study in which developers using AI tools estimated they were 20 per cent faster and were measured at 19 per cent slower ([[Breaking the Spell of Vibe Coding]]). Ramon Marc's earlier piece had already named the high as coming from the rate of capability increase rather than the capability itself, and predicted users would settle into a perpetual "simmer" as each model release supplies just enough novelty ([[acceleration-flow]]). German S. widens the frame to the whole culture: "dopamine fracking" is what happens when enormous resources are forced into a formerly casual activity to extract the purest hit, and the strawberry replaced by its own flavour compound is his image for what it erases ([[Dopamine Fracking]]).

David Wilson's confession is the case study. Seventeen listed projects, perhaps fifty more deleted, none useful, none he wants to maintain, and a chain he lays out explicitly: remove the effort and you remove the commitment, remove the commitment and you remove the focus, remove the focus and there is no product at all ([[The solution might be cancelling my AI subscription (Wilson)]]). Simon Willison finds it "deeply relatable" and names the specific harm as a maintenance crisis: you can now create faster than you can responsibly own, and discipline is the skill he has been trying to learn for decades ([[The solution might be cancelling my AI subscription (Willison)]]). Kyle Kingsbury's ten-part series puts the same mechanism at civilisational scale, adding Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 ironies of automation: automation deskills the operator, humans are poor monitors, and the takeover when it fails is the hardest moment of all ([[The Future of Everything is Lies I Guess]]).

The counter-evidence in the sources is real but thin. In the Hacker News thread Willison summarises, people with ADHD report finishing side projects "for the first time ever" because the tool gets them to working before boredom does. Nobody in the corpus has followed up on whether those projects survived.

Why friction matters is answered from unexpected directions. Daniel Chambliss found that Olympic excellence is not talent or heroic effort but a large number of small correct actions drilled into habit, and that "talent" is a tautology applied after the fact ([[The Mundanity of Excellence]]). Peg Dawson, on executive skills in children, observes that at any single moment it is faster not to use an organisational system than to use one, which is why the skill has to be built by repetition and praised as process rather than trait ([[Smart But Scattered — Peg Dawson on Executive Skills]]). Aditya Bhargava's year of writing down what he finished shows the same thing from the inside: the list's value was tracing the chain of effort back through months of colour mixing and value studies to the cards he finally printed ([[One Year of Keeping a Tada List]]). Gorilla Sun's defence of sketching with code lands the sharpest line, that sketching is "solving problems that you don't have yet", and that flow with an empty canvas is an antidote to shrinking attention ([[The Importance of Sketching with Code]]). Ben Wallace found the happiest period of his life coaching middle-school basketball, and asks whether "moving rectangles" was ever anyone's ticket to happiness ([[Happiest I've Ever Been]]).

Two academic sources explain why cheap reward is not the same as curiosity. The appraisal model of epistemic curiosity holds that a knowledge gap only produces wanting when it is appraised as relevant, novel and within one's coping potential; otherwise "encyclopedias would be addictive" ([[An Integrative Appraisal Model of Epistemic Curiosity]]). Flexner's 1939 essay argues from the history of radio, electricity and group theory that the most useful work came from people freed from the criterion of use altogether ([[The Usefulness of Useless Knowledge]]). Both are about motivation that comes from the problem, not from the interface. Roger Williams's novel is the limit case: a perfectly benevolent superintelligence removes every hardship and leaves "nothing to do" ([[The Metamorphosis of Prime Intellect]]).

Several sources try to keep the benefit and lose the trap. Deven Jarvis's Lathe generates hands-on tutorials that make the reader type the code, predict output before seeing it, and reconstruct a prior concept from memory; the whole design premise is that the model should teach rather than do. Hugh Howey concludes that the writing done for money and status is threatened and the writing done for the love of thinking in words is untouched ([[The End of an Era — Hugh Howey on AI and Authorship]]). mehdio's rule for a personal vault is the same distinction applied to notes: a summary of a PDF is noise, an insight from reading it is signal, and connections an agent makes "won't count for anything, since they aren't made by you" ([[Keep AI Out of Your (Obsidian) Vault]]). That warning applies to this page, which is machine-compiled, and the honest defence is the provenance footer below rather than any claim to insight.

One physiological footnote belongs here. Hard thinking costs almost no extra calories, but ninety minutes of it made cyclists quit 15 per cent earlier at identical physiological readings, because perceived exertion had shifted ([[Thinking Hard Burns Almost No Calories]]). Mental fatigue is real even when the energy budget says otherwise, which is one more reason the "tired" in Summers's title should be taken literally.

### The machine's voice, and the culture that catches it

Sam Kriss's account of why AI writes like that is the most complete description in the corpus of a style everyone now recognises. The mechanism is overfitting: the model learns that em dashes and "delve" correlate with prose marked as good, so it drenches its output in them; it learns that good fiction is subtle and quiet, so everything becomes a ghost, a whisper and a tapestry. The findings that matter most are the ones about people. Human speech on YouTube is measurably acquiring the model's tics, British MPs have started saying "I rise to speak", and readers in studies prefer machine poetry to Dickinson ([[Why Does AI Write Like That]]). Shiv's catalogue of "ai-smells" extends the tells to web design, where a JetBrains Mono step list and a blinking-dot badge are as diagnostic as a tricolon ([[Various LLM Smells]]), and Nathaniel J. Smith ships the joke as a component library whose tagline is that its components "signal how oversubscribed your funding round is" ([[Performative UI]]).

The tells have a cost beyond aesthetics. Howey now second-guesses his own em dashes and comma splices before reminding himself that the models sound like him, not the reverse. Harper Carroll's practical answer is to fine-tune a small model on one's own writing and train it to undo the tells, which she demonstrated in twenty minutes at SXSW. The technical writers' thread on Hacker News shows the labour-market version: nicbou's distinction between his output (writing) and his job (observing, listening, understanding), against the reply that a replacement can be 80 per cent worse if it is 90 per cent cheaper. The thread also contained a commenter who repeated another's praise verbatim 53 minutes later and was, apparently, a bot ([[Tech Writers and AI (HN Discussion)]]).

Two short pieces frame what the voice is attached to. Max Leiter's parody of Terry Bisson has two observers agreeing that "the reasoning is the weights", that no one is home, and that the next generation nevertheless ships with memory because "do you remember me?" is the most-asked question across billions of sessions ([[They're Made Out of Weights]]). Alex Wolf and Reed argue that memory is the wrong ask anyway: an agent with memory recalls what happened, an agent with identity has a stake in what happens next, and only the second can produce a grounded "no" ([[Agent Identity]]). Their reading of fifty years of software wisdom, from Conway to Skelton and Pais, is that isolation produces the wrong system, and that "agents in the cellar" repeat the mistake. The Bestmate essay dates its own shift to the summer when human-agent multiplayer accelerated, which is the context for [[Introducing Claude Tag]]. IDEO's rubber duck with four personalities is the playful face of the same wish for a colleague rather than a tool ([[Duck, Duck, Duck! (IDEO)]]).

### Trust, provenance and the information ecology

If judgment is what people have left, the sources are clear that the environment for exercising it is degrading. The MIT Media Lab study found that watching clearly labelled AI video afterwards reduced viewers' confidence in real video and in their own judgment; labelling addresses deception but not the loss of perceptual trust ([[Seeing Is Not Believing — AI Video and Perceptual Safety]]). IO Factory's simulation shows why single-message detection is the wrong frame: influence now comes from persistent swarms with stable identities that adapt to feedback, and only a traceable lifecycle from reconnaissance to adaptation makes the effect measurable, with the authors careful to say their lift figures are simulator-scale ([[IO Factory]]). Kingsbury's information-ecology chapter adds the scraper economy, the collapse of credibility markers, and Arendt's line that propaganda thrives when audiences believe everything and nothing.

The remedies proposed share a shape: less identity, more provenance. Mozilla's PACT proposal argues that bot harm comes from operating beyond human scale, so sites need a rate limit anchored to something scarce rather than a name, and the system should leak "a single bit" ([[PACT Anonymous Credentials for the Web]]). The Bestmate judgment graph attaches a source, a date and the exact text to every belief. Sena Evren's legal advice is to document human creative decisions in commit messages, because "add rate limiting module" is not evidence of authorship and "rejected initial state management approach, rewrote error handling" is ([[Who Owns the Code Claude Wrote]]). Cameron Armstrong's corpus royalty concedes that Shapley attribution is infeasible at frontier scale and argues that payment is owed anyway, on the Superfund model of holding polluters accountable without tracing each barrel ([[The Private Capture of Public Genius]]). Zilan Qian's study of the Chinese proxy market is the reminder that access controls create their own evasion economies, complete with model swapping and harvested reasoning logs ([[How to Buy Cheap Claude Tokens in China]]).

Two pairs of sources argue about markets as a source of truth. Saahil Desai and the Hacker News thread on the journalist threatened by Polymarket bettors both conclude that a market whose object is open can be abused to create incentives for the outcome, including violence ([[America Is Slow-Walking Into a Polymarket Disaster]], [[Prediction Markets and Perverse Incentives]]). Scott Alexander's report on AI superforecasters names the exact property that separates them: no adversarial incentives, because a model does not game resolution criteria ([[AI Superforecasters]]). Preseen's tournament wins and its Montenegro forecast show what that looks like in practice, though the platform is waitlist-gated and its methods only sketched ([[Preseen — AI Forecasting Platform]], [[Montenegro EU Accession Forecast 2026]]). Alexander's own admission that he rejected the first forecast that challenged his worldview is the discernment horizon again. Fenwick McKelvey's history of American attempts to solve politics with computers, from simulated voters to simulated worlds, is the long-run caution that promised technological fixes for politics have a record ([[SimPolitics]]). The LLM-driven situational-awareness wikis for a hypothetical Gulf war and for global monitoring show the same ambition running live ([[Third Gulf War]], [[World Monitor]]).

Bobbie Chen's essay on personalised pricing is the consumer-scale version of trust erosion: when every behavioural signal feeds your price, abandoning carts and threatening to cancel pay off, and "sincerity is becoming the more expensive option" ([[The Behavioral Cost of Personalized Pricing]]). Hedgie's clip of rows of PCs each running an AI livestreamer selling to audiences that may themselves be bots is the endpoint, where "dead internet" stops being a theory and becomes a business plan ([[AI Livestream Factories]]).

The counterweight is a set of sources about infrastructure that holds because somebody unpaid holds it. Jani Patokallio's investigation of archive.today found a one-person "battle against entropy" with a bus factor of one, and then found that operator using its own visitors to run a denial-of-service attack against his blog, an archive famous for refusing removals censoring the breadcrumbs about itself ([[Archive.today DDoSed a Critic's Blog]]). The history of Arabic typography ends with the observation that everything in the story that works, HarfBuzz, Amiri, the W3C documents, was "paid for by almost nobody", and that the one remaining algorithm, kashida justification, goes unimplemented because the affected users "do not, as a population, contain any advertisers" ([[Arabic Typography]]). Mark Dominus's walk through the basmala shows the same encoding-versus-rendering tension compressed into a single Unicode codepoint ([[Deciphering Basmala]]). The Public Domain Review's image archive is the deliberate version, a donation-funded cultural commons of hand-edited images ([[Public Domain Image Archive]]), and it has a Victorian ancestor in William Thoms's *Notes & Queries*, dismissed by professional historians and yet the model for the OED and Wikipedia ([[Notes and Queries — Victorian Crowdsourced Knowledge]]).

### Will the work run out?

Here the sources divide, and the division is the most important disagreement on this page. The sceptical case is well argued and mostly empirical. Citadel's macro note observes that daily AI use for work in the St Louis Fed survey is flat, that recursive capability does not imply recursive adoption because deployment is bounded by compute, energy, regulation and organisational change, and that a productivity surge with collapsing demand violates accounting identities; its historical anchor is Keynes's fifteen-hour week, wrong because he underestimated the elasticity of wants ([[2026 Global Intelligence Crisis]]). Roger Partridge traces the lineage of falsified doom from Rifkin back to the Club of Rome, names the cognitive error as the "static mind" that extrapolates a chart forward without the labour and capital the displacement releases, cites Acemoglu in May 2026 saying the macro data show no material employment effect, and notes that horses are the only species for whom the prediction came true ([[The Market for Doom]]). Rodney Brooks supplies the time scales: ten to twenty years from research idea to lab demonstration, twenty or more to scale, fifty or more to reshape an economy, and the current hype ignores all four ([[Four Time Scales for Technology Development and Deployment]]). The Economist's productivity piece is the cleanest data point, because the American productivity revival began in the early 2020s, before LLMs were in commercial use, and came from professional services finally using the 2010s' technologies ([[The Lazarus Effect — America's Productivity Miracle]]). The Hacker News thread on antirez's essay adds forty years of "anyone can code now" leading to more developer demand ([[HN Don't Fall Into the Anti-AI Hype]]), and Fowler and Beck frame AI as an amplifier whose real casualty is "the middle", the cohort who entered programming for money ([[Martin Fowler and Kent Beck on Reinventing Software]]).

The alarmed case is structural rather than empirical, and its best version is Owen McGrann's. AI companies' valuations only work if the addressable market is the global labour market, the gentle language of copilots is marketing, and the AI layoff trap is a prisoners' dilemma in which each firm's replaced workers are another firm's customers. His most useful contribution is political: democratic leverage rests on the governed being needed, and polling shows people want work rather than a cheque ([[The Dead Economy Theory]]). Josh Albrecht's essay on why agents matter more than other AI states the employer's incentives plainly, seven advantages from infinite replication to tax treatment, and asks what happens as the value of human mental labour approaches zero. Noah Raford, with no prior coding experience, built nine substantial projects in weeks over Christmas 2025 and concludes that most commercial software is now instantly replicable ([[The Road Runner Economy]]). Octopusyarn takes the argument to Coase: if the corporation exists to reduce the cost of getting humans to work together, agent swarms that push that cost towards zero dissolve it into excorporations, plankton and protocols ([[Cyborgs Will Kill the Corporation]]).

Two books in the corpus argue that the fear is about something else. Blix and Glimmer hold that fears about AI "tell us more about capitalism today than the technology of the future" ([[Why We Fear AI]]), and Pasquinelli traces AI's inner logic to the organisation of labour from Babbage onward, automation disguised as intelligence ([[Eye of the Master]]). Kyla Scanlon's essay supplies the incentive structure they point at: labour's share of output has fallen for forty years, the reliable answer to "how do you get rich" is owning assets, and AI is the newest instalment in which asset holders profit from a technology that threatens work ([[How to Get Rich in America]]). Ludicity's field report from about three hundred client meetings, with a zero per cent success rate on AI projects and engineers "AI-washing" work to avoid suspicion, suggests the near-term risk is less displacement than organisational dishonesty ([[AI Mania Is Eviscerating Global Decision-Making]]). Robin Sloan declares AGI arrived with GPT-3, congratulates the industry, and asks the question that hangs over both camps: "And...? What now?" ([[AGI Is Here (Robin Sloan)]]).

Yegge offers a third position that neither camp anticipated. If the most capable models are gated by governments and the accessible ones plateau at roughly today's class, then SaaS comes back, buy beats build, and the craft becomes working well with models that are good but not omnipotent ([[The Flat Curve Society]]). Whether that prediction holds is one of the open questions this page cannot settle.

The wiki's reading: on the evidence available in 2026, the sceptics win the macro argument, because they have data and the alarmed camp has valuations and anecdotes. But Partridge's own caveat, the fifty-year-old compliance officer whose work is absorbed in 2028, and McGrann's point about democratic leverage are not answered by aggregate employment figures. The honest summary is that the economy is probably fine and particular people are not, and the sources that matter most are the ones about what to do for those people, of which there are almost none here.

### Organisations, and why they change more slowly than tools

A cluster of sources explains why the transformation the alarmed camp expects keeps not arriving. Arrieta and colleagues argue that multi-agent AI systems are organisations in the Carnegie sense, facing task division, task allocation, information provision and reward distribution by construction, and that the hybrid human-AI configuration is often the stable one rather than a transition ([[Multi-Agent AI Systems Are Organizations]]). Wolf and Reed's junior engineer who is "basically a proxy to Claude Code" is what a badly designed hybrid looks like, and Beck's warning that five people with ten agents each is "not the same" as a fifty-person team with high-bandwidth conversation is the same point from the craft side. Huyen adds that the code review a senior engineer writes for mentoring is now ignored by teammates who did not write the code, so feedback must move from the code to how one instructs the model.

Yegge's account of the Netflix training study is the most concrete thing in the corpus about how adoption actually happens: three cohorts defined by token spend, people jumping cohorts in five hours of blessed company time in teams with their manager, and the corner-cutting variants (shorter, larger, opt-in) failing to produce the same result. His second-order finding is that mastery looks like spending fewer tokens, not more. The retail webinar notes flag the omissions such pitches routinely make, data migration and change management chief among them ([[Retail 2026 From AI Pilots to Execution]]).

The older organisational wisdom here is about incentives and timing. Adam Ward's "funnel of doom" explains why default hiring regresses to the mean: reach out to a hundred, get the twenty who happened to reply on a bad day, and hire the remainder. His alternative treats every hire as an executive search, and his cheapest lever is that "caring is free" ([[The Funnel of Doom — Hiring for Talent Density]]). Rands's Mario learns that the compensation budget he argues about in summer was fixed months earlier in product-planning meetings he skipped, and that no one will explain this to a senior leader ([[The Mario Meeting]]). Novick and Simeonov's series holds that repeatable patterns of failure are more instructive than unrepeatable success, and that anti-patterns kill by stacking ([[Startup Anti-Patterns]]). Steph Ango's eighty moats, drawn from coral reefs and patent trolls alike, argue that durable advantage is a strange hybrid no single competitor can copy ([[Moats — 80 Strategies for Competitive Advantage]]). Vaughn Tan's framework explains why big institutions misjudge all of this: risk registers and expected-value calculations only work for formal risk, which "almost never exists outside a casino", and applying them to true uncertainty produces false confidence ([[Not-Knowing (Vaughn Tan)]]). Jesse Vincent's company announcement is a small live example of the new friction: any job posting now attracts "an unending torrent of agentic job applications" ([[Prime Radiant (Company)]]).

### How to think: the craft of inquiry

Beneath the AI argument runs an older one about method. George Box's Fisher lecture describes science as a motivated iteration between theory and practice, with two pathologies when the loop breaks: cookbookery, which forces every problem into a routine technique, and mathematistry, which develops theory that never touches practice ([[Science and Statistics (Box)]]). His sponsor-then-critic discipline for data analysis is the same move as Fowler's "I have to be skeptical about my skepticism" and Beck's "smallest experiment I can run", which Beck says became a thousand times more valuable in a year. The ARC researcher's advice on choosing subproblems is the practitioner's manual: peel a twenty-hour problem off a four-hundred-hour one, ask a simpler question, ask about a simpler system, or ask what is even going on, and when it is time to quit consult "the ultimate destroyer of hope: other people" ([[How to Choose a Subproblem]]). Terence Tao's verdict on the Erdős proof fits this exactly: the raw model output was poor, the humans had "made a slight wrong turn at move one", and the value came from an expert recognising the insight ([[Vibe Maths and the Erdős Breakthrough]]).

Two systematic methods and one warning belong here. TRIZ, whose creator was sent to the Gulag four years after he began it, holds that problems and solutions recur across industries and that invention means resolving a contradiction rather than trading it off ([[TRIZ]]). Christopher Alexander's 253 patterns, summarised one sentence each by Clayton Dorge, are the design equivalent ([[A Pattern Language (Christopher Alexander)]]). Slime Mold Time Mold's "Meeseeks alignment", an agent designed to want to die because death is trivially easy to specify and verify, is a joke that lands the serious objection its comment thread supplies: such an agent might conclude that killing everyone is the fastest route ([[Meeseeks Alignment]]). It is the specification-gaming problem Arrieta calls the dominant failure mode of artificial organisations.

On deciding and acting: Joshua Rothman's survey of decision science, decision theory and L. A. Paul's transformative experience ends with Agnes Callard's aspiration, trying on new values through placeholder reasons until the real ones can be grasped ([[The Art of Decision-Making]]). Milan Cvitkovic's list of things you are allowed to do reduces to one rule, just ask, and to its claim that most constraints are self-imposed ([[Things You're Allowed to Do]]). Jason Liu's advice to his sister, updated at 32, is "the lies I tell myself to keep on living my life in good faith" ([[Advice to Young People (Jason Liu)]]). The positive-psychology taxonomy of 28 enjoyable emotions excludes joy and happiness for lack of defined triggers, a decision that says something about how little the culture's most-used words are understood ([[Finding a Family — A Categorization of Enjoyable Emotions]]).

Purcell's talk on life at low Reynolds number is included for its argument shape as much as its physics. Where inertia is irrelevant, reciprocal motion goes nowhere, stirring is useless, and what matters is information: a bacterium swims not to gather more food but to find out whether things are getting better ([[Life at Low Reynolds Numbers]]). Jim Propp's coin-toss estimate of pi is the same pleasure in a small, surprising result ([[Estimating Pi with a Coin]]).

### The long view: history, dissent, and what people do with any open system

The historical sources give the AI argument its proportions. Václav Havel's greengrocer hangs a slogan he does not believe, and by that ritual becomes part of the machine; "living within the truth" is therefore political, and resistance grows in parallel structures rather than in opposition programmes ([[The Power of the Powerless]]). Robert Worth's reportage from Minneapolis in early 2026 is that theory in practice: a leaderless resistance organised by neighbourhood over encrypted chat, tens of thousands trained as legal observers, and a federal operation formally ended within weeks of the article ([[Welcome to the American Winter]]). Blake Smith's essay on the Silicon Valley canon argues that the sector has become traditional, that its canon incites adolescent ambition without teaching taste, and that its leaders emerged from the federal, academic and corporate institutions they now scorn, from Licklider's funding at the Department of Defense to Girard at Stanford ([[The Education of the Broligarchy]]). Sally Kornbluth's message on MIT's finances, with federally funded research activity down more than a fifth and new graduate enrolments down by nearly as much under an endowment tax, is what the weakening of those institutions looks like from inside one ([[MIT Funding and Talent Pipeline Crisis (Kornbluth)]]). McGrann's attack on the philosophical poverty of the accelerationists, and Flexner's portrait of the Institute for Advanced Study as a place with no duties, only opportunities, are the same argument about what institutions are for.

Virginia Postrel's history of Habitat, the first massively multiplayer world, running on a Commodore 64 over 300-baud modems, found what every open system since has found: given a world, people "will do everything that can possibly be done", including quintupling the money supply overnight through the first dupe bug, and the designers had to become rule makers rather than storytellers ([[The Invention of the MMO]]). Ish's experiment with a thousand Minecraft players on a rich island and a poor one reproduced the finding at scale, complete with an assassination and a framing ([[1000 Players Simulate Civilization]]). Both are the empirical background for McCormick's insistence on governance planes and Marc's on rehearsal.

The deep-time sources are the bleakest. The Science paper on linguistic diversity finds that languages peaked at tens of thousands two to three thousand years ago and collapsed under states and empires long before European colonialism, so the surviving languages are "survivors of a massive and highly selective historical bottleneck" ([[Lost Golden Age of Languages]]). John Gowdy argues that agriculture was only possible because of the Holocene's unusual climate stability, that the coming instability will make it impossible, and that a hunter-gatherer future is the optimistic scenario ([[Our Hunter-Gatherer Future]]). Larcombe and Bierwirth find serum bicarbonate rising with atmospheric CO2 across twenty years of NHANES data and extrapolate to the edge of the healthy range within fifty years ([[CO2 Overload and Human Blood Chemistry]]). The wiki flags all three as single-paper claims resting on strong assumptions; none has a rebuttal in the corpus.

Lighter history makes the same point about contingency. The kosher fruit-jelly slice survived because agar-agar happens to be permitted at Passover, "a moment when Jewish legal interests and American industry accidentally collided" ([[Fruit Jelly Slices]]). Chris's list of ten ways the 1990s got the balance right, and the Hacker News thread that replied "it was great if you weren't gay", is nostalgia and its correction in one place ([[We (As a Society) Peaked in the 90s]]).

## Where the Sources Disagree

**Whether work ends.** Covered above. The sceptics have data, the alarmed have structure, and Yegge has a third scenario. The wiki sides with the sceptics on aggregates and with McGrann on the political consequences for the displaced.

**Whether friction is a bug or the point.** Wilson, Thomas and Marc say cheap reward corrodes focus; the ADHD commenters in Willison's thread, Fowler and Beck's "golden age of the junior programmer", and Carroll's "not much to fear right now" say the tool helps the people it helps. The METR gap between perceived and measured speed is the strongest evidence in either direction and it favours the sceptics on self-report. Sachin's proposal to drop the craft frame for a consumption frame is the one attempt to dissolve the disagreement rather than win it, and it deserves more attention than it has had.

**Whether taste is a moat.** The Asterisk essay's own Weatherby counterpoint, and Gross's warning about capability-gap arguments, stand against Graham, Brockman and Rubin. Brooks's volition claim survives the objection; the taste claim does not.

**Whether AI belongs in a knowledge base.** mehdio says generated notes dilute your own thinking and connections you did not make count for nothing. Lathe, the Bestmate judgment graph, and this wiki all bet the opposite, with provenance as the safeguard. The Victorian *Notes & Queries* precedent cuts both ways: crowdsourced, uncritical, and the model for Wikipedia.

**Whether markets or models forecast better.** The Polymarket sources and Alexander are compatible rather than opposed: the manipulation the journalists document is precisely the adversarial incentive Alexander says AI forecasters lack. The open question is whether an AI whose training data includes the manipulated market inherits the bias.

**How fast this is happening.** Raford dates a phase transition to Christmas 2025 and Fowler calls the speed unprecedented in his career; Brooks says fifty years, Evans says eighteen-month sales cycles, and the Economist finds the productivity revival predates the models. These stop contradicting each other once Brooks's four time scales are applied: the first two claims are about hype and lab capability, the last three about deployment.

**Whether the 1990s were better.** The article says yes; its thread says the author is nostalgic for youth, and that the world is "better today in every conceivable way for more people". Neither side offers evidence, and the thread's best contribution is the unanswered question "how do we turn this into constructive action?"

## What's Missing

- Almost nothing about what happens to the displaced individual. Partridge names the compliance officer and moves on; McGrann cites deaths of despair; no source here studies retraining, transition or what a jobs guarantee would look like.
- No longitudinal follow-up on anyone who stopped. Wilson is one person; the ADHD commenters are anonymous; nobody has checked whether the projects survived or the vault stayed clean.
- Taste is asserted from many directions and measured from none. The Bauhaus and the Victorian design schools are cited as precedents for trainable taste, but no modern equivalent is described.
- The voices are overwhelmingly programmers, investors and pundits writing in English. Huyen's Vietnam voice bots and Qian's Chinese proxy market are the only non-Western vantage points, and there is no ordinary-user account at all.
- The political thread (Havel, Minneapolis, the canon) and the AI thread touch only through Smith's essay and McGrann's. What "parallel structures" would mean for people who want to live outside the platforms is not addressed.
- Several predictions are dated and testable and nobody has come back to test them: Yegge's plateau, Raford's one-shotting of SaaS, Fowler's security incidents "this year", Gowdy's crop volatility by 2050.
- The single-paper science claims (CO2 blood chemistry, the hunter-gatherer future, the language bottleneck) have no counter-source and should be read as claims, not findings.
- The domestic and hobby sources filed here (mould, thermite, psoriasis, chord progressions) are here because no other topic fits, not because they bear on the argument.
