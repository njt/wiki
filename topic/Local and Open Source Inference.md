# Local and Open Source Inference

Local inference has crossed a genuine quality threshold. The signal is not a benchmark score — it is that [[A Few Words on DS4|antirez]], a person who knows what frontier model quality feels like, built his own inference engine and then used it instead of Claude or GPT for his own serious work. He describes the local experience as "a lot more B than A" — closer to frontier than to toy. The same trajectory is visible at every scale: a 27B model retaining 90% of baseline quality on a phone ([[Bonsai 27B]]), a 3B safety classifier matching models seven times its size on a single GPU ([[Shieldstral]]), and systematic benchmarks showing self-hosted models serving dozens of concurrent developer sessions at usable quality ([[GPU Self-Hosting for Coding Agents]]). Local inference is not yet a wholesale replacement for cloud APIs — the economics are unsettled, the best tools are Mac-first, and multi-model orchestration on local hardware is unsolved — but the default assumption that "serious work requires a cloud model" no longer holds. Underneath the models and the cost curves is a deeper conviction, articulated in one form or another by nearly every project in this survey: AI is too critical to be just a provided service.

## The Argument

### The Quality Threshold Has Been Crossed

[[DS4 (DwarfStar 4)]] is a 65,000-line C inference engine that antirez built in about a week with GPT 5.5's assistance. It targets a single model — DeepSeek V4 Flash, a 284B-parameter Mixture-of-Experts architecture — and hardcodes the model shape as compile-time constants: 43 layers, 256 routed experts (6 active per token plus 1 shared), 128-token sliding window attention, and 4-stream hyper-connections. It runs on high-end Macs and GPU-in-a-box setups like the DGX Spark, requiring 96–128 GB of RAM.

The engine's key technical move is asymmetric quantization. Routed experts — which account for 48 of the model's 49 parameters in DeepSeek V4 — are compressed to IQ2_XXS for gate/up projections and Q2_K for down projections. Shared experts, attention projections, the router, and embeddings stay at full F16 precision. This is what makes 284B parameters fit in ~96 GB with usable quality: quantize the bulk, preserve the critical paths. The engine also treats the disk KV cache as a first-class citizen, checkpointing session state so that long conversations survive restarts and context reuse is keyed by SHA1 of the rendered prompt prefix.

In [[A Few Words on DS4]], antirez is explicit about what changed. This is, he writes, "the first time since he began experimenting with local inference that he's relied on a local model for serious tasks he would normally ask Claude or GPT." He calls it "really a big thing." The project is designed to be model-agnostic at the architectural level — the underlying model can change over time — but deeply model-specific in implementation. antirez's bet is that deep integration with one high-quality model beats shallow support for many, and he speculates about expert variants tuned for coding, legal, and medical domains: "you load what you need depending on the question."

[[Bonsai 27B]] pushes the same trajectory from the opposite direction — not a flagship model on a workstation, but a 27B-class Qwen 3.6 derivative running on a phone. PrismML's 1-bit variant weighs 3.9 GB and fits within an iPhone 17 Pro's ~6 GB usable memory budget, retaining roughly 90% of the full-precision baseline across 15 benchmarks. The ternary variant (5.9 GB) retains roughly 95%. The largest quality drop is in agentic tool-calling and instruction-following — precisely the capabilities that matter most for the local-agent vision. Even so, 90% at phone scale was not achievable even a year ago, and the trajectory is clear.

[[GPU Self-Hosting for Coding Agents]] provides systematic benchmarks the literature has been missing. The imec team tested coding agents on SWEBench Pro tasks across four hardware tiers — DGX Spark, single H200, 4×H200, and 8×B200 — pairing each with a best-fit model and measuring throughput, task completion time, and resolution rate at increasing concurrency. The headline finding: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on a single H200 comfortably serves 32 concurrent developer sessions. DeepSeek V4 Flash on 4×H200s matches that concurrency with higher quality. Kimi K3 achieves the highest raw quality (86.4% resolution rate) but runs 8× slower than the Claude Code API baseline and requires B300s to fit in memory — a lesson that quality and throughput pull in opposite directions, and that model selection is always a three-way trade-off between quality, speed, and hardware cost.

### The Economics Are Turning, but Not Uniformly

The same GPU self-hosting study analysed costs directly. Renting GPUs can be up to 35× cheaper than API pricing for smaller models, but more expensive for large ones. A 4×H200 must stay 89% busy to beat DeepSeek API pricing; an 8×B200 rack needs only 15% utilisation. Real-world enterprise GPU utilisation typically runs 15–25%, which means owned hardware often idles at the small scale but can pay off decisively at the large scale. The authors note that overnight automated-agent sessions are emerging as a way to soak up idle capacity — turning what was waste into a cost advantage.

The practical implication is that the economic case depends entirely on workload pattern. Continuous high-volume tasks — transcription, document processing, always-on coding agents — favour local hardware. Bursty low-volume use favours APIs. Most real-world setups will be hybrid, and nobody has yet built the calculator that takes a usage pattern and tells you where to draw the line.

### Voice and Document Processing Are Already Solved Locally

Local speech-to-text is a solved problem at production quality. [[Doing]] ($49, Mac-only) transcribes 60 seconds of audio in roughly 400 milliseconds using NVIDIA's Parakeet model — roughly 150× real-time. [[Handy]] (free, cross-platform, 21.6k stars) offers both Whisper and Parakeet V3 backends through a Tauri-based architecture with push-to-talk interaction. Neither sends audio off the device.

The insight from [[Doing]] is worth dwelling on: "LLMs know what you mean, even when your words aren't perfect." When the downstream consumer of transcription is an AI rather than a human reader, the quality bar drops. You do not need perfect transcription; you need good-enough transcription that an LLM can recover the intent from. This changes the economics of the entire voice-to-agent pipeline — and it means local transcription, which is slightly less accurate than cloud transcription, is good enough for the use case that matters most.

Local text-to-speech is equally viable. [[Pocket TTS]] (Kyutai, 100M parameters) runs voice cloning on CPU with no GPU required. [[Inflect-Micro-v2]] pushes the boundary further: 9.4M parameters, 37.5 MB on disk, competitive quality at 6× real-time on CPU. It scored a 66.2% blind preference rate against comparable models with a UTMOS22 naturalness score of 4.395. The trade-off is narrow scope — English only, single synthetic voice, no cloning — but for embedded and privacy-sensitive deployments, the size makes deployment almost frictionless.

The compact models solve single-purpose TTS. At the other end of the scale sits [[AuK]], a just-released open foundational model that unifies speech generation *and* editing behind one instruction interface — the open-source answer to the unification problem the small models deliberately leave aside. "Voice is solved" now has two meanings: the 9.4M-parameter local chip, and the open general foundation.

Document intelligence has crossed the same threshold. [[Dolphin]] (ByteDance, ACL 2025) achieves 89.78 on OmniDocBench with a 3B-parameter model, handling both digital and photographed documents through a document-type-aware two-stage architecture. [[Capybara]], also from ByteDance, unifies text-to-image, text-to-video, and instruction-based editing in a single model with FP8 quantization and ComfyUI integration — production-grade visual generation that runs on local GPUs.

### Safety Does Not Require the Cloud

[[Shieldstral]] (Mistral, 3B parameters, Apache 2.0) demonstrates that content safety classification can run locally on a single 16 GB GPU while matching or outperforming open guard models up to 7× its size. Its policy-adaptive design — safety policies are supplied as plain-language queries at inference time rather than baked into the model weights — means a single checkpoint adapts to new moderation requirements without retraining. The model unifies prompt classification, response moderation, refusal detection, and toxicity detection into a single interface that handles text, images, and text-and-image content.

The implication is that the full local inference stack is becoming viable: a coding or reasoning model for generation, a small safety model for moderation, all on one machine with no cloud dependency. The safety model does not need to be the same scale as the generation model; it just needs to be good enough to catch what the bigger model might produce.

### The Infrastructure Layer Is Filling In

Between the model weights and the user sits a growing infrastructure layer — servers, API gateways, calculators, and decentralised networks that make local models accessible to applications that were not built to run them.

[[Lemonade (Local AI Server)]], backed by AMD, provides cloud-API-like capabilities — chat, vision, image generation, speech-to-text, text-to-speech — through a local server with OpenAI, Anthropic, and Ollama-compatible endpoints. Its sub-10 MB embeddable binary is the feature that matters most for adoption: any application can bundle a full multimodal AI server without a cloud dependency, and the user never needs to know it is there.

[[maclocal-api]] targets Apple Silicon specifically, exposing Apple Foundation Models and Hugging Face MLX models through an OpenAI-compatible API. Its gateway auto-discovers and proxies Ollama, LM Studio, and Jan — one endpoint for every local model on the machine. The tool-calling support is deeper than most cloud APIs: seven format auto-detection, streaming deltas, strict JSON schema enforcement with xgrammar EBNF, and a radix-tree prefix KV cache for reusing context across turns. The constraint is that it requires macOS 26 (Tahoe), which limits adoption to the most recent hardware.

[[Self-Hosted LLMs]] provides the practical bridge from curiosity to deployment: an interactive calculator that maps specific GPU models to specific LLMs, estimating memory requirements, maximum concurrent requests, and token throughput. The formula it encodes — tokens per second is roughly (memory bandwidth divided by model size) multiplied by efficiency, quantization boost, and context impact — gives the first-order answer most people actually need: can I run this model on my hardware, and will it feel fast enough? The extensive model database covers Llama, Qwen, Kimi, DeepSeek, Mistral, and GLM families with MoE active/total parameter breakdowns, and the concurrency estimates account for KV cache memory pressure — the detail that catches most first-time self-hosters by surprise.

[[Petals — Decentralized LLM Inference]] takes a fundamentally different approach to the hardware problem: instead of fitting the model to one machine, split it across a peer-to-peer network. Each participant serves a slice of the model; the network stitches responses together. It supports models up to Llama 3.1 405B and reports roughly 6 tokens per second for Llama 2 70B on single-batch inference — usable for interactive applications but far from the speed of a local single-machine engine like DS4. The project also supports fine-tuning and hidden-state inspection, capabilities that hosted APIs typically do not expose.

[[FreeToken — Edge-Native MoE Serving Engine]] is the strongest counterweight to Petals' "one machine isn't enough" premise: it runs 290B+ MoE models on a single consumer GPU by treating the CPU, host RAM, and PCIe bus as first-class execution resources, splitting each decode step between GPU and CPU according to measured bandwidth. Where DS4 bets on deep single-model integration and Petals on a peer network, FreeToken bets on making one heterogeneous box behave like a small datacenter. The accompanying paper pushes the claim further still — 35B on a laptop, 284B on a gaming desktop, and the 753B GLM-5.2 on a single workstation GPU — though that last figure sits against [[GPU Self-Hosting for Coding Agents]], which benchmarked GLM-5.2 needing an 8×B200 node.

### Data Sovereignty Is the Unifying Conviction

What connects these projects is not just cost or latency. It is a conviction about who controls the infrastructure, who sees the data, and whether the user can walk away. [[Thunderbolt]], the open-source AI client from Mozilla's MZLA Technologies, makes this explicit in its tagline: "AI You Control: Choose your models. Own your data. Eliminate vendor lock-in." It supports self-hosting via Docker or Kubernetes, air-gapped deployment, and any model with an OpenAI-compatible API. Its partnership with deepset's Haystack for EU sovereign cloud deployments signals that data sovereignty is a market, not just a philosophy — and one with regulatory teeth behind it.

The same conviction shows up in tools that are not about AI at all. [[QMD]] keeps document search entirely local — hybrid BM25 plus vector plus LLM re-ranking, all running on three small GGUF models that auto-download on first use. It ships an MCP server so that Claude and other LLM clients can search local documents without uploading them. [[tolaria]] designs its knowledge base around plain Markdown files and git repositories: files-first, git-first, offline-first. The editor serves the user's data, not the other way around. [[Upwelling]] rethinks collaborative editing through the same lens — branching and merging for writers, borrowing from Git's model while hiding the technical complexity, so that writers get the power of version control without the command line.

### The Extreme Edge Shows the Direction of Travel

[[MimiClaw]] runs a ReAct agent loop on a $5 ESP32-S3 microcontroller — 0.5 W, pure C, no operating system, no cloud dependency. It connects through Telegram, maintains persistent memory in NVS flash, and runs cron-scheduled autonomous tasks. The models it calls are still cloud-based (Anthropic and OpenAI), but the agent runtime itself is local. Its 5.4k stars are not about practicality — they are about the idea that an AI agent does not need a datacentre.

[[Gemma Gem]] runs Google's Gemma 4 entirely in a Chrome browser tab via WebGPU — no server, no API keys, no cloud. The models are modest (~500 MB and ~1.5 GB), but the capability is real: read and analyse webpage content, click buttons, fill forms, answer questions about the current page. Browser-based inference is not a replacement for a local engine like DS4, but it is a zero-install entry point that works anywhere Chrome runs.

[[Smolbox]] pushes the same idea from inference to a whole agent: an x86_64 Alpine VM compiled to WASM plus a WebGPU LLM exposed to it as tool calls, so an entire Claude-Code-like environment runs in one browser tab with no server, no API keys, and no data collection — a zero-install, zero-permission sandbox aimed at school iPads and library computers rather than developer workstations.

[[AI Brain for Flipper]] connects a Flipper Zero to AI through OpenRouter, giving natural-language control over RF, IR, NFC, and BadUSB hardware. The risk-classification architecture — low-risk actions auto-execute, medium-risk show diffs for review, high-risk require explicit confirmation — is the right pattern for any AI-controlled hardware, regardless of where the model runs. The model is in the cloud, but the conviction that the user should control the hardware through an AI interface rather than a menu system is the same local-first instinct applied to embedded devices.

## Where the Sources Disagree

**Single-model depth versus multi-model breadth.** [[DS4 (DwarfStar 4)]] bets on deep integration with one model — the architecture is hardcoded, the quantization recipe is hand-tuned, the disk KV cache is designed around that specific model's compression ratios and layer structure. [[Lemonade (Local AI Server)]] and [[maclocal-api]] bet on supporting many models through plugin engines and auto-discovery. The tension is real. DS4 achieves quality that the broader engines do not claim to match, but at the cost of being locked to DeepSeek V4 Flash or whatever model antirez ports next. The broader engines give flexibility but deliver whatever quality the upstream model runners provide. For a coding agent where reliability and consistency matter, DS4's approach is more convincing. For an application that just needs "a model, any model," the broader engines win. Neither approach is wrong; they optimise for different things.

**The economics of local versus cloud are not settled.** [[GPU Self-Hosting for Coding Agents]] shows that renting GPUs can be 35× cheaper than APIs for smaller models but more expensive for large ones — and even then, only at high utilisation. Real-world enterprise GPU utilisation of 15–25% means most owned GPUs idle most of the time. [[Petals — Decentralized LLM Inference]] proposes a different model entirely: share idle capacity across a peer-to-peer network. Neither approach has clearly won, and the imec authors' own recommendation — measure your workload before committing — is the honest answer, not a cop-out. The economic case for local inference is real but conditional; anyone claiming it is always cheaper or always more expensive is oversimplifying.

## What's Missing

**End-to-end local agent benchmarks.** [[GPU Self-Hosting for Coding Agents]] benchmarks coding agents on SWEBench Pro across hardware tiers, but no source benchmarks a fully local pipeline — voice to transcription to search to generation to text-to-speech — against the equivalent cloud pipeline on real, end-to-end tasks. Without this, claims about local quality are disaggregated and optimistic. A pipeline is only as strong as its weakest component, and the weakest component in a local pipeline may not be the model everyone is focused on.

**Multi-model orchestration on local hardware.** If you are running transcription, embedding, generation, and safety models simultaneously on one machine, who manages GPU and CPU allocation? [[maclocal-api]]'s fair-request queueing with concurrent batch decoding is a start, but it is designed for multiple requests to one model, not multiple models competing for the same resources. Multi-model resource management on consumer hardware is unsolved, and it is the problem that will matter most once the individual models are all good enough.

**Hybrid cost modelling.** Most real-world setups will be hybrid — local for some workloads, cloud for others. No source provides a calculator that takes a usage pattern and tells you which models to run where, with cost projections for each configuration. The [[Self-Hosted LLMs]] calculator handles the local side, but nobody has built the tool that compares local and cloud costs for the same workload.

**Windows and Linux parity.** The highest-performance local inference tools — [[maclocal-api]], [[Doing]], DS4's Metal backend — are Mac-first. The Tauri-based tools ([[Handy]], [[tolaria]]) are cross-platform, but Apple Silicon's unified memory and Neural Engine create a genuine advantage that has no direct equivalent on x86 or Qualcomm hardware. Whether this gap widens or narrows depends on how aggressively Intel, AMD, and Qualcomm compete on AI hardware in the next generation of chips.

**Long-term maintenance for single-developer projects.** [[A Few Words on DS4|DS4]] was built in a week by one person. Its quality is remarkable, but the model it targets will be superseded, and the engine will need to be ported or rewritten. The broader engines (Lemonade, maclocal-api) have institutional backing; DS4 has antirez's attention span. The sustainability of the single-model-deep-integration approach is unproven, and it is the approach that currently delivers the best quality.

## Also on This Theme

- [[Talon]] — Hands-free computer control through voice commands, mouth-sound clicks, eye tracking, and user-written Python scripts. Processes speech locally for sub-100ms latency and privacy. Patreon-funded by a single developer rather than VC-backed.
- [[SuperStation One]] — Open-source FPGA gaming console recreating the original PlayStation on MiSTer-compatible hardware. Not about AI, but its "open source from day one" stance as a competitive strategy against proprietary hardware is the same conviction applied to a different domain.
- [[Introduction to Obsidian]] — A practitioner's field report on Obsidian. The "file over app" philosophy, plugin minimalism, and honest scepticism about graph-view hype share the local-first, user-control conviction with every tool on this page.
- [[Headscale]] — Open-source, self-hosted implementation of the Tailscale control server. WireGuard mesh networking without the cloud coordination server. 38.4k stars. The networking equivalent of local inference: same protocol, your infrastructure.
- [[Locker]] — Open-source self-hostable file storage platform with multi-store backends (local disk, S3, R2, Vercel Blob), an AI-powered knowledge base, and a plugin system. "Your files, Your cloud, Your rules."
- [[Claude's System Prompt]] — Leaked Claude Opus 4.6 system prompt. Relevant here as an accidental catalog of what a cloud model's safety and behavioural architecture looks like — the thing local inference is trying to replicate, replace, or deliberately diverge from.
- [[RedGridLink]] — Offline team navigation via BLE mesh with MGRS coordinates and encrypted data exchange. No cellular service, no external servers, no cloud. The field-operations equivalent of the local-first philosophy.
