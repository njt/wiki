# The Dead Economy Theory

Owen McGrann extends "dead internet theory" to the economy itself: a system where productive capacity hums along without needing human participation. The essay is the sharpest political-economy critique of AI acceleration I've read — it names the financial incentive structure honestly, traces the self-destructive logic of the AI layoff cascade, and frames the entire moral question through the Camus-Sartre break, arguing the present person is the unit of account, not some future trillion-soul utopia.

---

## Key Quotes

> "The gentler language ('copilot,' 'assistant,' 'augmentation') is marketing."

McGrann is blunt: the valuations require elimination, not augmentation. OpenAI at $830B and Anthropic at $380B didn't get there on "making humans more productive." The TAM has to be the global labor market itself.

> "Remove labor from that equation and watch what happens."

Democratic governance is a bargain. The governed provide labor, tax revenue, military service, and consumer spending. The governors provide order and services. AI breaks that bargain asymmetrically — a handful of corporations own the productive capacity, and nobody needs the governed anymore.

> "People don't want a check. They want work. They want purpose."

Citing David Shor's polling showing a federal jobs guarantee polls better than UBI, and Case & Deaton's "deaths of despair" research. The mechanism isn't poverty — it's the loss of purpose, status, and perceived future. This is the essay's deepest insight: the AI labor crisis is existential before it's economic.

> "The person who exists now… is the unit of account."

The Camus-Sartre break in one line. Sartre and the Marxists said present people can be acceptable casualties in pursuit of a better future. Camus said no. McGrann is Camusian. This is the philosophical core the accelerationists have no answer for.

> "Warmed-over Parfit without the rigor."

On Longtermism. And on Effective Altruism: "utilitarianism reinvented by people who have apparently never encountered Bernard Williams." The whole section on Silicon Valley's intellectual poverty is devastating — Nietzsche misreadings, Bayesian rediscoveries made without Kuhn/Lakatos/Feyerabend, a tradition that keeps reinventing wheels badly.

> "All of them require functioning democratic institutions with the will to challenge the richest companies in human history."

The solutions are known (public stakes, antitrust, taxing automated labor, capital distribution). The problem isn't intellectual — it's political. And "the window is closing."

## Key Themes

- #concept **Dead Economy Theory** — the successor to dead internet theory. Productive capacity without human participation. "Plenty happens and none of it requires you."
- #concept **The AI Layoff Trap** — the three-turn cascade: cost-cutting surge → customer base evaporates as laid-off workers stop spending → the company discovers its customers were other companies' workers. A prisoners' dilemma where "every firm rationally automates beyond the socially optimal level" (Falk & Tsoukalas, Wharton).
- #concept **Camusian AI Ethics** — the moral case that present people are the unit of account. Rejects utilitarian sacrifice of the present for a hypothetical future. The philosophical framework accelerationism has no answer for.
- #pattern **Deskilling as Active Harm** — Anthropic's own research shows junior engineers using AI agents complete tasks no faster and understand their work less. The harm isn't just economic (lost jobs) but epistemic (lost understanding).
- #concept **Democratic Leverage Erosion** — the governed lose bargaining power when their labor, tax revenue, and consumption are no longer needed. The public funded the research; private companies captured the reward.
- #person **Owen McGrann** — writer at The Palimpsest (Substack). Sharpest political-economy critic of AI acceleration in the current discourse.

## Critical Analysis

**This is the essay the AI safety crowd should be writing but isn't.** The alignment/safety discourse has been captured by longtermist abstractions that treat present harm as a rounding error. McGrann does the obvious thing — starts with the financial incentives, traces their logic, and lands on a moral framework that treats actually-existing people as what matters. It shouldn't be radical. It is.

**The three-turn cascade is the mechanism that makes this structural, not cyclical.** The usual counterargument to automation panic is "new jobs will be created" (the Autor point that ~60% of today's jobs didn't exist in 1940). But the AI Layoff Trap is different — it's not about whether new jobs eventually appear. It's about the speed at which the cascade destroys demand relative to the speed at which new demand sources emerge. Frey's point that the Industrial Revolution took 70 years for wage recovery is the real counter to "it'll work out."

**The Camus-Sartre framing is correct but strategically incomplete.** McGrann's moral clarity is bracing and needed. But Camus's position — present people are the unit of account — doesn't give you a political program. It gives you a refusal. What McGrann describes in Part VII — public stakes, antitrust, tax policy — requires precisely the kind of institutional power the essay's own analysis suggests is eroding. The essay diagnoses the trap brilliantly but doesn't escape it.

**The "intellectual poverty" section is satisfying but a bit inside-baseball.** The takedown of EA/Longtermism/rationalism is deserved and the scholarship gap is real. But I'm not sure it changes anyone's mind. The people who need to hear it already agree; the people who should hear it will dismiss it as "unfamiliar with the literature." The more politically potent move is the one McGrann makes elsewhere in the essay — show the contradiction between OpenAI's public white papers (32-hour workweek, public wealth fund) and its political spending (super PAC against taxing AI). That's not philosophy; that's receipts.

**"Dead economy theory" risks the same fatalism as "dead internet theory."** Both are descriptive power moves — they name a felt reality so precisely that the naming feels like analysis. But dead internet theory also became a convenient excuse to stop trying. If the economy is "dead," what's the intervention? The essay gestures at political solutions but its emotional register is elegy, not call-to-arms. That might be honest. It might also be self-defeating.
