# Fruit Jelly Slices

How Passover dietary law accidentally preserved a fragile, impractical candy that should have died with the penny-candy era — and what that tells us about tradition, constraint, and the history we inherit without noticing.

---

## Key Quotes

> "Passover is an Apollo 13 puzzle. Imagine you are a food scientist and I take away your flour. Now, with the ingredients you have left, I want you to recreate standard American packaged foods."

The whole article is built on this frame: religious law as engineering constraint. It works because it's specific — not "tradition is interesting" but "here is the exact mechanism by which a seaweed-derived gelling agent saved a midcentury candy from extinction."

> "The fruit jelly slice is too delicate for easy transportation and, like penny candy, it would certainly have been driven to extinction — if not for agar-agar."

Agar-agar is the hero of the story. Seaweed is kosher for Passover. Gelatin (animal-derived, requiring slaughter certification) is a kashrut nightmare. The accident of agar-agar being both functionally superior for this specific candy AND religiously permitted created a protected market niche.

> "Much of the Ashkenazi Passover diet uses ingredients that the ancient rabbis either rarely ate (sugar, seaweed), had never heard of (coffee), or were cultivated in places that the rabbis didn't know existed (potatoes, chocolate and quinoa)."

The best line in the piece. It collapses the distance between an ancient legal system and a modern industrial food supply into one sentence. The rabbis who codified Passover law could not have conceived of any of these ingredients, yet their legal framework now governs them. This isn't a bug — it's how all legal systems work. The law outlives the lawgiver and gets applied to things the lawgiver couldn't imagine.

> "Tradition is quirky; the bigger our universe, the quirkier it gets. The best we can do is enjoy its fruits."

A deliberately punny closer, but the argument is sincere: the more stuff humans produce, the more accidental survivals we accumulate. Tradition isn't planned; it's selected.

## Key Themes

#food #history #religion #tradition #constraints #accident

The core argument is that tradition is contingent. The fruit jelly slice isn't on the Passover table because anyone chose it — it's there because agar-agar happens to be kosher, and that created a protected market that kept the product alive when its secular counterparts died. This is how most "traditions" actually work: not design, but drift plus a filter.

Kalman traces the thread backward: industrial candy manufacturing → sugar refining → candied fruit → 8,000 years of sugar cultivation. Each step was contingent. If thermometers hadn't been perfected in the 18th century, candy wouldn't have scaled. If agar-agar hadn't been discovered, the jelly slice would be a historical footnote.

The article is secretly about path dependence — the same dynamic that explains why we use QWERTY keyboards and why VHS beat Betamax. Religious law just happens to be a particularly vivid filter.

## Critical Analysis

This is a small, perfect essay. It takes one weird fact (why do we eat those specific jelly candies on Passover?) and unpacks it into a history of sugar, a tour of industrial confectionery, and a meditation on how tradition actually works. Kalman writes like someone who has spent a lot of time thinking about Jewish law and also a lot of time eating candy, and refuses to see those as incompatible.

The piece is better than it needs to be. It could have stopped at "here's a fun fact about Passover candy" and been forgettable. Instead it follows the thread all the way back to the invention of sugar refining and all the way forward to a theory of tradition-as-contingency. That's the move that separates a good explainer from a great essay — the willingness to treat a small subject as worthy of serious intellectual attention.

The weakness: Kalman gestures at but doesn't really explore the tension between tradition-as-accident and tradition-as-obligation. If the fruit jelly slice is just a random survival, why should anyone care about preserving it? He seems to answer "because it's delightful" — which is a fine answer, but sidesteps the harder question about which accidents deserve to become obligations. Not every survivor is worthy.

That said, this connects to something [[The Mundanity of Excellence]] gets at from a different angle: the things that matter most are often the result of small, boring, contingent choices rather than grand designs. The fruit jelly slice and the Olympic swimmer have more in common than either would admit.

Also resonates with [[Our Hunter-Gatherer Future]] — both pieces argue that what we treat as natural and inevitable is often just a series of historical accidents that happened to stick.
