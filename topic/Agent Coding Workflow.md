# Agent Coding Workflow

Coding agents have made generation nearly free, so the whole of the practitioner's craft has migrated downstream to where the cheap part stops: verification, comprehension, and the harness that makes delegation safe. The sharpest single finding in the evidence is that these two things do not move together — across a large codebase, autonomous coding agents raise commits by roughly 180% while shipped releases rise by only about 30%, because what governs shipping is review, verification, and human attention, not typing. The workflow that wins is therefore not the one that generates fastest but the one that has built the tightest loop from intent to proof: tests that run without being asked, context curated like code, specs kept as the durable artifact, and a human operator who stays able to understand and judge what the fleet produces. The bottleneck was never typing; it is now thinking speed, taste, and the discipline to verify before shipping.

## The Argument

### The Maturity Spectrum

[[Five Levels from Spicy Autocomplete to the Dark Software Factory]] gives the cleanest framework: five levels from manual coding through task offloading, collaborative partnership, human-in-the-loop management where AI becomes the senior developer, autonomous specification where developers act as product managers, and finally fully autonomous black-box automation — the dark factory. Shapiro notes that 90% of AI-native developers sit at Level 2 and most teams plateau at Level 3, where you are not coding anymore but not really designing either — you are reading.

[[Compound Engineering]] maps a similar ladder through five stages of adoption, from manual development through chat-based assistance, agentic tools with line-by-line review, plan-first PR-only review, idea-to-PR, and finally parallel cloud execution. The critical claim: most developers plateau at Stage 2 — approving every action — because they have not built the systems that make delegation safe. The compound step — capturing learnings, updating CLAUDE.md, creating new agents, codifying patterns — separates productive teams from ones that just go fast. Its 50/50 rule is the most concrete prescription: half of engineering time on features, half on improving the system.

The ladder is not linear per task. [[Simon Willison — Engineering Practices That Make Coding Agents Work]] identifies a rung that arrived only recently: *not reading the code*. Willison calls this "clear insanity" but argues it becomes possible if you shift energy into making the agent *prove* the code works — through TDD, manual testing by the agent, and conformance suites. This is a qualitatively different posture from both the careful Level 3 reviewer and the Level 4 spec-writer, and it is the point where the spectrum stops being about trust and starts being about proof.

[[ThoughtWorks Future of Software Engineering Retreat]] adds the structural insight: a *middle loop* of supervisory engineering work now sits between inner-loop coding and outer-loop CI/CD — decomposing problems into agent-sized packages, calibrating trust in agent output, and keeping architectural coherence across parallel streams of generated work.

### How Practitioners Actually Work

[[How Boris Uses Claude Code]] reveals the creator's own setup: 5–15 parallel sessions, plan mode before execution, CLAUDE.md as team memory checked into git, and verification as the force multiplier that "2-3x the quality." Cherny runs one large model with thinking rather than a smaller one with corrections, on the grounds that fewer retries beat a cheaper model. [[Addy Osmani's Workflow]] is the responsible professional's version: start with a detailed spec.md — "waterfall in 15 minutes" — work in focused chunks, review like a senior engineer, and treat classical practice as more critical than ever.

[[Matt Pocock — Grill Me, Then Go AFK]] turns planning into an interview: a "grill me" skill interrogates the developer question by question until a shared design concept exists, then a PRD the human deliberately does not read, then a Kanban board of vertical-slice issues that agents implement away from keyboard. Pocock's two structural claims deserve weighing against the rest of the evidence: models have a "smart zone" of roughly the first 100k tokens and degrade quadratically beyond it, and "bad code bases make bad agents." [[Loop Engineering]] is the generalisation of the same move — "replacing yourself as the person who prompts the agent." Osmani's components (automations, worktrees, skills, connectors, sub-agents, state) describe the loop; his three warnings describe the cost: verification still sits on the engineer, comprehension debt grows with every loop, and designing the loop is the cure when done with judgement and the accelerant when done to avoid thinking.

[[Claude Code on the Go]] pushes the envelope differently: six agents on a cloud VM supervised from a phone, with push notifications when Claude needs input — when runs take hours, you are not coding from your phone, you are supervising from it. [[Don't Wait for Claude]] diagnoses the real bottleneck: it is not Claude's speed but the human's ability to hold parallel sessions without losing context; externalising state so resuming requires no mental recall turns four work cycles per hour into twelve.

[[Inside the AI Workflows of Every's Six Engineers]] shows six engineers at one company converging on planning-first, multi-model, guardrail-heavy workflows while diverging on tools, models, and review strategy. [[Claude Code Cheat Sheet]] documents the feature density underneath: 30+ slash commands, a four-level memory hierarchy, hooks, on-demand skills, and MCP. [[How Intercom Uses Claude Code]] is the most complete enterprise deployment published — 13 plugins, 100+ skills, hooks that intercept raw `gh pr create`, OpenTelemetry observability, and a session-end classifier that files gaps to Slack with pre-filled issue URLs. [[Claude Code is a Beast — Tips from 6 Months of Hardcore Use]] documents a solo dev rewriting 300k LOC with skill auto-activation and 11 subagents; [[Claude Chic]] and [[Agentic Engineering at Kenn]] are the tools and team process behind Wes McKinney's version, where "vibe coding is not caring at scale" and the human-operator loop is the whole point.

### The Non-Coder Dimension

[[Two Kinds of User Are Emerging]] identifies a divide between AI power users and casual users, but the surprise is that many power users are non-technical — the finance person converting a 30-sheet Excel model to Python "almost one-shot" is a different disruption than the engineer speeding up. [[vibes-cli]] targets people who do not code at all; [[life-system]] applies the same tools to personal productivity. [[AI Killing B2B SaaS]] and [[The Road Runner Economy]] argue the democratisation threatens software businesses — Raford, with zero prior coding experience, built nine substantial projects in weeks — while [[HN RIP Low-Code 2014-2025]] captures the parallel flip in the build-versus-buy calculus.

### Verification: The Force Multiplier

The single most consistent finding across the evidence is that verification, not generation, is the force multiplier. [[Writing Code vs. Shipping Code]] gives it a number: autonomous coding agents raise commits by 180%, but that cumulative effect falls to 50% for the number of projects and to 30% for actual releases — the weak-link hypothesis, with an estimated elasticity of substitution of 0.25 between AI and human effort. The system ships at the speed of its slowest human-verified constraint, which is why every serious workflow in this page spends its attention downstream of generation. [[Software Engineering at the Tipping Point]] generalises it: AI is a 10× amplifier, not a director, so weak fundamentals amplify into mess and strong ones into leverage.

[[Building low-level software with only coding agents]] validates the pattern at scale: Pixo, Lee Robinson's Rust image compression library, produced 38,000 lines and 900+ tests for $2,871 with zero hand-written code — but Robinson made hundreds of architectural decisions. [[Building 200+ Integrations with OpenCode]] arrived at a 3:1 guardrail-to-generation ratio, because agents optimise for task completion regardless of accuracy. [[Poor Man's Loop Engineering]] reduces the whole discipline to two ingredients: the agent must be able to test its own work, and someone other than itself must review it. [[Don't Fear the Dark Factory]] reframes the concern — a dark factory is "just a really simple loop" of sessions plus a validation harness — and [[Simon Willison — Engineering Practices That Make Coding Agents Work]] makes the same point harder: "Tests are no longer even remotely optional. Tests are — they're free now."

[[Designing Agentic Loops]] names the meta-skill — choosing tools, guardrails, and success criteria so YOLO-mode agents converge — and prefers shell commands over MCP because agents already know `curl`, `jq`, and `ffmpeg`. [[Dmitry Sotnikov's LLM Workflow]] names the mechanism: the loop is a genetic algorithm and tests are the selection pressure, which is why a solution that is not mostly right on the first shot should be reverted rather than debugged. [[Guardrails and Feedback Loops]] and [[Principal Drift]] reach the same conclusion from the governance side: mechanical enforcement does not degrade the way prompt-level instruction does, and the one rule that survives every risk tier is never to let the agent that authored a change be its only reviewer.

### Context Engineering: The Harness Matters More Than the Model

[[How Claude Code Works in Large Codebases]] is the official position: the harness matters more than the model, and its seven components — CLAUDE.md, hooks, skills, plugins, LSP integrations, MCP servers, subagents — are the extension surface. [[The New Software Lifecycle]] sharpens the claim to a ratio: an agent is roughly 10% model and 90% harness, and most agent failures are configuration failures, which is encouraging because configuration is fixable without waiting for better models. Osmani's advice to leaders is the pithiest version of the verification thesis: "Set the bar at the eval, not the demo."

[[A Guide to Claude Code 2.0]] names the discipline — "the art and science of curating what will go into the limited context window" — against sessions running 200K–500K tokens with effective windows at 50–60%. [[Intent Layer]] makes the argument from the other direction: agents fail on large codebases because they lack the tacit knowledge senior engineers carry, and folder-boundary AGENTS.md files cut token consumption from 40K to 16K in one case. [[CLAUDE.md (Universal)]] distills the file to six token-efficient rules; [[Writing a Good CLAUDE.md]] argues it must be short, universal, hand-crafted, and never auto-generated, because frontier models follow roughly 150–200 instructions and Claude Code's system prompt already spends about 50.

[[Benchmarking AGENTS.md Changes]] is the one empirical test, and it is a caution: the best AGENTS.md iteration improved training-set performance but regressed on a clean holdout — footprint widened, tokens climbed, review correctness dropped. The failure mode is not "everything gets worse" but "enough gets better that you miss the damage." [[Pre-Commit Lint Checks]] and [[claude-ctrl]] make the opposite-direction argument: a linter is the guardrail that cannot be talked around, and "an instruction that lives only in model context is not a constraint."

[[Talking to Transformers]] supplies the mental model — attention as budget, domain language as compression, inhibition over instruction — echoed by [[Code Field]] and [[How to Effectively Write Quality Code with AI]], whose sharpest line is that every decision you do not take and document will be taken for you, usually badly.

[[Platform Engineering as the AI Control Plane]] folds harness engineering into the existing discipline: it is platform engineering with an AI-specific layer, and the prediction is that more engineers will end up building the harness than the product, because the harness is now the harder part. [[Managing AI Coding Costs at Scale]] adds the economics: chase the efficiency frontier rather than the intelligence frontier, and treat context bloat as the hidden cost driver — at Databricks, simple harness and caching tuning cut generated tokens by roughly half with no quality loss. [[The Economic Benefit of Refactoring]] is the most satisfying close to the argument: Fowler measured an 83% drop in input tokens from refactoring agent-generated code, not by deleting lines but by decomposition that let the agent read only the files it needed — refactoring helps reading far more than writing, and it compounds across every future change.

### Specs as the Durable Artifact

When generation is cheap, the specification becomes the primary artifact. [[Specifications as the Product]] captures the shift: the spec is what you *want* the software to be, and it endures when the code is disposable. [[The AI-Native SDLC Playbook]] scales this to the whole lifecycle — every stage commits an artifact the next stage reads (`intent.md` → `spec.md` → `plan.md`) — and [[How to Write a Good Spec for Agents]] gives the five principles, including the "curse of instructions" and the three-tier boundary system (Always do / Ask first / Never do).

[[Structured-Prompt-Driven Development]] treats prompts as version-controlled delivery artifacts with the golden rule "when reality diverges, fix the prompt first"; [[Spec-Driven Development]] models specs, tests, and code as a triangle where implementing improves the spec; [[OpenSpec]] treats specs as descriptions of current behaviour with deltas for intent-based review; [[Specsmaxxing]] argues for YAML acceptance criteria with stable IDs threaded through code and tests. [[recursive-mode]] is the file-backed, phase-gated variant — each phase produces one locked document, audited phases loop draft → audit → repair → re-audit, and the whole rationale survives in the repo rather than the chat. [[SDD Case Study — 13 Apps in 70 Days]] is the strongest field result for the family: thirteen applications shipped solo in seventy days, because "the agent is a fast bricklayer with no blueprint" and the spec is the external memory you give it.

[[Code Field]] pushes back: a four-line prompt of pure negations lifted bug detection from 39% to 89% in its tests, because inhibition shapes LLM behaviour more reliably than instruction — let the code emerge smaller than your first instinct. [[The Cost YAGNI Was Never About]] reframes the oldest heuristic for the agent era: YAGNI was never thrift — it was two pieces of price theory, the Optionality Bill and the NPV Bill. Free generation does not weaken YAGNI; it makes the violation cheaper to commit, which is worse — and you comprehend the speculative structure less because you did not write it. [[The Plan Is the Program]] catches the shift in a phrase — the plan becomes the atomic unit of work — and [[AI for Product Management]] shows the same structure outside engineering: the magic is in how you combine structured context, not any single prompt.

### The Human Role: Taste, Judgment, and Cognitive Stamina

[[Radical Accountability]] argues AI eliminates the excuse of insufficient engineering time — taste is all that remains. [[The Mythical Agent-Month]] extends Brooks: agents crush accidental complexity but generate new accidental complexity, and essential complexity — deciding what to build — was always the hard part, with a "brownfield barrier" somewhere past 100 KLOC. [[The Claude C Compiler]] makes the point technically: AI implements known abstractions well but invents nothing new. [[Automatic Programming]] draws antirez's bright line: programming is now automatic, vision is not.

[[On a Year of Multi-Model Development]] quantifies the variation — 25–71× acceleration depending on domain specificity — and its construction-trade taxonomy (Claude the architect, Codex the drywall contractor, Gemini the surveyor) is the most practical characterisation of model strengths in the wiki. [[Probabilistic Engineering and the 24-7 Employee]] names the asymmetry: "generation has become cheap, but validation has not," and the failure mode is not collapse but slow, silent degradation. [[Simon Willison — Engineering Practices That Make Coding Agents Work]] identifies the surprising constraint — cognitive stamina: keeping three or four agents busy at full throttle leaves Willison "done for the day," which is "what saves us," because one engineer cannot replace a thousand.

[[AI Zealotry]] makes the senior-engineer case — experienced engineers are best positioned because they can distinguish quality from slop — while [[Opus 4.5 Changes Everything]] records the ambivalence of a veteran watching the craft become trivial, and [[Gas Town After 10,000 Hours of Claude Code]] refuses the pure-delegation model: "Even if I am vibe engineering, I still care about the code. I still look at it."

### The Cognitive Cost

[[Cognitive Debt]] names the structural problem: "code has become cheaper to produce than to perceive," production velocity now exceeds comprehension velocity, and the deficit is invisible to productivity metrics until failures expose it. [[Agents and Acquiring Debt]] sharpens it to the agentic case: agents do not eliminate debt, they shift when it is acquired — and the AI-native type is comprehension debt, "the new highest-interest loan you can take out against your code," accepted without agreement when you LGTM an edit you did not understand. "Every agent is the new hire on day one, forever." [[Understand to Participate]] and [[The Joy and Power of Understanding]] supply the counter-argument in normative form: understanding deeply is a prerequisite for staying an active collaborator, not a luxury, and the models are "force multipliers — but we must have the force first and keep it strong." [[Silently Resolved Ambiguity Is Comprehension Debt of Intent]] names the most senior form of the debt: when an agent resolves an ambiguity silently, a decision is made with no signature, and the only fix is provenance — knowing whether an outcome traces to something you authored, a verifiable reference, or a statistical guess.

[[acceleration-flow]] describes the psychological experience as slot-machine gambling — near-misses, abstracted token costs, a high from the rate of change rather than the capability — and [[Breaking the Spell of Vibe Coding]] develops it into an addiction model with a roughly 40% gap between perceived and actual productivity (developers thought they were 20% faster and were 19% slower in a METR study). [[Slowing the Fuck Down]] prescribes deliberate friction — agents do not learn from their mistakes, so the pain signals that once triggered refactoring are gone — and [[Slowing Down in the Age of Coding Agents]] offers the most unusual response: reading agent-produced design documents on an e-ink tablet with a pen, because the physical act of forming letters forces the thought to sit still. [[The Cult of Vibe Coding Is Insane]] rejects the ideology outright — "pure vibe coding is a myth," and even the never-look-at-code developers are building plan files, skills, and rules; the framework *is* the engineering. [[Compound Engineering]] offers the systems answer to all of it: do not rely on willpower to slow down, build infrastructure that makes speed safe.

### Agents at Scale: The Overnight Fleet

[[RepoMirror]] put Claude Code in a `while` loop overnight — six codebases ported, roughly 1,100 commits, $800 — and the key finding is that a 103-word prompt beat a 1,500-word one. [[Agent Flywheel]] packages a one-command VPS installer for 10+ agents at $440–656/month, pitched against a $5,000/month junior. [[Coding Agents and Complexity Budgets]] recounts Robinson's $260 weekend migration and the insight that "the cost of abstractions with AI is very high."

[[TextForge Case Study]] shows the six-layer discipline that makes greenfield generation production-grade — planning mode, reference architectures, skills, PRDs, verification pipelines with snapshot testing, and periodic human-led refactoring. [[The Lifecycle of a Swamp Issue]] implements a five-phase state machine so "the agent physically cannot skip steps"; [[AI-Driven Development Life Cycle]] proposes "bolts" — cycles measured in hours — across inception, construction, and operations; [[agent-pr-replay]] derives steering rules by replaying merged PRs and measuring the gap against real human solutions.

[[What a User Story Actually Costs in a Dark Code Factory]] is the first honest metering of the fleet: 7.7 million tokens and $10.88 per story, with 95.4% of tokens as cache reads — "a dark code factory is mostly a reading machine that occasionally types," and the code is no longer the expensive artifact. [[Dev Machine Foundry]] is the cautionary tale from the other end: Schillace's dev machine mapped 1,111 obscure OOXML properties but never built a basic ruler, which sat in the candidate list for 22 sessions because the reliable green checkmark always won. "The machine is honest, but it isn't strategic. Strategy is still my job." [[Lean Software Production]] restates the discipline positively: the core question is "what *can't* the agents do?", and the work becomes engineering the system that produces the software.

[[I Don't Want Your PRs Anymore]] argues LLMs have inverted open-source economics — maintainers can generate code faster than they can review stranger PRs — and [[If AI Is Doing the Investigation, Version the Investigation]] prescribes committing the session transcript alongside the change so the reasoning does not vanish with the terminal tab. [[Scaling LLMs to Larger Codebases]] divides investment into guidance and oversight and gives the blunt imperative: "Read every line of generated code. Just because you told an LLM to sanitize inputs, doesn't mean it actually did." [[A Practical Guide to Brownfield AI Development]] is the best brownfield piece in the wiki: tests as system boundaries, documentation as context, incrementalism as risk management, and the counterintuitive point that speed enables boldness — "the faster you can fix things, the bolder you can be about breaking them."

### Organisational Adoption

[[Minions — Stripe's One-Shot Coding Agents]] is the most significant deployment: 1,000+ unattended PRs per week on hundreds of millions of lines, with a North Star of "a pull request produced without any human code." [[Uber — Agentic Engineering Shift]] adds the caution that scale is not free: Uber's agentic shift saw costs rise at least 6× since 2024, to the point of CFO-level approval — and activity metrics no longer satisfy the finance org, which wants revenue impact. [[Inside OpenAI's In-House Data Agent]] shows the same pattern in a different domain — the hard problem is making the company's data reality legible through six context layers, not model intelligence.

[[Agentic Software Engineering (Hassan)]] is the canonical text, treating agentic SE as an engineering discipline — actors, process, artifacts, tools, and four trust disciplines — with the refrain that a fool with an agentic tool is still a fool: speed without an engineered system of constraints scales mistakes faster than value. [[Laura Tacho — Data vs Hype]] supplies the sceptical data layer: adoption is not transformation, and outcomes vary far more across companies than the marketing would suggest. [[Continuous AI]] extends the loop to the delivery pipeline — CI/CD plus the automation of the subjective judgments that used to block it.

### The Mobile and Community Layer

The tools are proliferating: [[happy]] solves the "I started a session and need to leave my desk" problem; [[MobileVibe]] connects a phone to a desktop machine; [[Claude Sidecar]] runs a parallel AI window sharing context with other models; [[Claude Chic]] offers an alternative terminal UI. [[Collaborator]] reimagines the desktop as an infinite canvas; [[Pencil]] puts a design canvas in the IDE; [[Kata]] and [[Lovelace]] make project state agent-native — one as a local service, the other as repo files. [[Awesome Vibez]] documents the community building tools around agents rather than agents themselves; the infrastructure layer is where the real problems live.

## Where the Sources Disagree

**Speed versus comprehension.** [[Cognitive Debt]], [[acceleration-flow]], and [[Slowing the Fuck Down]] argue unchecked speed destroys understanding; [[Compound Engineering]] counters that you do not need discipline if you have systems. [[Breaking the Spell of Vibe Coding]] goes furthest with the addiction model. The systems school is more convincing on teams, the discipline school on individuals — but the METR productivity-gap finding tilts the verdict toward the sceptics, because it shows the delusion is measurable.

**Read every line versus do not read the code.** [[Addy Osmani's Workflow]], [[How to Effectively Write Quality Code with AI]], and [[Scaling LLMs to Larger Codebases]] insist on reading every line; [[Simon Willison — Engineering Practices That Make Coding Agents Work]] says the newest rung is not reading, and shifts energy into proof. [[Principal Drift]] is the most convincing synthesis: dissolve the binary into task-routed governance — route authentication, money, and destructive changes to line-by-line review, everything else to systems inspection, with the one universal rule that the authoring agent can never be its only reviewer.

**The harness matters versus the harness is not enough.** [[How Claude Code Works in Large Codebases]] and [[The New Software Lifecycle]] put the ratio at 90% harness; [[Harness Engineering is not Enough]] replies that maintainability is not RL-trainable — a better harness cannot make a model produce code that stays changeable, because no fast oracle exists for good design. Both are right at different depths: the harness fixes configuration and context, but not the quality of the code it produces, and [[The Economic Benefit of Refactoring]] shows the residual is a human job.

**Multi-agent orchestration versus pair programming.** [[Agent Flywheel]] and [[ctx – Agentic Development Environment]] bet on fleets in isolated worktrees; [[Gas Town After 10,000 Hours of Claude Code]] rejects the delegation model for loss of visibility and git pollution; [[How Boris Uses Claude Code]] lands in the middle with parallel human-agent dialogues. The middle is the most convincing, because it keeps the verification loop human while still parallelising.

**CLI versus MCP.** [[Designing Agentic Loops]] and [[Simon Willison — Engineering Practices That Make Coding Agents Work]] favour shell commands; [[MCP Is Dead; Long Live MCP]] argues HTTP MCP is transformative for organisations. The split is cleanly individual-versus-organisation, and the MCP side wins at scale for the same reason harness engineering does: centralisation, auth, and observability.

**Specifications as the product versus exploration as the method.** [[How to Write a Good Spec for Agents]], [[Structured-Prompt-Driven Development]], [[Specsmaxxing]], and [[OpenSpec]] treat the spec as durable; [[Code Field]], [[Simplicity in the Age of AI-Assisted]], and [[RepoMirror]] argue over-specification produces bloat and the unlock is discovering what to remove. [[Compound Engineering]]'s distinction — vibe code to discover what you want, spec-driven to build it — is the most convincing resolution, and [[The Cost YAGNI Was Never About]] explains why: the two modes have different prices now.

**One model versus many.** [[How Boris Uses Claude Code]] uses one big model; [[On a Year of Multi-Model Development]], [[Inside the AI Workflows of Every's Six Engineers]], and [[Claude Sidecar]] run several. The multi-model camp's evidence — different models fail differently, and cross-model review catches what single-model workflows miss — is stronger, but Boris's counter that fewer retries beat switching is right for a single well-understood task.

## What's Missing

**Team adoption patterns.** How does a team of ten adopt agent coding, what is the onboarding sequence, what fails first? [[Zero Alignment]] names the gap, [[ThoughtWorks Future of Software Engineering Retreat]] surfaces the problem, but neither fills it.

**Cost modelling.** Pixo cost $2,871; Nango's integrations under $20; [[Agent Flywheel]] estimates $440–656/month. [[What a User Story Actually Costs in a Dark Code Factory]] is the first real metering and [[Managing AI Coding Costs at Scale]] the first playbook, but nobody has published a full cost model for a sustained practice: tokens per sprint, cost per feature, cost per bug found, full TCO.

**Causal evidence.** [[Writing Code vs. Shipping Code]] is the strongest number in the wiki, but its weak-link hypothesis is still correlational — the wiki needs experiments, not just field reports. [[Five Studies That Are Changing How I Think About AI in Software Engineering]] is a survey, not a control group.

**Failure case studies.** Boris does not discuss where Claude Code struggles; the Django team in [[A Practical Guide to Brownfield AI Development]] shelved their experiment in a paragraph. [[Dev Machine Foundry]] and [[Uber — Agentic Engineering Shift]] are the first honest accounts of what goes wrong at scale, but the wiki still needs post-mortems of agent-assisted projects that died.

**Agent-native project management.** [[Kata]] and [[Lovelace]] are two different bets — a local service versus repo files — and neither has proven the model at team scale.

**The apprenticeship crisis.** [[Probabilistic Engineering and the 24-7 Employee]] and [[The Next Two Years of Software Engineering]] raise it but do not answer it: if juniors lean on AI from week one and seniors review agent output, the pipeline from junior to senior breaks.

**The visual gap.** [[What Frontend Developers Still Hate — 2026 Survey]] documents that agents still lack eyes — the wiki has no treatment of how to verify the parts of software a model cannot see.
