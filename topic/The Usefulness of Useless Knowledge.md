# The Usefulness of Useless Knowledge

Abraham Flexner's 1939 *Harper's* essay is the foundational text of science policy — the argument that curiosity-driven research, pursued without thought of practical application, is the ultimate source of transformative utility. Flexner wrote it as the founding director of the Institute for Advanced Study at Princeton, and the essay doubles as both a philosophy of science and a political polemic against the totalitarian states (Germany, Italy) that were then dismantling academic freedom. It remains the single most cited defense of blue-sky research, and Flexner's examples — Maxwell/Hertz → radio, Faraday → electricity, Gauss → relativity, Ehrlich → modern medicine — form the canonical case for funding things that look useless.

---

## Key Quotes

> "Marconi was inevitable. The real credit for everything that has been done in the field of wireless belongs... to Professor Clerk Maxwell, who in 1865 carried out certain abstruse and remote calculations in the field of magnetism and electricity."

Flexner's opening move in a conversation with George Eastman (of Kodak). Eastman named Marconi as the most useful scientist; Flexner dismantles that by tracing the genealogy: Maxwell's equations (1865) → Hertz's experimental confirmation (1887-88) → Marconi's coherer (a trivial last step). The argument is not that Marconi contributed nothing, but that *credit* and *causality* are different things. Marconi got the fame and the money; Maxwell and Hertz did the thing that made radio inevitable. This is Flexner's sharpest framing, and it maps directly onto modern debates about whether specific AI advances are genuine breakthroughs or merely the last obvious step on a path others laid.

> "Curiosity, which may or may not eventuate in something useful, is probably the outstanding characteristic of modern thinking. It is not new. It goes back to Galileo, Bacon, and to Sir Isaac Newton, and it must be absolutely unhampered."

This is the essay's thesis in one sentence. Note the timeline: Flexner traces curiosity-driven inquiry back to the Scientific Revolution, not to some timeless human essence. He's arguing that modernity *is* the institutionalization of curiosity, and that institutions which deflect it with "considerations of immediacy of application" are betraying their purpose.

> "I am not for a moment suggesting that everything that goes on in laboratories will ultimately turn to some unexpected practical use or that an ultimate practical use is its actual justification. Much more am I pleading for the abolition of the word 'use,' and for the freeing of the human spirit."

The pivot that makes the essay more than a cost-benefit argument dressed in historical anecdotes. Flexner explicitly rejects the instrumental defense of basic research ("it'll pay off eventually") in favor of a deeper claim: the pursuit of knowledge is self-justifying. A poem, a symphony, a painting, a mathematical truth — these need no further justification. This is the essay's most radical move, and the one most often conveniently ignored when people cite Flexner to justify science funding on economic grounds.

> "The real enemy of the human race is not the fearless and irresponsible thinker, be he right or wrong. The real enemy is the man who tries to mold the human spirit so that it will not dare to spread its wings."

Written in 1939, this is not abstract philosophy. Flexner names Germany and Italy explicitly. He notes — with dark irony — that the Institute for Advanced Study is "indebted to Hitler for Einstein, Weyl, and von Neumann." The essay is simultaneously a theoretical argument, an institutional memoir, and a war document.

> "You have no duties — only opportunities."

Flexner's reply to a Harvard professor who asked what his obligations would be at the Institute. This is the institutional design principle in five words: remove friction, remove obligation, provide only time, peers, and permission. The Institute had no faculty meetings, no committees, no building (at first). Scholars worked where they liked, with whom they liked, for as long as they liked. One mathematician described the effect as "the blinds are raised; the room is light; the windows are open."

## Key Themes

**#concept** **Curiosity as infrastructure**: Flexner treats curiosity not as a personal virtue but as the output of institutional design. The question isn't "how do we find curious people?" but "what conditions allow curiosity to produce its unpredictable fruits?" The Institute's design — no meetings, no committees, no duties — is the answer.

**#pattern** **The inevitability thesis**: Flexner's claim that Marconi was "inevitable" is a theory of innovation: fundamental discoveries create a space of possibilities that clever technicians will inevitably fill. The credit belongs to those who *create* the space, not those who pick the low-hanging fruit within it. This has clear echoes in current debates about who "really" invented the transformer, attention mechanisms, or RLHF.

**#pattern** **The recursive application of useless knowledge**: Flexner's examples form a nested structure: pure math → physics → engineering → daily life. But he also shows the loop closing — practical problems (grapevines, beer brewing, syphilis) stimulating theoretical breakthroughs that then enable further practical advances. Pasteur is his model for the scientist who moves fluidly between the two registers.

**#concept** **Science as a river system**: "Science, like the Mississippi, begins in a tiny rivulet in the distant forest. Gradually other streams swell its volume. And the roaring river that bursts the dikes is formed from countless sources." This is an argument against the heroic theory of discovery and for the cumulative, distributed nature of scientific progress. Every discovery has "a long and precarious history."

**#concept** **The political dimension of intellectual freedom**: The essay makes no separation between the philosophy of science and the defense of liberal institutions. Academic freedom is not a perk for professors; it is the condition under which the human spirit produces everything worth having, from penicillin to poetry.

## Critical Analysis

**The blind spot Flexner couldn't see.** The essay's most uncomfortable passage, read today, is Flexner's argument that scientists bear no responsibility for the military applications of their work. Dynamite, poison gas, the airplane — all are "innocent inventions" abused by "men whose hearts were poisoned and whose brains were addled." This is a clean argument in 1939 but becomes much harder to sustain after the Manhattan Project, which Flexner's own Institute housed (von Neumann, Einstein's letter to Roosevelt). Flexner doesn't anticipate the organizational form — the mission-directed megaproject — that would fuse curiosity-driven science with industrial-scale engineering to produce weapons of existential consequence. He was right that Maxwell didn't build the radio, but wrong to think this exoneration would always hold.

**The Marconi straw man.** Flexner's dismissal of Marconi as a mere technician who "picked other men's brains" is rhetorically effective but unfair on its own terms. The coherer was not trivial (it was the first practical radio wave detector), and the engineering work of turning Hertz's laboratory sparks into transatlantic communication was genuine innovation of a different kind. Flexner's essay conflates "more fundamental" with "more valuable" — a move his own argument elsewhere resists when he insists poems and paintings need no justification. The essay itself performs the hierarchy it claims to reject.

**What the essay actually argues vs. what it's cited for.** Flexner is routinely cited to justify science funding with the promise of eventual payoff. But the essay's strongest claim is the opposite: that the pursuit of knowledge needs no external justification at all. This is a much harder political sell, and the fact that Flexner's argument gets reduced to "basic research → GDP growth" is itself a demonstration of the problem he diagnosed — that our conception of what is useful is "too narrow to be adequate to the roaming and capricious possibilities of the human spirit."

**The Institute as institutional argument.** The most radical part of the essay may be Section IV, where Flexner describes the Institute for Advanced Study's organizational design. No meetings. No committees. No duties. No building. Scholars from 22 countries. The message is that the *form* of the institution is the argument — you can't claim to value curiosity-driven research while subjecting it to the same administrative machinery that optimizes for predictable output. This directly anticipates modern critiques of grant-writing overhead, publication metrics, and the bureaucratization of academic life.

**Contemporary resonance.** Re-read in 2026, Flexner's essay maps almost perfectly onto current debates about AI research. The argument that "you can't predict which research will be dangerous" is the same argument safety advocates make about capability research. The demand that institutions "prove their usefulness" is the same demand made of humanities departments and theoretical computer science. Flexner's claim that the Nazis inadvertently strengthened American science by driving out Jewish scholars is a grim preview of the brain-drain dynamics that still shape global science. And his description of the Institute — "a paradise for scholars who, like poets and musicians, have won the right to do as they please" — is essentially the dream behind [[Guardian Angels]] and the personal AI assistant movement: a space where the tool does the administrivia and the human does the thinking.

The essay's deepest tension, unresolved and perhaps unresolvable, is between its two claims: (1) useless research often produces enormous practical dividends, and (2) useless research needs no such justification. Flexner wants both, and the reader is left to decide whether (1) is a genuine argument or a rhetorical concession to a world that can't hear (2).
