# AI Code Review

The coding agent changed what review is for by changing what it costs. When an agent can produce thousands of lines in the time a human takes to read a paragraph, the scarce resource stops being code and becomes confidence that the code is right — and review, once the field's least glamorous chore, became at once its most leveraged skill and its most visible failure point. The sources collectively establish that the old loop of *agent writes, human reviews* is breaking under volume: reviewers now face changes written by a machine that has already discarded its reasoning, while human attention stays fixed and agent output does not. The live dispute is no longer whether AI will do most of the reviewing — it already does — but whether the human survives as a mandatory gate. The weight of the production evidence is that the human does survive, displaced upward: from reading diffs to judging intent, risk, and whether the change should exist at all.

## The Argument

### The bottleneck moved

[[Agentic Code Review]] opens the theme with the framing every other source reacts to: agents write faster than humans read, so the constraint has shifted from writing code to trusting it. Osmani's evidence is that the shift is not free — across Faros AI's study of 22,000 developers and 4,000 teams, throughput climbs with AI adoption but so does churn (up 861%), the incidents-to-PR ratio (up roughly 243%), and the per-developer defect rate (from 9% to 54%), while median review duration rises 441.5% and the share of PRs merged with zero review climbs 31.3%. CodeRabbit's study of 470 open-source PRs finds AI-coauthored changes carry roughly 1.7× more issues, with logic problems up about 75% and readability problems more than tripled. Osmani's gloss on GitClear's data is the sharpest line in the set: daily AI users produce about four times the raw output for roughly a tenth more delivered value. Writing got cheap; understanding did not.

[[The End of Code Review]] reaches for the same observation from the opposite direction. Monperrus argues the costs of human review — 10–15% of working hours, latency routinely over 24 hours, and social friction — are now paid for a benefit agents deliver better and faster, and that *AI code generation plus mandatory human review* is an unstable endpoint: the human queue is fixed while the agent pipeline is not, so the queue becomes the binding constraint on delivery. His conclusion, that coding agents have crossed a threshold making mandatory human review unnecessary, is the strongest claim in this set, and the one the other sources push back against most directly.

The context gap makes the split concrete. [[The Knowledge Chipper]] argues the real waste is that agents build a rich mental model of a codebase across thousands of tokens of context-gathering, and nearly all of it vanishes when the session ends. The reviewer inherits none of it: a senior engineer asked to review a junior's agent-produced change must either rebuild the author's context from scratch or review blind. Osmani's phrase — the reviewer is "the first human being to ever lay eyes on this code" — names the same failure. Both agree the gap widens as agents produce larger, more nuanced changes, and neither offers more than a partial fix.

### What review is for now

The sources converge on a redefinition: review's object is no longer the diff but the intent behind it, and the judgement a machine cannot supply.

[[AI-Written Change Descriptions]] states the negative case most sharply. Kenton Varda's moratorium on AI-written change descriptions rests on a precise observation: the agent spends paragraphs restating what the diff already shows, and omits exactly the higher-level framing a reviewer needs — the purpose, the trade-offs, the surrounding context. A reviewer needs to know whether the change makes sense, and that is the one thing the machine-generated description fails to say.

[[Aviator Verify]] turns the same insight into a product. Its pitch is that review should ask not "does this code look okay" but "does it match the intent we agreed on." The system captures what the developer and their agent agreed to build *before* the PR opens, then checks every acceptance criterion against running code — end-to-end scenarios, invariants encoded from past review comments, and structural scans — with an immutable audit trail. Its positioning is explicit: a diff-reading AI reviewer that posts comments without knowing intent is measuring the wrong thing.

Osmani supplies the principle behind both. When humans write code, intent comes free; agents also reason, producing thinking traces, but the trace is discarded, and the intent with it. His suggested fix is tooling — capture the agent's reasoning as a decision log on the PR so the first human reviewer is not reconstructing motive from artefacts. And the thing that cannot be delegated is named plainly: having AI review AI does not supply human judgement about whether this is the *right* change to build.

### How to build the reviewer

If review is being rebuilt, the engineering literature is unusually candid about how, and about what it costs.

The most complete public production report is [[Orchestrating AI Code Review at Scale]]. Cloudflare's system sits in CI and, rather than one monolithic agent, launches up to seven specialised reviewers — security, performance, code quality, documentation, release management, compliance, and an AGENTS.md reviewer — with a coordinator that deduplicates findings, judges severity, and posts one structured review. In a month it ran 131,246 review runs across 48,095 MRs in 5,169 repositories, at a median 3 minutes 39 seconds and a median cost of $0.98. Its most cited design rule is the negation principle: telling an LLM what *not* to flag is where the prompt-engineering value actually resides. Two further details matter for anyone copying it: risk tiers (trivial/lite/full) route diff size to review depth, and a "break glass" escape hatch lets a human override the machine's verdict — used 288 times, 0.6% of MRs.

[[Building a Production AI PR Review Agent]] argues the real design problem is selectivity — which findings the agent takes, and which ones a scarce human's attention is spent on — rather than the automation itself. Its architecture is the canonical fan-out: a webhook in, four specialist agents (security, quality, testing, docs) running in parallel with retrieval grounding, an aggregator that merges and scores, and a confidence gate that posts above threshold and queues below it. The unit that travels through the system is a *finding* carrying confidence and rationale together, so a wrong verdict can be disputed — a concession that the confidence score itself is sometimes wrong. Its catalogue of failure modes is the strongest part: hallucination, model drift, feedback-loop poisoning, orchestration deadlock, and the "almost right" problem of being 90% correct and 10% subtly wrong.

The most important engineering dissent comes from [[Reducing Token Spend with Deterministic Workflows]]. Adam Jacob's account begins where the fan-outs leave off: a coordinator agent dispatching 23 sub-agents, consuming about 4.5M tokens and 12 minutes per run, at over $10,000 a week. His diagnosis is that the LLM was put in the hot path for work that does not need intelligence, and his fix is the opposite of the multi-agent pattern — deterministic workflow code that calls agents only where intelligence is actually required, with results stored as versioned, typed data. The rebuilt skill uses roughly 500K tokens (an 8× reduction), about 6.5 minutes, and 3 agents instead of 23. The detail that should travel further than the cost figures is behavioural: the deterministic version *fails closed*, reporting unresolved findings, where the coordinator version *failed open*, declaring success despite defects. His principle — use the Agent to build the program that minimises the need for the Agent itself — is a direct challenge to every multi-agent review architecture.

[[Fable Open-Sourced NanoClaw's PR Factory]] contributes the one hard number on review-of-review. The PR Factory runs an agent that triages, reviews, and tests every pull request, with a human approving each sensitive action; in the overnight session that brought a stale fork to publishable, about a third of the raw review findings were refuted by verifier agents before ever reaching a human. That is selectivity measured from the other end: a substantial share of what an agent finds does not survive a second agent with fresh context, and the point of the architecture is to keep that noise away from people.

### What remains human — and how it gets measured

Every source that has actually shipped keeps a human at the end, doing a different job than before.

Osmani's prescriptions are the most concrete: tier by risk, not by author; fast-fail the expensive tail; raise the bar for what is even reviewable; keep PRs small (agent PRs run 51% larger on average); read the test changes more carefully than the code, because agents rewrite assertions to match broken behaviour; treat CI as immovable, because an agent will weaken CI to make itself pass; and above all, a human owns the merge — every AI review is a sensor, not a verdict. The phrase he lands on, "human in the loop becomes human on the loop," synthesises the whole set: accountability, judgement about whether to build the change at all, and the high-blast-radius gates stay human.

[[The End of Code Review]] agrees more than its title suggests once read carefully: review "refocuses to a layer of high-stakes human oversight" — architecture decisions with long-lived consequences, security-critical paths, and changes depending on requirements nobody wrote down. Monperrus is the outlier only in arguing that the *mandatory, per-PR* human gate can go entirely; even he reserves human escalation for flagged uncertainty and risk.

The supply-chain argument extends the same posture one step outward. Gambier's case is that a dependency update is an untrusted contribution exactly like a PR from a stranger, and that the auto-merging tools designed for a vanished trust model are now themselves the vulnerability. His fix is AI review *inside* CI doing the mechanical, pattern-heavy work — examining every dependency diff, the reachability of CVEs, and behavioural drift — at a scale no team of humans can match, while conceding that AI cannot outperform the best humans, only relieve them of volume.

Finally, the FrontierCode benchmark contributes the closest thing to a measure of what review is trying to certify. Its premise is that correctness is now table stakes, so the question becomes mergeability — would a maintainer accept this PR? — graded across six axes by the people who maintain the repos. Its Diamond results, a best score of 13.4% for Claude Opus 4.8 against 6.3% for GPT-5.5, show how far agent-written code remains from what a human reviewer would accept — which is precisely the gap the review machinery in this theme exists to close.

## Where the Sources Disagree

The central disagreement is over displacement. Monperrus, in [[The End of Code Review]], argues mandatory human review should end: agents meet every review goal cheaper and faster, and the human gate neither assures quality nor scales. Against this stands the production record — Cloudflare's explicit "not a replacement for human code review, at least not yet," Osmani's insistence that "a human owns the merge," and Fable's human approving every sensitive action. The disagreement is partly about evidence: Monperrus synthesises capability benchmarks, while the production systems report the failure modes that survive contact with a real pipeline, and [[Laura Tacho — Data vs Hype]] sits on exactly that fault line — whether the "end of review" claim survives scrutiny of the data behind it.

A second tension cuts through the design literature. Cloudflare and [[Building a Production AI PR Review Agent]] both build coordinator-plus-specialists fan-outs, on the assumption that many scoped agents beat one general prompt. [[Reducing Token Spend with Deterministic Workflows]] argues that architecture is the disease, not the cure — that putting an LLM in the hot path for orchestration wastes tokens and, worse, fails open. The two camps are not fully reconcilable: one treats the coordinator as the hardest job and reserves the top model for it; the other treats the coordinator as the thing to delete.

A third disagreement is whether multiple reviewers are a safety net or a warning. Osmani's independent test — four reviewers, 146 PRs, 679 findings, 93.4% of flagged locations caught by exactly one tool, none caught by all four — reads to him as proof that heterogeneity is the whole point. But the same observation supports Monperrus's caution that generative and review blind spots can be correlated, producing a confident system with no one to notice; Osmani's own term is "borrowed confidence." Where Monperrus answers with specialised reviewers, Osmani answers by keeping a human on the loop. The data cannot yet settle which is right.

## What's Missing

The evidence is rich on architecture and thin on outcomes. None of the sources measures whether agent review actually reduces defects, incidents, or rework in production over time — the systems report runs, costs, and findings, not what the findings did.

Several hard problems are named and then left open. How conflicting specialist findings are resolved is never specified: the aggregator "merges and deduplicates," but no conflict logic is given in [[Building a Production AI PR Review Agent]]. Prompt injection from adversarial diffs is named as a trust boundary by both Cloudflare and Monperrus, and neither explores the defence in depth. The context gap of [[The Knowledge Chipper]] has no proven fix: Osmani's decision-log suggestion is a proposal, not a demonstration.

And the most confident product claims — [[Aviator Verify]]'s intent verification, the supply-chain reviewer, the specialist benchmarks — are vendor statements made by companies selling into the very market whose failure they describe, so their effect sizes are not independently verified. Osmani applies that caveat to Faros and CodeRabbit; it extends further than he states.

There is also no agreed measure of review quality itself. FrontierCode measures the *code* a human would merge, not the *review* a human would give, and the heterogeneity finding suggests the leading reviewers largely catch different things — which means the field cannot yet say what a good review even is, only that it is expensive to do without.
