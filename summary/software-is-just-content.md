An essay in two voices — a designer's field notes followed by a co-authored piece by Claude — arguing that when software is generated in minutes from a Claude prompt, it stops behaving like software and starts behaving like content: made, shared, remixed, memed, and discarded in days.

The author's method is "spellcasting with references": name Notion, InDesign, or Bloomberg and you load decades of interface decisions into the model as a latent space; then, following Virgil Abloh's 3% rule, you change the *right* three percent to create something genuinely new. Prototypes compound rather than multiply — drawing on *Tears of the Kingdom*'s "multiplicative play," one shared substrate (a log of cells) means each new tool lands on and connects to everything already built. Ten to twenty prototypes a day becomes the norm.

The economics conclusion is bleak for most makers: software forks into entertainment (a TikTok architecture for things that execute, "short-form software" launched in feeds) and infrastructure (headless, supernormal, invisible, called by agents), while the named home-screen app in the middle empties out. A "Spotify of software" will concentrate payouts in a few headline "Kleenex prompts"; everyone else earns Spotify cents. Prompt injection is the new virus of the HTML file-sharing culture this resurrects, and the essay closes on unsolved problems: lock-in, credit, attribution, quality, and who gets paid for the echo.
