Van den Veld's essay on the Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) boom asks one question: who owns the roadmap, and who captures the value created by closing product gaps? He lays out six positions an FDE business can occupy, ranked by strategic defensibility.

1. **Time-and-materials reseller** — you bridge gaps in a vendor's product you don't control. AI makes engineering capacity cheap and universal, dissolving the delivery advantage; you're competing around a commodity.
2. **Value-added reseller (VAR)** — you own middleware, connectors, vertical functionality around a vendor's roadmap. But AI collapses the cost of building that IP too. His prescription: stop selling raw model deployments and own the layer that makes AI "usable, reliable, auditable, predictable, and ultimately — boring": observability, deterministic gates, routing, governance. He notes the mismatch between model vendors' T&Cs (disclaiming stochastic failures) and your E&O insurance.
3. **Open-source contributor** — contributing code isn't capturing value; frontier labs ingest open-source codebases as training data, synthesising your domain edge into the next model's weights. He expects "peek licenses" and dual licensing with revenue share.
4. **Strategic partner** — you influence the vendor's roadmap, but influence is not control. The vendor absorbs your proven capability (Copilot for Legal, Microsoft Work IQ) and ships it free with the license: "You thought you had a seat at the roadmap table. Turns out, you were lunch."
5. **Palantir** — embed so deeply that removal is an existential risk to the client. For everyone else, the FDE/core-R&D split ("front-house vs back-house") creates product drift and a brutal synchronisation tax that only government-scale margins can absorb.
6. **The engineer at the gate** — you sit on the buyer's side and hold decision rights: buy vs build vs change-the-process. Four moats: decision rights, a client-owned control layer (routing, guardrails, evals, observability), a client-owned context layer (ontology over the data swamp), and accountability — building the governance harness that makes the client's risk committee willing to deploy. Execution traps: unicorn talent (structured as a law-firm-style partnership, not an agency pyramid), stochastic accountability (governance, never indemnification), and the ever-present temptation to drift back into building products.

He discloses he runs a Position-6 business, and a commenter suggests a seventh: the independent systems integrator rolling AI out ERP-style, department by department, with model changes as integration work rather than existential threat.
