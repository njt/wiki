Adam Ward, the Valley's most-admired builder of high-talent-density teams (Pinterest 200→2,000, Facebook 1,000→10,000, now head of talent at Cursor), argues that the default hiring model is broken. He calls it the **funnel of doom**: companies treat recruiting like a sales funnel, reach out to 100 people, get 20 replies — "not the top 20%, just the 20 people you caught on a bad day" — weed them through stages, and hire the remainder. This "remainder hiring" regresses to the mean over time and can never reliably produce a talent-dense team.

The alternative is to treat **every hire like an executive search**. Start with a "pillar of excellence" instead of a wide funnel: (1) *scope* — objectively stack-rank the skills and experiences that define the top 1% for this specific role; (2) *market-map* — identify the ~50 best people in the world for it by mining trusted networks with highly specific questions ("who's most collaborative with designers?", never "who's the best engineer?"); (3) *relentlessly pursue and activate* those individuals over days, weeks, or years. The atomic unit of hiring is the person, not the job spec.

Several reframes follow. Recruiting is a **confidence engine, not a decision-making engine** — hiring managers own the call, recruiters supply the confidence to make it. **Caring is free** and is the #1 driver of candidate satisfaction ("the company generally wanted me; they made me feel like I belong"). **Closing happens throughout** the process, not at the offer stage — uncover motivations early, remove objections continuously, and treat the close as a team sport with daily standups and per-candidate Slack channels. Tactics include on-site work trials, bespoke (non-availability-driven) interview slates, personalized closing gifts, and a post-offer "pre-boarding" motion to combat the rising trend of renegs.

Ward reads the current market as a "tale of two cities": demand for AI researchers, forward-deployed engineers, and engineer/product/design hybrids is "11 out of 10," while overly-specialized ICs and under-experienced new grads are waning. Time, not comp, is the scarcest asset. He also warns founders against collapsing "fill roles now" and "build the system" into a single first recruiting hire, and argues the recruiting team should be among the highest-paid in the company — and that every recruiter should become a "talent engineer."

The transcript (a Lenny's Podcast episode) closes with an explicit list of unanswered questions: DEI and the homogeneity of "trusted networks," scalability beyond elite roles, retention after hiring, comp internal equity, and the absence of quantitative success metrics.
