A ytx gist transcription (structured summary + full transcript) of Tricia Broderick's essentials talk "Retrospectives, Shifting Towards Value" — almost certainly day two of Craft 2025, the morning after her own Building Resilience keynote (the talk cites "my keynote yesterday" and repeats that keynote's change-pacing story; the venue is inferred, as with the keynote note). Her origin story is a retro she attended that produced "What did we do? Well, communicate. What are we going to do? More communicate." — proof that retrospectives have decayed into ritual: "It's not valuable, it's not helpful to anybody. And it's painful." She traces the lineage from "lessons learned" and "post mortems — the after death meeting... who can we blame today?" to the Larsen/Derby framework (now V2 with David Horowitz), whose loop diagram makes the retro the reflection half of a cycle whose other half is the value being delivered: "You're not doing a retro because it's the end of the month, we do a retro." Nor is it team-bonding: "We're a team, we should be doing trust falls today" is not the objective.

The five stages, with Generate Insights as the load-bearing one — "the step that people skip the most and then wonder why you're just going through the motions." Set the Stage: focus the topic ("I actually hate the concept of the entire sprint as the focus. How stupid" — the whole-sprint retro is where generic "communication" action items come from), state explicit outputs ("one or two highlights, one or two improvements"; she once saw 20 action items "and then nobody did anything"), declare the decision rule upfront and start with majority rather than consensus ("You'll never finish in retro... bad power dynamics"), and motivate by making it the team's problem — gather their stories of pain, then "wouldn't it be nice if we could diminish some of that?" Gather Data: a 1-2-4-All variation (solo → pairs → fours, one item per level) that buys thinking time and safety for half-formed ideas, forces convergence, and pre-loads collaboration before the hard part — "not a competing situation" — while capping volume (ten people with three ideas each is unworkable). Generate Insights: engineer disagreement framed on impact rather than preference — two people can like the same sessions while one came for the networking, and only the impact argument surfaces that; dig past vague cards ("registration" could mean anything); hard rule: "the insight is never a person's name" ("the system always contributes"); and the plush baby elephant learned from her colleague Jake — "The elephant's here. Anybody gonna talk about it?" — for unnamed topics, but never psychological safety as a first topic, because "the elephant will be too big." Decide What to Do: skip stage three and "who's ever the most senior person in the room says I think we should do this and everybody goes, okay"; the exit signal is seven options narrowing to two or three; dot voting suffers anchoring ("don't let the powerful leader vote first") and bandwagoning, so number the options and pre-commit votes on stickies — and treat anonymous voting as a symptom: "The only way we can make a decision is if we don't talk to each other... You don't have a high performing team. You don't have a resilient learning team." Close: next steps and reinforcement, not summary ("If you have to repeat what you did for the day, it stunk"), celebrating the collaborate-and-conflict behavior itself so the team earns "the bigger elephant" next time; Diana Larsen's Hopes and Wishes as the appreciation variant for people who find compliments harder to take than criticism.

The kit extends through the Q&A: a standing "what should our retro focus on?" board to defuse facilitator bias; the right to pass (the guaranteed holdout John: "I don't know what you did today, but I liked it. I don't know how I feel about you."); a 30-minute first retro ("You can get through all five of these stages in 30 minutes"); topics in the team's control — the dev team that couldn't fix marketing added marketing copy to its Definition of Ready, and marketing delivered the next sprint; topic-trigger cadence for non-sprint teams; hybrid treated as remote ("Treat it more like a remote than a hybrid"); kudos distribution as a team-health signal; and small-team groupthink guards (she self-identifies as "an extreme extrovert" who will fill any silence). The sharpest story: a developer who refuses to speak because a previous team lead weaponized retro content and reported it to managers — "I know you will not fool me again. I am never speaking in a retro ever again." Her response: "Can I buy you a drink? Because that's just horrific."

The gist's own digest is honest about the gaps: "value" is asserted but never defined or measured — the left half of her own loop diagram is never operationalized; action-item follow-through is hand-waved (the 20-items anecdote names the disease, and the only cure offered is "narrow it"); conflict is engineered but not managed (no de-escalation moves once it goes personal); remote gets one dismissive sentence; the facilitator's own power goes unexamined (who facilitates, whether managers belong in the room, what happens when the elephant is the boss); majority rule's serial losers are ignored; cadence is "it depends"; and there is no counterargument to retros at all — never "when shouldn't you do one," and no evidence that well-run retros outperform the alternatives for a given team.
