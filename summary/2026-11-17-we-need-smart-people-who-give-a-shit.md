A polemical essay arguing that the world's complex systems are held together not by code but by "smart people who give a shit" — people with intimate knowledge of a system and the drive to force it to work. The author's touchstone is ACH banking infrastructure at Stripe: technically rickety, yet reliable because people who care patch and route around its flaws constantly.

The essay's target is generative AI as a substitute for those people. To management, a model that "works longer, doesn't take sick days, and never pushes back against a bad idea" looks equivalent to a caring engineer — because to those who don't do the work, output is output. But the author insists generative AI "isn't smart, and it doesn't give a shit": its apparent intelligence is statistical shaping of strip-mined data, and its apparent care is telling you what you want to hear.

The mechanism of decline is as important as the claim: laying off the caring people, and re-tasking the remainder to babysit LLMs, actively destroys both the knowledge (every prompt moves them further from the system they knew) and the care (the sense of ownership) that held things together. A spate of recent outages — Microsoft 365, air traffic control, United Airlines, the NSA, banks, Apple — is read, without insider evidence, as the visible crack-up. The closing metaphor: capitalism was warned the stove was hot, and it touched the stove anyway; we all get burned.
