Kent Beck's Craft 2025 keynote, preserved here as a ytx gist holding a structured summary plus the full talk transcript including Q&A. It opens with the origin mystery: at XP Universe around 2000–2001, Beck counted five teams in the audience that were spectacularly successful by their own organizations' standards — "more code in less time, with no bugs, happy customers" — and all five were fired. His resolution: "It's not that the teams doing software badly are doing software badly, it's that they're doing something else very well. That something else just doesn't happen to be software development." The metaphor arrived via his daughter Beth's manager, who rejected her proposal with "That would be fine if we were in a forest, but we're living in a desert."

The framework: forest and desert are not points on a spectrum but two self-consistent universes with different philosophical DNA — Rousseau and Mary Parker Follett ("power with") underlie the forest; Hobbes, Parkinson's Law, and Frederick Winslow Taylor (coercion, work-expands-to-fill-time, the "one best way" — built on data Taylor fudged) underlie the desert. "Taylor won," Beck says, though "it's still only a century, so we don't know how this is going to play out." The desert optimizes for managing the work — visible, quantified, the "illusion of control," blame diffusion — while the forest optimizes for doing the work and is "messy, complicated, complex, chaotic" because the projects are. Every shared vocabulary word is a lightning rod heard in opposite ways: estimates, metrics, dependencies, compliance, accountability, status. "The problem's not the problem. The problem is the context."

The economics: software value follows a power law, with most changes worth zero and a rare few enormous. The forest is "optimized to find, seize on, implement and extract value from" the big spike — the whole cake; the desert "is designed to make that big spike of little changes that don't really make a difference come out on a regular basis" — crumbs, which Beck concedes "are pretty tasty. You can make a lot of money in the desert." On resources, the desert minimizes consumption (stack ranking as survival triage) while the forest creates them: "if you design, you'll have time to design." And the desert has stronger gravity: "These are two attractors, and the desert has more gravitational pull than the forest does."

The practical material is a forest toolkit: half-full planning (never plan more than half of what you think you can accomplish; spend the rest on community), leading with purpose rather than pressure ("discretionary emotional energy" as the fuel), metrics for self-awareness never as goals (individual metrics are never reported two levels up), accountability as "required to render account" to your community, resolving cross-team concerns at the boundary instead of declaring dependencies (which enable "scheduled chicken"), and compliance as a resource — his Swiss client Square Life went from product idea on Friday morning to first contract sold Monday afternoon, the "year-long hundred million euro process compressed into a weekend." In Q&A he gives the fruits-and-roots answer to management wanting forest results with desert behavior ("The one where you get the fruits without the roots, it just doesn't happen"), tells a manager to send the 3 a.m.-PR engineer home ("I don't want your tired results, I want your rested, energized results"), predicts executive resistance to AI on country-club status grounds ("If you could get done with 10 people what you're currently getting done with 1000, then your status at the country club goes way, way down"), and answers "is the forest scalable?" with "I think it's the only thing that is scalable." The talk is explicitly not a good/bad polemic — Beck appreciates desert dwellers succeeding "in spite of all the obstacles they put in their own way" — but recognition comes first: you can't talk across the divide until you accept there are two universes.
