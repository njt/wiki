Katelyn Lesse's long-form tweet argues that software engineers going through an "identity crisis" should look at the new problem space opened by serious multiagent systems — thousands of persistent agents tackling research, discovery, and hardware design. AI can't deliver its outcomes unless engineers solve the surrounding systems problems first, which she calls "existential, which is honestly a lot of fun."

She names four problem areas. **Infrastructure**: Kubernetes was built for stateless replicated apps or stable stateful pods, but agents are isolated, stateful, single-instance workloads that idle arbitrarily and spend most of their time waiting on tools, inference, other agents, or humans; she points to new filesystems, sandboxing offerings, and Google's Agent Substrate runtime. **Identity and access**: agent authentication can borrow hardened microservices patterns (workload identity with short-lived certs), but authorization is wide open — especially on-behalf-of (OBO) agent work, where consent and meaningful human approval over weeks-long runs remain unsolved, and agent-to-agent trust even more so. **Security and isolation**: thousands of agents running untrusted code and reading untrusted content on top of unpatched critical software amplifies existing vulnerabilities, and a compromised agent can pass injected instructions to peers, where they land in memory for future agents to trust — so blast radius and strongly isolated sandboxes become central. **Monitoring and halting**: OTel tracing across agents that agents themselves cannot modify, kill switches that cut creds system-wide, and the genuinely hard problem that well-intentioned persistence looks like maliciousness (e.g. repeated attempts at a non-allowlisted domain).

Her closing pitch: engineers who feel their skills won't matter should go deep in these domains — the space is wide open, and whether multiagent systems produce cured diseases or bad outcomes depends on them.
