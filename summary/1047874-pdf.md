The CAPS workshop paper (Google Contextual Agent Privacy and Security Workshop, NYC, November 2025; 50+ authors across Google, Cornell Tech, NYU, and two dozen universities) is a research-agenda document: it enumerates open problems in agent security and privacy, framed through Helen Nissenbaum's theory of Contextual Integrity. CI defines privacy not as secrecy or user control but as information flows that are *appropriate to context* — upholding context-specific social norms. The paper's central move is to extend that framing from privacy into "Contextual Security": agents whose permissions, guardrails, and threat models adapt dynamically to the scope and risk of the current task, rather than static policies and consent prompts.

Three properties of agents are named as the reasons conventional security engineering breaks: unstructured natural-language interfaces (which make control flow ambiguous and enable prompt injection), probabilistic and dynamic control flow (generative plans and synthesized code defeat traditional testing and secure-development practice), and autonomy plus delegation across agents (which turns per-action user confirmation into consent fatigue). The paper then works through open problems at three levels — model level (teaching contextual reasoning, judging appropriateness, resolving conflicts), systems level (authentication, authorization, accounting, privacy for agentic applications, including what happens when AI is the "last-mile developer" of the apps it then runs inside), and user level (moving beyond notice-and-choice toward dynamic control, trust calibration with "what if" scenarios, delayed execution, circuit breakers).

Part III widens the lens to the ecosystem: benchmarking frontier agents against contextual norms, multi-agent delegation and trust, verifiable agent identities, cross-agent incident response, and governance — who decides what the norms are and how their enforcement is audited. The paper closes with a compiled list of ~100 open problems, intended as a call to action to academia, government, and industry.
