A transcript of the *Developer Voices* podcast: host Chris Jenkins interviews James Brown, engineering lead at Schroders Asset Management, about why AI has turbocharged individual side projects while enterprise gains lag. Brown's framing is a ground-zero case study — asset managers carry a bank's regulation and organisational headaches but a startup's move-fast pressure.

The central observation is a **productivity mismatch**. Individually, career programmers are shipping more working code than ever while writing none of it by hand. Inside enterprises, the same explosion hasn't materialised — because coding speed was never the bottleneck. The road to production is full of approval processes, technical debt, and risk aversion, and Brown's panel consensus is that a company's *current* state predicts its AI success: if things are already a mess, AI makes them worse.

Brown names the personal cost "**Claude Mania**" — an addictive rush of round-the-clock agent-assisted coding that left him unable to stop, anxious when *not* running agents, and surrounded by friends who are "more burnt out than ever, especially cognitively." The ability to leave sessions running overnight creates FOMO-driven anxiety about missing productivity.

He offers several other themes: the **cost of trying ideas has collapsed**, so companies should relax innovation gatekeeping and get better at killing bad ideas fast; teams will stay small (2–7 people) and tilt toward "**builders**" (solving user problems) over "**crafters**" (perfecting the machine); the **junior talent pipeline** is at risk as hiring freezes and dead graduate programs strand the next generation with an obsolete curriculum; and non-engineers now spin up prototypes they can't productionise, making "refactoring vibe-coded projects into production-ready systems" a critical emerging skill.

On the technical side, Brown describes his side project **Claire (Clairvoyance)**: a Claude plugin giving multiple agents in one repo "proximal awareness" via a Git shadow (orphan) branch, using progressive disclosure to keep context small and triggering direct agent-to-agent contact when two agents drift too close. It is honestly described as "a readme and a measurement harness" — the proof-of-value work is unfinished. He also sketches the "**high throughput evolutionary harness**" (define "good" as a metric, let agents generate and test features against it) and the "**dark factory**" (a repo that processes its backlog unattended).

Unanswered: how to balance speed against regulatory caution, burnout prevention at scale, what juniors should learn instead of "learn Java, learn OOP," the security of shadow-branch communication, and the ethics of dark factories that auto-copy competitor features.
