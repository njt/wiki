Felix Rieseberg, who works on Claude, offers a practical tutorial on the now-familiar exhortation to "be more ambitious" with AI. He decomposes ambition into four types — Speed, Altitude, Depth, and Range — and gives concrete, worked techniques for each, using the mundane example of a quarterly business review deck.

Speed is set aside as the well-understood baseline. **Altitude** means asking "what's the goal beyond the goal" — going from "fix this crash" to "keep our apps crash-free," which required giving the model access to crash reports, version-to-commit mappings, and machines on three OSes. **Depth** means working with the instruments of top professionals: insist on real databases and statistical models instead of markdown tables, set unreasonably hard goals, give the model raw data to adversarially check assumptions, ask it to produce new knowledge (e.g. simulate customers from logs and find unknown early churn signals), and always use an outside judge — "Do *not* ask the model to judge itself." **Range** means importing fields you wouldn't normally reach for: replace nouns with verbs (a QBR is "getting busy people to believe a story about cause and effect in 30 minutes"), then "assemble the crew" — analyst, journalist, short seller — while avoiding mere role-play: ask the model to *do* what a short seller does, not write like one.

The closing section grounds the advice in psychology: self-efficacy — belief in one's own ability, which measurably predicts accomplishment — is now the binding constraint, and it can be hacked (the Dantzig unsolved-problems story, Bannister and the four-minute mile, hard specific goals per Locke and Latham, permission to fail). All of this presumes the model has a computer, software installation rights, and access to raw data via MCP or similar.
