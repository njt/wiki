# How to Choose a Subproblem

A researcher at the Alignment Research Center (ARC) diagnoses the biggest weakness they see in junior researchers: **problem-solving strategy**. Olympiad medalists and straight-A MIT grads who have never touched a problem that took more than ten hours flounder when handed a 400-hour research problem, because nobody can write down a 400-step plan. The skill to learn is decomposition — peeling a 20-hour subproblem off a 400-hour problem, then 1-hour problems off that, in a fractal structure.

Three natural moves generate a subproblem:

- **Ask a simpler question** — "start small": take the simplest case you don't already understand (means before cumulants, tree-level before loop amplitudes, polynomial-time before O(N²)).
- **Ask about a simpler system** — toy problems and limits. The goal is "the very simplest system which has the thing you actually care about."
- **Ask what the f*** is even going on** — fact-finding (read the papers; learn the asymptotics), plus its key variant *fact-processing*: reshape information so your brain can use it, above all by putting it in a picture.

A good subproblem pays off in three currencies: scientific information (especially **actionable** information that changes your strategy), **human capital** (getting better at the problems you want to solve), and **strategic information** (learning early whether an approach is doomed). The author pushes back on Jacob Steinhardt's "sort tasks by expected failure probability per unit time" — the underlying assumption that one failed subtask kills the whole approach and all intermediate work is worthless is, in their words, "a quitter attitude." Steinhardt's model fits ARC's unusually all-or-nothing payoff structure better than most research.

Red flags for a bad subproblem: too vague (you can't tell if you're making progress), too hard, too easy, too useless. The best gauge of difficulty is how complete your **plan of attack** is — number of plans, completeness of the best one, length of each. And when it's time to quit, turn to "the ultimate destroyer of hope: other people" — tell someone what you've tried and get their frank read, because the cost of giving up too late vastly exceeds giving up too early.
