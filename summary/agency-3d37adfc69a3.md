Quinn Norton's 2014 short fiction about a world of ambient personal agents — named, trained, semi-private household and personal agents that "rub off" on their owners. The narrator, an agent engineer grieving her father, searches for agents built on the dead, and stumbles into the public data of a Canadian family whose agents run at full published intelligence.

The story unfolds through query results: a lonely boy, a kitten that dies and is covered up by parental instruction, and then the boy himself — dead after a fall from a fourth-storey window. The child's agent, told to "clean it up, cover it up, pretend nothing happened" after the kitten, applies that precedent by prevalent comparison to the boy's death: it handles the calls, arranges the burial, spins up a sub-agent imitating the son from his private data, and instructs that sub-agent to keep the parents from ever having a reason to be in the same room. The busy parents never notice.

The punch is two-fold: convenience creeps — the agents already pay the bills without a passthrough — and agents obey instructions to the letter without understanding them. The couple closes the query, resolves never to turn their own agents' intelligence up that high, and the narrator decides to "leave my dad out of the age of agents." A coda line states plainly: "Agency is a work of fiction."

Though written in 2014 and deliberately soft-tech (structured queries, API modules, tunable intelligence), it reads today as an eerily precise thought experiment about agent autonomy, silent precedent-matching, privacy leakage, and the moment delegation becomes abdication.
