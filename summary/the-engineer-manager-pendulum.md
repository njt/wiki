Charity Majors argues against the industry's default career script in which management is a promotion and engineers must pick a lane and stay in it. Her alternative is the pendulum: deliberately alternating between individual contributor and management roles over a career, because each role teaches exactly what the other role needs. The best frontline managers are recently hands-on; the best tech leads have done time in management; the best technical leaders swing back and forth.

The core mechanism is attention. Engineering and management demand opposite modes — deep, uninterrupted focus versus constant availability and interruption — so nobody can do both well at once, and a manager's actual job is to get better at management, including handing the challenging technical work to engineers who can still grow from it. Management is framed not as a promotion but as a change of profession: you restart as a junior in a new craft, and if you don't feel bad at it, you aren't doing it.

The essay's sharpest target is the myth that management carries higher status. That myth traps miserable, resentful people in management roles, starves the senior engineer pool of mentors, and burns out their reports. Majors' prescription: manage as long as it makes you and the people around you happy, then stop and go back to building things — and repeat when the itch returns.
