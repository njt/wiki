A controlled study of how multi-agent system performance scales: 260 configurations across six benchmarks, five architectures (Single-Agent plus Independent, Centralized, Decentralized, Hybrid), and three LLM families, with tools, prompts and compute standardized to isolate architectural effects. The paper fits a predictive model of performance (cross-validated R²=0.373; R²=0.413 with a task-grounded capability metric).

Key findings: a robust capability-saturation effect — coordination yields diminishing returns once single-agent baselines exceed a threshold; tool-heavy tasks incur multi-agent overhead; architectures without centralized verification propagate errors more. Relative performance versus a single-agent baseline swings from +80.8% on decomposable financial reasoning to −70.0% on sequential planning, so architecture-task alignment, not agent count, determines whether collaboration helps. The model picks the best architecture for 87% of held-out configurations and its architecture preferences transfer to unseen frontier models.

This is the arXiv paper (v3, April 2026) behind the January 2026 Google Research blog write-up; it broadens the blog's 180-configuration result to 260 configurations and six benchmarks.
