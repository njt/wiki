The largest synthesis yet of automated verbal deception detection: a systematic review of 25 years of research (289 reports, 6,136 models) with a meta-analysis of 3,653 models nested in 97 datasets. Pooled accuracy is 74.4% (95% CI 71.2–77.4%), which converges with meta-analyses of manual, theory-driven approaches at a 70–75% ceiling — a quarter century of NLP progress has not lifted it.

The striking finding is what drives accuracy: methodological quality, not model sophistication. Only 12.46% of reports used verifiable ground truth; ground-truth rigor systematically shifts reported performance (non-verifiable labels inflate accuracy by ~5.7 points, indirectly inferred labels crater it). Only 23.96% of models were evaluated on independent data, and testing on new data costs ~4.7 percentage points. The adoption of embeddings bought only ~1.5 points over simple linguistic features; fine-tuned transformers bought ~3.5 points; LLMs used by prompting performed *worse* than statistical learning models (−1.2 points). The field is also narrow: 63.67% of reports study fabrication, 82% English-only, 41.5% fake reviews — and the most-reused dataset (Deceptive Opinion Spam) has known origin confounds between TripAdvisor-scraped genuine reviews and MTurk-crowdsourced fake ones.

The authors' verdict: computational methods remain the best available alternative — they beat unaided human judgment (which hovers at chance even for professionals), match manual coding approaches, and scale. But progress requires collective infrastructure, not architecture: a unified multilingual deception corpus, mega-analyses, collaborative shared tasks, pre-registration and model cards, and ultimately a "foundation model of deception" fine-tuned across the full breadth of corpora. On human–machine allocation, they note evidence that human involvement tends to *degrade* an otherwise accurate model, so the question is not replacement but allocation of responsibility.
