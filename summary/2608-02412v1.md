Garnelo and Czarnecki systematically investigate *why* LLMs fail at in-context tabular prediction — one of the most common ML workloads — using controlled experiments that falsify four of five popular explanations before isolating one decisive factor: dimensionality.

They test Claude Opus 4.6 in "pure inference mode" (single prompt with full training and test data, no tools, no scaffolding, no fine-tuning) and evaluate five hypotheses drawn from practitioner folklore. H1 (class overlap/non-separability), H2 (linearised CSV format obscuring column structure), H3 (numeric tokenisation/precision), and H4 (per-query test load) are all **rejected** by targeted interventions. H5 (dimensionality) is the only one that survives: in a sweep of random linear projections over 31 benchmark datasets, the LLM is the *only* method among nine whose accuracy decreases as dimensionality grows, while every classical baseline stays flat or improves.

A memorisation probe first cleans the benchmark: the model recovers original labels for breast cancer, iris, and wine almost perfectly — direct evidence of dataset contamination — and these are excluded from downstream analysis.

On 2D tasks where the model *does* work, its decision boundaries are reproduced almost exactly by distance-based methods (Gaussian processes and low-k NN, 91–92% grid agreement). In higher dimensions, however, no classical model among 252 configured baselines exceeds 64.8% prediction agreement, and adding dimension-dependent label noise to every baseline improves agreement by at most 0.64 percentage points. The LLM's high-dimensional predictions are not those of any standard learner, nor of such a learner degraded to random guessing — the mechanism of the collapse remains open.

Reasoning traces are also examined: in 14 of 20 2D tasks, the model's verbal explanations fail to match either its predictions or the ground truth, consistent with known faithfulness gaps between stated reasoning and actual computation.

The practical implication: no amount of prompt engineering aimed at format, precision, or batching will fix high-dimensional in-context tabular prediction. Purpose-built tabular foundation models remain the right tool.
