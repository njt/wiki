# Agentic Context Management — Précis

A Maximem paper arguing that production AI agents fail less from weak reasoning than from unmanaged context: they drown in accumulating history, miss recalls within and across conversations, and pay a token cost that grows with every turn. The incumbent response treats this as a *storage and retrieval* problem ("memory"). The paper argues that framing is too narrow — actively managing what an agent holds in mind is a **lifecycle**, not a store.

The author names the discipline **Agentic Context Management (ACM)** and decomposes it into five primitives: **architecting** (designing the per-agent memory schema before anything is stored), **ingesting** (turning raw signals into structured, retrievable memory), **scoping** (which fraction of knowledge is relevant, at what organizational level), **anticipating** (prefetching what the next turn will need), and **compacting & consolidation** (reducing context to fit a budget without dropping what matters). A system that does one primitive well is a memory tool; one that does all five coherently, across scopes, is a context-management platform.

Two arguments make the case quantitative. First, the *economic* one: full-append context grows token cost **quadratically** with conversation length, while crude summarization buys linear cost at the price of an "accuracy cliff" (one prior study compressed 18,282 tokens to 122 and dropped accuracy from 66.7% to 57.1%, below the no-context baseline). Only **validated compaction** — compaction that checks itself for information loss — reaches linear cost with preserved fidelity. Second, the *retrieval* one: an original five-domain study shows keyword and vector search win in different regimes, and that hit-based scoring cannot detect the "reasoning-sufficiency gap" — retrieving a relevant document while missing the bridge document needed to finish the chain.

The reference implementation is **Maximem Synap**, a multi-tenant service realizing all five primitives across a scope hierarchy (user → customer → client). It reports 92.0% on LongMemEval and 93.2% on LoCoMo categories 1–4, using a smaller answer model (gpt-5-mini) than competitors — which the author reads as evidence the gain comes from the context layer, not the model. The paper closes by pointing at the frontier: decision-level context (capturing *why* an organization decided what it did) and the benchmark gaps no current eval measures — latency, token efficiency, and context-rot resistance.
