# Cyborgs Will Kill the Corporation — On Excorporations and the Death of the Firm

**Author:** Octopusyarn (Substack: @octopusyarn)
**Published:** April 9, 2026
**Stats:** 48 likes, 10 comments, 16 shares

The piece opens with a Gregor Samsa parody — a developer friend who bought a Mac Mini, disappeared for six weeks, and re-emerged as a human-AI hybrid. The author argues that language like "AI assistants" understates what's happening; these are not subordinate tools.

## Key Frameworks Referenced

**Donna Haraway (1985 "Cyborg"):** The cyborg is "a hybrid of organism and machine where the boundary between the two can no longer be drawn." The author extends Haraway's social/political analysis into the economic domain.

**Ronald Coase (1937, transaction cost economics):** The piece calls this "the best boring question ever asked" — why companies exist. Coase's answer was transaction costs. As the author puts it: "The corporation is a machine for reducing the cost of getting humans to work together." AI breaks this because agent swarms make external coordination costs approach zero.

**Primavera De Filippi ("extitution"):** Contrasted with institutions. Institutions have roles and rules; extitutions are "defined by identities and relationships." They emerge from repeated interaction rather than paperwork. Wikipedia is cited as a large-scale example.

**Venkatesh Rao:** Referenced for the concept of an "agent factory" — two people running interlocking agent infrastructures across a shared folder and metadata server.

## The Cyborg Model

The cyborg is described as having a human interior with an AI agent exoskeleton. Agents "do the gripping and lifting while the human provides direction, judgment, and taste." Like arthropods, cyborgs molt — swapping agents as goals change.

## Why Humans Remain Essential

1. **Embodied cognition and judgment** — "good judgment looks more like vibes than algorithms." Intuition is built through lived experience, lives in the nervous system, and can't be uploaded.
2. **Trust requires bodies** — "Skin in the game requires skin, attached to a nervous system that feels consequences." Two cyborgs collaborating are fundamentally two humans deciding to be vulnerable.
3. **Finitude and choice** — A human changes because of age, mortality, and the necessity of choosing. An agent that "can be forked, rolled back, or run indefinitely doesn't face this pressure."

## The Excorporation

Where a corporation is an "in-corporation" (declaring a collective body into existence through legal agreements), an **excorporation** takes activity *out* of a single cyborg's body. It is "constituted at the intersection of two or more cyborgs, like two exoskeletons interlocking at the joint." It emerges for a specific collaboration and dissolves when interaction stops. No paperwork, no bylaws, no persistence beyond purpose.

Trust in excorporations is multilateral — "a rhizomatic web of permissions" across databases, agents, and calendars.

## Three Destinations for Corporate Disintegration

1. **Excorporations** — Activities requiring deep judgment and domain knowledge, unbundled as the most agentic employees leave to capture more surplus.
2. **Plankton** — Automated processes that "break free of the corporate shell" and drift through the economy as commodity services.
3. **Protocols** — Infrastructure functions (payment processing, matching algorithms, communication standards) shed their institutional wrapper. Stripe is "halfway to being a protocol"; Ethereum and Uniswap are cited as fully autonomous infrastructure.

## Two Possible Futures

The piece suggests a transitional period of "zombie firms" where employees collect paychecks while agents do the work undetected. Corporations will lobby for legal moats, but "the tree that insists on pulling everything through vertically is going to find itself outcompeted."

The closing "Soft Part" contrasts competitive thinking ("picking low-hanging fruit is for plankton") with a relationship-centered approach. "Relationships will turn out to be the best investment" — trust between humans, not better agents, is what matters.
