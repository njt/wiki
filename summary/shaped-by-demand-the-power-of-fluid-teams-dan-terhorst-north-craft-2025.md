Dan Terhorst-North's Craft 2025 talk ("Shaped by Demand: The Power of Fluid Teams") argues that organisation design has been solving the wrong problem since the 2000s. Cross-functional teams solved the eight-person alignment problem; the open question is how you get 80, then 800 people pointing in the same direction. His goal: "build the best structure we can to do the best work we can for now, knowing that it's going to change" — with the "for now" doing the heavy lifting. Big consultancies that deploy cookie-cutter structures have no stated outcome: "they go billable hours."

The foundation is a five-part demand taxonomy: delivery, discovery (problem-space and solution-space), kaizen (improvement of the whole system of work, including teaching someone as first-class work), BAU, and failure demand — work you should never have had to do. Only the first three are discretionary; backlogs show only delivery, so the mandatory demand hiding in plain sight goes unmeasured. The flagship case is Gene Kim's HP LaserJet firmware story: a team at ~95% failure demand with two hours a week of discretionary time, driven to 40% by eight months of deliberately dull automation and test-writing. "Guess what, kids, it doesn't matter what methodology you're following, if you've got two hours a week to get work done."

He then demolishes the stable-long-lived-team orthodoxy. Tuckman's forming-storming-norming-performing came from a meta-study where 50% of the groups were therapy groups and 25% HR training — only 25% were actual work groups; later studies (US military) show the stages run concurrently and fast, and ThoughtWorks' shared culture plus hiring bar got new teams "performing within days, sometimes hours." Long-lived teams also presume homogeneous work, which Bank of America's estate of 30–40 ancient legacy systems contradicts quarter on quarter — "Scrum isn't set up to deal with that." So he inverts the usual move: demand is the fixed piece; shape teams to fit demand, not demand to fit teams.

The playbook is demand-led planning: make all demand visible, allocate discretionary spend with a no-zero rule (features are "walking around money"; no discovery means "you're flying in the dark"), have sponsors pitch to everyone in one room, and let people self-assemble under governing constraints (3–10 people; full autonomy defined as never blocked on another team; all pitched work gets done) and enabling constraints (mover and tenure limits) tuned like dials — John Lewis started with "only two people are allowed to stay in the team" to force tacit knowledge out of heads, later flipped to max-movers to stabilise metrics. Roving "bumblebees" (max three teams each, three per team) carry knowledge between teams. Identity belongs to the program, not the squad — Spotify's 2012 tribes had the right idea but made squads long-lived; squad membership should be "kind of an implementation detail." The lead is whoever cares enough to pitch the work; a manager "manages a system of work that has people in it"; appraisals and recruiting move out of band to guilds. The compounding payoff: John Lewis went from two exhausting, tear-filled days to planning 100 people's quarter by lunchtime, because after a few cycles planning becomes deltas.

The gist's own digest is candid about the gaps: no counter-evidence beyond anecdotes (he concedes it — "empirical, anecdotal sense... before Dave Snowden comes for me with pitchforks"), no answer for 500 people, unpicked work and unpicked people, careers and pay explicitly deferred, annual budgeting vs quarterly planning, remote dismissed as "a bit rubbish," and no opt-out for people who need stability. The Q&A adds two clarifications: on-call and ownership stay with the program team, and on-call signals become a rich source of kaizen pitches; and full autonomy is not binary — "autonomy through alignment," rules of the road, the minimum constraints that let people "surprise you," tracing back to mission command.
