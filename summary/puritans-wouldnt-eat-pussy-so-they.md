Renee argues that colonial women who were captured by Native American communities and repeatedly refused to return home reveal, by their choices, what Puritan society denied them: autonomy over their bodies, pleasure, property, and marriages. She documents the pattern with colonial observers' own words — Franklin in 1753, Colden in 1747 — and with the case studies of Eunice Williams, Mary Jemison, and Frances Slocum, each of whom chose to stay with their adoptive communities for life.

The article then traces what white society did about this "problem": the captivity narrative, America's first homegrown literary genre, in which ministers like Increase Mather edited women's accounts into redemption propaganda. Scholars (Derounian-Stodola, Toulouse) treat the genre as a targeted response to women not coming back. From there the piece follows the template's descendants — the dime-novel and Hollywood damsel-in-distress, Slotkin's argument that the genre served as self-absolution for conquest, and Faludi's tracing of it into the post-9/11 rescue myth (Jessica Lynch, The Searchers' rewrite of Cynthia Ann Parker).

Renee is explicit about the darker context: Native captive-taking was real, but largely a mourning-war practice of replacing family members lost to European disease that had killed 50–90% of some populations. Her provocative thesis is that female pleasure — sexual and otherwise — is an honest diagnostic of how much a society values women's autonomy, and that colonial women voted with their feet.
