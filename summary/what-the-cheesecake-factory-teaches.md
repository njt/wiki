Arcolano's essay uses The Cheesecake Factory's 250-item menu as an extended metaphor for what AI coding agents have done to software: a small set of cheap primitives remixed into combinatorial abundance, where the kitchen absorbs the cost of complexity but the *customer* pays the cost of choosing.

The restaurant half of the argument is behavioral economics: choice overload is real, diners cope with heuristics (shrink the menu, pick the default, follow the crowd), and the arrangement persists because it's only dinner — the stakes are low and the huge menu looks valuable while positioning the restaurant as the safe default for group meals.

The software half is sharper. Coding agents hand organizations the same enormous menu, and most organizations are worse at choosing than diners because they've spent their whole lives on a prix fixe menu — a short list of options curated by their constraints, customers, tech stack, and habits. Their responses to the sudden abundance look like "lack of ambition" but are really unpracticed choice: reuse AI on what they already do, run the same roadmap faster, ship another in-app chatbot.

Unlike dinner, a company's unchosen dishes are real losses a competitor may order. The essay's prescriptions: choose ambitiously *and* decisively (the new failure mode is also ordering one of everything and finishing nothing), treat mapping the menu — figuring out what's now buildable — as someone's actual job, and start by re-pricing the 2026 roadmap's shelved bets at today's costs. Closing line: soon everyone will have a great kitchen; the winners will be those who know what to order.
