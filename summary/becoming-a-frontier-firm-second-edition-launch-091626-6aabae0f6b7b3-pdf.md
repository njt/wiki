Microsoft's second-edition "Frontier Playbook" (launch filename dated 2026-09-16), signed by Kathleen Hogan (EVP, Strategy and Transformation) and Katy George (CVP, AI & Work Transformation). It is a self-portrait of Microsoft's own in-progress AI transformation, offered as a reusable methodology rather than a product pitch — though every pattern happens to route toward Microsoft's stack. The playbook is organised around five elements: (A) set business ambition and goals, (B) build your diffusion engine, (C) invest in your people, (D) codify your advantage and controls, (E) safeguard your security.

Element A anchors AI to four enterprise outcome goals — the Frontier Firm Success Framework: enrich employee experience, reinvent customer engagement, reshape business processes, bend the curve on innovation. Each goal has a named executive owner; each function runs its own AI Transformation Office, coordinated by a small central "learning accelerator". The recurring framing: efficiency is the floor, but experience, innovation, and growth are the ceiling — every initiative is challenged to justify itself beyond cost-out.

Element B is the core methodology. Its premise: "a tool licensed and rolled out to 100,000 employees does not change how the work gets done" — the operating system around the tool does. Built from 100+ internal case studies and Continuous Improvement discipline ("a new science of knowledge work"), it offers three recipes: **Persona Acceleration** (make a role's work visible, map AI to real moments, peer-led huddles; manager role-modeling is the single biggest adoption predictor, worth +17 points in recognized AI value); **AI-Powered Process Redesign** ("lean before agents" — the Supply Chain team de-wasted end-to-end workflows and built a shared data foundation before deploying 111 purpose-built agents, cutting cycle time 75% on selected workflows; dropping an agent into an unrepaired process "just automates the dysfunction"); and **AI-First Possibility** (zero-base the work in small expert sandboxes — the Copilot Cowork team ran nine humans as meta-engineers, meta-designers and meta-PMs producing 18.6K commits / 9.3M lines of code at 123 commits/day, first release in 35 days, on spec-driven development with spec/evals/context as the shared layer). Three levels of AI maturity span the recipes: human-with-assistant, human-agent teams, and human-led agent-operated. A measurement model separates input (2–4 weeks) → throughput (1–3 months) → output (3–6 months) → outcome (6+ months), so a stall with healthy inputs is diagnosed as a habit-formation problem, not a tooling problem. "CI + AI = Capability Add": continuous improvement removes waste, while AI's Capability Add creates new strategic output — predictive quality intelligence, real-time sensemaking, parallel cognition, dynamic opportunity sensing.

Element C, self-described as possibly the most important section, covers people: engage employees with honesty and optimism ("future-proof their careers, not all jobs as they exist today"); reimagine the people model (T-shaped roles, engineers as "full-stack product builders", judgment/taste/delegation-to-agents as the premium skills, early-career roles redesigned rather than automated away via the PRAISE two-way apprenticeship, value-stream operating models); reinvent change management as continuous experimentation (Camp AIR bootcamps, Learn-Do-Teach, peer huddles); and give employees agency through LinkedIn skills data, internal talent marketplaces, and learning in the flow of work.

Element D is the playbook's distinctive technical claim: codify your "secret sauce" — point of view, proprietary performance data, taste, and risk boundaries — into **private evals**, because "if you don't define what 'good' looks like, AI defaults to something generic." The "law of hill-climbing": any enterprise whose competitive advantage cannot be measured by its own evals, improved through its own expertise, and preserved across any foundation model will be arbitraged. The "hill-climbing machine" is a reinforcement-learning environment where evals define "good", context and harness steer execution, and foundation models stay interchangeable; data residency, model independence, and tenant isolation (the tenant as control plane governing humans and agents identically) keep the compounding intelligence inside your boundary. Nadella frames capturing tacit knowledge in model weights as "probably the most important sovereign consideration for firms."

Element E covers security at machine speed along four dimensions: defend with AI (a Security Graph, since human-centric defenses cannot keep up), safely enable AI (agents as independent actors with identity, risk-based permissions, full-lifecycle governance), trust AI with your data (accelerated classification, "data debt"), and prepare for human-led and agent-led attacks (exposure management, patching, identity, network isolation). The conclusion restates the five elements, admits "we will get some of this wrong," and invites feedback. Throughout, impact numbers are self-reported and each case-study page carries the caveat that results are "in the context of our company."
