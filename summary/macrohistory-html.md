Basil Halperin condenses the essential lessons of macroeconomics and macrohistory into "six (plus or minus) facts," built around a chain of charts. The framing move is to take GDP per person as the measure of how well a society is doing — defended by showing that richer countries are, on average, happier, longer-lived, less burdened by infant mortality, working fewer hours, and trusting their neighbours more. He concedes the correlation-not-causation caveat, the CO2 emissions downside, and then runs with it.

The facts: (0) GDP is the yardstick; (1) everyone used to be poor — humanity lived in a "Malthusian hellscape" where wage gains were always eaten by population growth, for all of recorded history until ~1750; (2) the Industrial Revolution was the single most important event in human history, lifting growth from ~0.01%/year through 0.1% to 1% to today's ~2%; (3) growth at the technological frontier has been a "shockingly steady" 2% per year for 150 years, with every invention showing up as a continuation of the same line; (4) incomes vary enormously across countries; (5) there are growth miracles (Japan, South Korea) and growth disasters (Argentina) and countries that never grew (Nigeria); (6) over the long sweep of a million years, world GDP's doubling time has shrunk from 100,000 years to ~35 years — aggregate growth is speeding up.

The essay closes on the genuinely interesting point: forecasting the future depends on which prior you anchor to — the steady 150-year 2% trend, or the million-year record of acceleration. Halperin calls the present "a weird time" and notes the six facts doubled as the pedagogical spine of his undergraduate macro course: the one thing worth hoping students retain is a gut-level sense that *everyone used to be poor*.
