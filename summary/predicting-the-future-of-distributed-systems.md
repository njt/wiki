A conference talk by Colin Breck, principal engineer for energy storage at Tesla, transcribed from video into a ytx gist: a four-part structured summary (arguments, quotes, adoptables, unanswered questions) followed by the full speaker transcript. Breck's stated goal is not to look smart but "to help organizations make effective engineering decisions" — the talk is his own "agonizing" over choices he doesn't want to regret next year, organized by Jeff Bezos's one-way vs. two-way door framework. The costly failure mode is not heavyweight process on trivial decisions but the reverse: "mak[ing] what you think is a two way door decision when it really was a one way door decision."

Object storage is his two-way-door substrate. The S3 API has become universal — implemented by other clouds, software, and on-prem appliances — so an app depending only on Kubernetes and the S3 API "can basically move it anywhere." He traces the lineage: Jay Kreps's "The Log" (reliable building blocks cut distributed-system implementation "from years to weeks" — Breck's gloss: "In other words, two way doors"), his own 2012 Azure system of stateless brokers over object storage (brokers scaled "instantaneously, without repartitioning data"; no Paxos needed), then the decomposition wave — Aurora ("the log is the database"), Snowflake, WarpStream (later acquired by Confluent), SlateDB, InfluxDB — all using object storage as primary storage. Above it: Parquet as the interoperable format, catalogs (Iceberg, Delta Lake, Hudi, and Duck Lake, announced "two days ago") to preserve format investments, DuckDB and DataFusion extending decomposition into query optimization and execution as libraries ("some of the leading database experts in the world... working for your company"), and database-per-customer/device models (Cloudflare D1, MotherDuck) blurring edge and cloud. His prediction: object storage increasingly used for transactional and operational workloads, not just analytics.

Programming models, by contrast, are all one-way doors. Today's container is a stack of undifferentiated plumbing — keys, certs, JSON serialization, gRPC, database and Kafka clients, logging, metrics — with business logic as a thin slice, repeated across hundreds of apps that each re-solve state, durable execution, and retries; Log4J exposed the inventory problem. His wish is that these capabilities sink into infrastructure so libraries patch like OS updates and developers "don't even need to care." The candidates come in three flavors — "adopt our API" (Akka, Temporal), "give us arbitrary code" (wasmCloud), and the uncategorizable (Golem event-sources the WASM VM; Unison is a whole new language) — but adoption is blocked by huge investment, engineers "skeptical about giving up control," survival risk ("In five years is the one I picked going to be dead"), and vendor lock-in: "a one way door decision after one way door decision."

AI may be the forcing function. Agentic AI "looks a lot like durable actors"; AI workflows look like durable execution frameworks; MCP/RAG/A2A/ACP are "really just interfaces and protocols for systems integration" — so operationalizing AI is "really just systems engineering," and because AI teams "move fast and take risks," it may be where the new programming models take hold before spreading to IoT, manufacturing, and commerce. Caution via Spolsky's iceberg secret: easy onboarding "is not where the hard problems are" — bet on platforms that solved durable execution before the AI pivot. He closes on Peter Alvaro — "Abstractions are going to leak, so make the abstractions fluid," read as an invitation to find as many two-way doors as possible — and one unanswered question: "How safe is it to just keep doing what we already know?"

The gist's fourth section is a genuine critique, and unusually sharp for a video summary: transactional semantics on object storage are asserted but never explained (multi-object consistency, concurrency control, OLTP latency on a blob store; S3 Express One Zone gets one sentence); cost and egress fees go unquantified, though moving petabytes turns two-way doors into one-way doors; concentration risk is invisible (if logs, warehouses, and AI infrastructure all sit on S3-class storage, S3 outages become systemic); the Log4J problem is "solved by wishful thinking"; "just systems engineering" flattens what is genuinely new in AI (nondeterminism, model versioning, evals, human oversight); the Tesla-shaped hole — one 2012 anecdote from a different company as his only personal evidence; WarpStream's acquisition quietly undermining the independence of his two-way doors; and the question of who classifies the doors, i.e. how you discover you misclassified one.
