Maciej Cegłowski's October 2016 talk at Web Camp Zagreb is the classic skeptic's tour of superintelligence alarmism. He opens with the Manhattan Project's worry that the first nuclear test might ignite the atmosphere, lays out the six premises Nick Bostrom's argument requires — thinking minds exist, the brain is ordinary matter, the space of possible minds is large, hardware has orders of magnitude left, computers run on fast timescales, recursive self-improvement is possible — escalates them into a deliberately absurd joke-robot takeover scenario, and announces that he is "not trying to convince you of it, but vaccinate you against it."

The inside-view rebuttals attack the load-bearing premises: "general intelligence" is too wooly a concept to maximize and may be a tradeoff; intelligence doesn't confer control (Stephen Hawking can't talk his cat into a carrier; Australia lost the Emu War); complex minds plausibly have complex motivations, so the paper clip maximizer would more likely write poems about paper clips; we can't build secure webcams, let alone a "moral fixed point" for a recursively self-improving mind; and the AI we actually have — opaque networks fed on massive data — has no recursive-tweak knob and took the slow, data-hungry road instead.

The outside view is the talk's heart: AI alarmism behaves like a religion, offering immortality, an apocalypse, urgency and a need for money, and multiplying enormous stakes by tiny probabilities to justify neglecting present problems — a rhetorical shape Cegłowski recognizes from communist Poland. Believers start cosplaying their fantasy of a hyperintelligent AI: rationalists calling other people NPCs, "rich dudes who think they are Batman," a plutocrat hiring coders to hack the simulation. Meanwhile the actual ethics of machine learning — exploitation, carelessness, surveillance, power — go unattended.

The close is an alchemy analogy: our theory of mind stands where chemistry stood in the seventeenth century — diligent, partly right, missing the decisive instruments, and obsessed with a neurotoxic false lead. His hoped-for effect is that the audience leaves "a little dumber than you started it," more immune to the seductions that bedevil smart people.
