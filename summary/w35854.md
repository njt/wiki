Marius A. K. Ring (NBER Working Paper 35854, October 2026) studies the economic effects of Norway's controversial 2021–2024 tax reforms, which raised the wealth tax rate, cut the assessment discount on business equity (nearly doubling the marginal tax rate on business wealth from 0.47% to 0.88%), raised dividend taxes, and progressively closed migration-related capital gains tax loopholes.

On migration: the reforms did trigger real outmigration, but it was small and concentrated — excess outmigration of about 1.78% among the top 0.1% of the wealth distribution (roughly 74–98 individuals in total), mostly in 2022–2023 and timed around the November 2022 closure of the five-year exit loophole. High-profile billionaire departures to Switzerland dominated headlines but there was no excess outmigration of startup founders, tech founders, or inventors (measured via LinkedIn/Revelio Labs and patent data), and startup investors did not leave either.

On revenue: reform-driven outmigration cost about 0.65 BNOK, or 2% of counterfactual wealth tax revenues — reducing the revenue gain from 75% (no migration counterfactual) to 71.5% by 2024. Avoidance did not come close to offsetting the mechanical revenue increase.

On firms: comparing Norwegian-owned firms with firms owned by tax-exempt foreigners (using Orbis ownership data merged with the Norwegian shareholder register), he finds no evidence of increased dividends, reduced investment, or excess bankruptcies after the reform. His calibrated model shows the investment effect is theoretically ambiguous — wealth taxes can even lower the user cost of capital when owners' elasticity of intertemporal substitution is low. Conclusion: the short-run economic costs of wealth taxation are far more limited than the public debate implies.
