Alphabet Security's Joseph Valente and Michal Zalewski argue in ACM Queue that zero trust's application-centric model — Google's own BeyondCorp — is breaking under three simultaneous pressures: AI agents requesting access at roughly 10× human rates, exponential growth in the volume and sensitivity of data being reasoned over, and attackers who have weaponized LLMs against static detection. BeyondCorp's founding assumptions (accessors are human, actions occur at human speed, applications are the correct trust boundary) no longer hold.

Beyond Zero shrinks the trust boundary to the individual action on an individual resource and evaluates it in realtime, for humans and agents alike. The architecture has four components in a continuous feedback loop: **autonomous governance** (an AI-maintained "enterprise world model" of who accessors are, what data sensitivity is, and how work is assigned — precomputed because access-time latency budgets can't fit inference); **event intake** (server, client/DLP, and agent-activity signals, split between a hot cache and a long-term store); a **reasoning engine** (fast policy evaluation plus slower anomaly inference, emitting allow/deny/challenge); and **challenge infrastructure** (granular challenges — justification, security-key touch, approval, biometric — versus durable containments that revoke access). Static policies form the floor; dynamic reasoning is the ceiling. Decisions feed back as risk attributes for subsequent evaluations, and agent intent (prompt inputs, execution plans, tool invocations) becomes a policy input to catch intent misalignment and limit prompt injection.

Worked scenarios — a contractor using stolen credentials, an administrator whose behavior suddenly suggests agentic assistance, and the rogue "SalesGenie" agent that BeyondCorp would have waved through — show the graduated response in action. The article closes with an industry agenda: standardized agent-introspection APIs, agentic-identity and request-annotation standards, and external pluggable policy evaluation as a first-class SaaS feature, with NIST's agent-security effort cited as a vehicle.
