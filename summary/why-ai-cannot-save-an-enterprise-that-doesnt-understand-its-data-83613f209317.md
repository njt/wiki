Younss and Mustapha Fonsau argue that AI amplifies whatever understanding an enterprise already has of its own data — and mercilessly exposes wherever that understanding is missing. A board reviewing $37 million of exposure needs more than an accurate number; it needs the relationships, dependencies and reasoning behind it, which organisations have always supplied with people, in meetings, and the reasoning rarely survives the meeting. AI does not remove that need — it removes the pause in which the reasoning used to happen.

The piece builds a thirteen-stage chain from data to learning (data → context → information → composition → relationships → meaning → knowledge → understanding → judgment → decision → action → outcome → learning), showing how the reasoning can break at any arrow, using a worked financial example where three seemingly separate positions hide a shared-supplier concentration. It grounds the semantics in Gruber's ontology definition, the EDM Council's FIBO, BCBS 239, and Evans's bounded contexts, arguing that enterprises always operate on a conceptualisation — most just lack an explicit specification of it.

The sharpest warning: when an AI recommendation triggers a workflow, an interpretation becomes an action before anyone reviews it, and fluency proves nothing about which concepts, sources and rules were used. The practical test for anyone granting AI more autonomy: can you explain how your systems move from record to interpretation to action and back to revised understanding? Where the answer is no, automation carries authority nobody can supervise.
