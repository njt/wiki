Kyle Kingsbury (Aphyr), creator of the Jepsen testing library, speaks at an Antithesis event on why building systems is hard and why testing distributed systems is harder. The receipts first: in 13 years of consulting he has tested about 50 systems and found 243 publicly discussable bugs — 63 lost updates and other data loss, 35 ordering violations, 42 crashes, 12 split-brain cases, 8 garbage reads ("you put in the number five and it comes back as a tuna fish sandwich"). Databases are better than the 2010–2015 wild west — better consensus, declared fault models — but he still finds bugs in nearly everything he tests.

The body of the talk is five tactics. (1) **Fault injection**: distributed systems are defined by recurrent *partial* failure — crashes, pauses (a 20ms EBS flush that sometimes takes 200 seconds, making a live node look dead), clock skew (a Cassandra node whose clock flips to 2040 leaves an undeletable tombstone), partitions, and disk corruption ("a portal to the underworld"); inject them during the test. (2) **The closed world**: many correctness properties (counter equals increments, every shipping record has a purchase) require total knowledge of all inputs and outputs, so test in an isolated cluster or an isolated keyspace — his example of a X/Y invariant broken by a forgotten corner service shows how unseen writers poison checkers. (3) **Indefinite errors**: every operation has three outcomes — definitely ok, definitely failed, or unknown — and "any indefinite operation... is logically concurrent with everything else the system does for the rest of the system's life"; checkers must reason over ranges (counter ≥ acknowledged, ≤ attempted) because error codes don't reliably say which case you're in. (4) **Concurrency structure**: record invocation/completion timing and check histories against an explicitly chosen consistency model — two histories with identical outcomes can differ only in timing, making one a bug and the other linearizability-legal; the "frozen accounts can't make transfers" requirement is incomplete until someone names the model. (5) **Generative/property-based testing**: two appends and one read already yield a 3×3 matrix of legal outcome sets ("we took a single-threaded program, and somehow we got concurrent execution out of it"), so enumerate properties instead — no lost committed data, no duplicates, no garbage, ordering constraints — and let random inputs find what humans never thought to write down (his Jepsen run found a serializability violation in Postgres's own tested isolation level almost immediately).

The conclusion turns the argument on the present. At the base of every software system are people — increasingly subcontracted through data-labeling intermediaries ("Uber for thinking" at Merkle) whose outputs feed the RL corpora beneath ChatGPT Codex and Claude. Engineers work "in the shadow of the Shoggoth," holding $50k/month token budgets and shipping three AI-generated PRs a day, reviewing cursorily or asking another LLM to review. What's lost is not just labor but context and *metis* — Detienne and Vernant's cunning craft wisdom — because, per Bainbridge's 1983 *Ironies of Automation*, the intuition only develops by doing the work. He reports (with hedged, anecdotal honesty) that LLM-assisted systems fail his tests "much more common[ly]," and argues for the "complete breakfast" of correctness: reason-able designs, proofs, types, tests at every scale, simulation environments (FoundationDB's Flow, Antithesis, Erlang's pulse scheduler), chaos engineering, and observability.
