# Vibe Coding and the Maker Movement

**Author:** Sachin (published on Substack's *Technically*)
**Date:** February 26, 2026

## Central Thesis

The author draws parallels between today's "vibe coding" phenomenon and the Maker Movement (~2005–2015), arguing that vibe coding skipped a crucial developmental phase and may end similarly — with value accumulating upstream rather than with the practitioners.

## Key Arguments

### Maker Movement as Predecessor

Sachin identifies the Maker Movement as vibe coding's "spiritual predecessor." Both generated low-value output (the Maker term "crapjects" for pointless 3D-printed objects parallels vibe coding "slop"). The author notes that Maker culture produced "the first internet-native network intellectuals," citing Chris Anderson (left *Wired* to start 3D Robotics) and Cory Doctorow (wrote *Makers*).

### Puritanism With a Soldering Iron

The article examines media scholar Fred Turner's 2018 paper, which argued the Maker Movement "had reinvented the theology of the Western Frontier for the digital age." Turner traced a "millenarian structure" — a belief that great transformation is coming and individual discipline determines who makes it through. The author extends this pattern to homebrew computer clubs (1970s), punk zines (1980s), and the early web (1990s) — each a "scenius" (Brian Eno's term) where small groups played with tools considered toys by the mainstream. Crucially, "The tools were unproductive on purpose" — nobody expected hobbyist projects to ship to customers.

### What's Different About Vibe Coding

The author argues vibe coding "broke this pattern" by skipping the scenius phase entirely: "It was deployed directly to the general public, and almost immediately into the codebases of enterprise companies." There was no protected playground period for playful, useless knowledge accumulation.

This creates what Sachin describes as "hypomania" — genuine productivity gains alongside an impaired evaluative faculty. The result is "evaluative anesthesia": you lose the ability to distinguish between "this is good" and "I feel good making this." The author draws a comparison to 1960s hippies trying LSD — "sometimes you may have a breakthrough, or you may have a breakdown."

### The Maker Movement's Quiet Ending

The Maker Movement's central promise — that "distributed digital fabrication would bring manufacturing back to America" — failed to materialize. The author applies Joel Spolsky's framework of "commoditizing your complement": cheap 3D printers made prototyping nearly free, but "the deep, compounding knowledge of how to actually manufacture things at scale" remained in Shenzhen. The pattern: "cheap tools democratize one layer, and the layer beneath captures the surplus."

The same is happening with vibe coding: rapid prototyping threatens SaaS models, but value "accumulates at the model layer, in the training data, in the infrastructure." Vibe coders "risk becoming interchangeable."

### A New Metaphor: Consumption

The author proposes **consumption** as a replacement for craft-based framing — specifically, consumption of "surplus intelligence" (AI's cognitive energy). This aligns with Rachel Thomas's comparison of vibe coding to the "dark flow state when you gamble."

Four productive ways to think about this consumption:

1. **Taste as a Residue of Expenditure:** When production is near-free, the scarce resource becomes knowing what should exist. The author invokes William Gibson's *Pattern Recognition* — someone paid simply to say yes or no based on finely tuned instincts.

2. **Attention as a Combustion Byproduct:** Building in public is a "performance of surplus." The "built this in a weekend" posts often produce middling products, but the performance captures audience and reputation. This is "structurally identical to how content creators already operate."

3. **Projects as Gifts:** Treating vibe-coded output as gifts (open source tools, free utilities) creates social bonds and reciprocal obligation. The gift economy "explains why it works psychologically" better than strategic career-building framing.

4. **Signal Capture Before Upstream Absorption:** Every prompt, iteration, and correction becomes training data for model providers — "unpaid labor for the infrastructure layer." But those who structure that signal into proprietary datasets build what the author calls a "data fortress."

### Sustainability of the Consumption Frame

The author concludes that craft-based framing — the "emotional architecture of craft" assuming inner struggle and transformation — is "a recipe for burnout" with AI tools. The consumption metaphor shifts the question from "what does this say about me as a maker" to "what's the most interesting thing I can spend this on."
