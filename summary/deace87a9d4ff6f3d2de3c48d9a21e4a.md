A ytx gist (key-points digest + full speaker transcript) of Dylan Beattie's conference keynote "There Is No Such Thing as Plain Text." Beattie — creator of the Rockstar programming language and director of Ursatile — argues that what developers call "plain text" is really "7-bit ASCII that opens in Windows Notepad," an encoding designed for mechanical teleprinters, "when a computer was a typewriter with a memory." The quirks are fossilized constraints: Ctrl+C is literally the end-of-text control character, CRLF vs. LF tracks whether your OS lineage had device drivers, and DEL is all-bits-set because you can't unpunch a paper tape. The closing applause is transcribed as "Kraft," suggesting the Craft conference; the gist is undated.

The eighth bit was a regulatory vacuum: every country invented code pages (CP437, KOI8) to reinterpret the top half of ASCII, so the same bytes mean different letters — data isn't lost in transit, it's reinterpreted. Hence Billy Joel's live album permanently titled "Kohuept" in streaming databases. Unicode won by being free, universal, and opinion-free, but it deliberately refuses to answer the hard questions: it hands you code points, combining characters, normalization forms, and collations, and leaves the judgment calls to engineers who mostly don't know they're making them. "Are these two strings the same?" has no context-free answer — two renderings of "Motley Crue" can look identical, sound identical, be binary-unequal, and still be equivalent under all four normalization forms. Alphabetical order is cultural, not mathematical: Latin1_General ("think like an American"), Finnish_Swedish, and Danish_Norwegian collations each encode a different alphabet, and Danish orthography forces a two-column sort pattern because "there is not enough data in the text to get it right."

Politics is the explicit thesis: the 1948 Danish spelling reform (distancing Danish from German after WWII) and its 2011 reversion (Google wasn't finding Åarhus and it was bad for tourism) determine sort order in your database; Apple makes the Taiwanese flag vanish when your iPhone region is set to mainland China; "if you try to find any technology which has no politics in it, there's nothing there." Encoding failures also impersonate security breaches: the "Chinese in the Windows event logs" incident was a faulty network switch dropping one byte every three minutes, shunting UTF-16LE text sideways into the CJK block — twenty-seven hours to diagnose. UTF-8 is "one of the most brilliant hacks in the history of technology," and emoji is old Unicode machinery repurposed (combining characters for skin tones, zero-width joiner for the female astronaut, regional indicators for flags — England's is "a hack").

The talk closes with a shibboleth, not a spec: ask "do you know Pike Matchbox?" — the letters shared between Ukrainian Cyrillic and Latin, visible on every Ukrainian license plate. If they know it, they've thought about encoding; if not, "they're going to send you a file that works on that machine. Maybe it works on your machine, maybe not." The final dedication goes to a YouTube commenter who watched the whole hour and left: "Leave politics out of software." Unusually, the digest also carries a candid omissions section: no prescriptive guidance (charset declarations, BOM, BCP 47), no homoglyph/security discussion despite an hour proving lookalike text is trivially constructible, East Asian legacy encodings absent, and folklore presented as fact (CP/M "evolved out of Minix"; emoji credited to "Shigetaka Morita" rather than Kurita; UTF-16 "every letter takes two bytes" contradicted by the talk's own surrogate-pair emoji examples).
