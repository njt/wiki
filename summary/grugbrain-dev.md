A cult-classic essay on software development, written in a deliberately simple
"caveman" voice by an experienced developer who calls himself "grug." The
essay distills decades of hard-won lessons into ~40 short, funny aphorisms
about complexity, abstraction, testing, tools, and the human side of
programming. Its central thesis: complexity is the apex predator of software
development, and the best weapon against it is the word "no."

The essay covers: why complexity is the root of all software suffering; why
saying "no" to features and abstractions is the senior engineer's primary duty;
why premature factoring causes more harm than good ("wait for cut points to
emerge"); why integration tests are the sweet spot between fragile unit tests
and opaque end-to-end tests; why tools (debuggers, IDE completion, logging)
matter more than process; why type systems are valuable primarily for
autocompletion, not correctness proofs; why DRY and Separation of Concerns
should be applied with restraint; why simple APIs for common cases beat
configurable APIs for all cases; why front-end development has been captured by
complexity; and why impostor syndrome is not just normal but universal.

The essay is notable for its voice — the "grug" persona makes genuinely sharp
engineering observations land harder by dressing them in self-deprecating
humor. Its popularity (frequently at the top of Hacker News) reflects how many
experienced developers recognize their own career arc in grug's journey from
overconfident "big brain" to humble pragmatist. The essay's closing litany
("complexity is *very*, *very* bad") has become a meme in engineering culture,
but the meme works because the observation is true.
