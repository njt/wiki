Laurie Winkless (Forbes) interviews the three authors of *Transportation and the Shape of Cities* (Princeton University Press, Aug 2026) — Christof Spieler, David Copeland-Loredo, and Mandi Chapa, all Houston-linked planner/engineer/architects. The book's thesis: physical space, not policy or politics, is the primary constraint on urban transport, and the US debate fails because nobody draws the wish-list to scale.

Each of the five major US modes — pedestrian, micromobility, car, train, transit — has its own geometry: a physical footprint it needs to operate safely. The authors visualise this with 500+ scale drawings (a parked car gets a 9×18 ft stall while an office worker gets an 8×8 ft cubicle; a 200-mph train needs ~13,000 ft of turning radius against a bike's ~8 ft). Pedestrians, usually excluded from engineering rigor, are treated as "mechanical objects that move in space" with space and slope requirements like any vehicle.

The interview ranges across network completeness (the car network is legally always complete — developers must connect a driveway; transit gets no such guarantee, and the resulting station scarcity is what produces gentrification effects), mode pairings (walking+transit, bikes+transit), and why "taking space from cars" should be framed as a capacity problem rather than an ideological one.
