A Netflix case study arguing that an LLM judge running in production is best
understood as a *lifecycle*, not a static artifact. The judge evaluates
user-facing recommendation explanations — hundreds of thousands per week,
served to millions of members — and must be built, trained, deployed, and
continuously maintained as the catalog and user base drift.

The framework has four phases. **Birth** builds a domain-specific, human-labeled
benchmark (~900 examples at launch, near-balanced, each fail label carrying a
free-text rationale). **Training** refines each criterion's rubric via
*Reasoning-Aligned Rubric Tuning* (RART), which uses a meta-judge to score the
judge's reasoning against human rationales on *agreed-fail* examples — a
right-verdict-but-wrong-reason case is treated as an error because the same
reason is later fed to the generator as a revision instruction. **Deployment**
puts one judge in two roles: a quality gate and the critic in a
generate→judge→revise loop with a bounded retry budget (drop, don't serve,
flagged explanations). **Monitoring** runs a weekly 300-example
human-in-the-loop sample, requiring the judge to stay within two standard
deviations of the average human rater (a relative, difficulty-adaptive
threshold); drift triggers re-tuning behind a human review gate.

A five-week A/B test over tens of millions of members found judge-aligned
explanations shifted viewing toward novel content and increased successful
browse-to-play sessions versus a no-explanation control, with no
quality-related takedowns. Takeaways: invest in a rationale-annotated
benchmark before the judge, reuse one tuned judge across roles, and plan
monitoring from day one.
