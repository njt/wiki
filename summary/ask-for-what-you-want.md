Robin Sloan updates his 2020 essay on apps as home-cooked meals for the agent era: building a personal app with an AI agent still counts as home cooking ("you're no longer grinding the flour by hand; fine"), and the skill the whole practice exercises is *asking for what you want*. He proposes an exploratory program: one paid month with a top-tier desktop agent (he names the summer 2026 generation: Fable 5.1 or GPT-6 Astra), spent building the app you actually want rather than absorbing an ambient AI presence.

His workflow is document-first: before opening the agent, he spent an hour writing `VISION.md` — a loose, exhaustive, self-contradicting description of a minimal macOS notetaker ("we are going to built a lightweight, super minimal, VERY VERY fast notetaking and reviewing tool!"), with inspirations (Notational Velocity, Jack Cheng's Bebop), explicit non-goals, and a closing "Ask me any questions... then proceed." The agent reads "everything at once," so "more is usually better"; a sensible shortcut is to skip straight to "ask me questions." Then a `PLAN.md`, approval, ten to forty minutes of quiet work — and that night he had Rocksteady, the notes app he uses daily. Refinement is "continuous co-building": use the app immediately, keep a list of missing capabilities, ask over coffee, and nitpick without embarrassment.

The essay is equally a cultural argument. He rejects the consensus of AI as "a constant ambient presence, sparkles in everything": his alternative is "get your wish, then stuff the genie back into the lamp." Personal software has its own rules — use it two months before posting, ideally never post, pick the name yourself, never distribute or publish the code — because "this is not software for glory; it is software for you." He names the costs honestly: he does not read the code the agent writes, and he felt his programming muscles wither "in realtime, the timelapse of the rotting apple," which frightens him enough that he intends to keep the practice "firewalled into this domain." Against that he sets "Battlestar Galactica engineering" (imagine the Cylon uprising; the tool must keep working) and repeated agent-run requests for simplicity and performance reviews. What is profound about the models, he argues, is not intelligence but "the other -ences: patience, diligence." And the closing warning: the moment you get the app you asked for feels like accomplishment — "You didn't. ... You do, at some point, have to use your tools."
