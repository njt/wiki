The running example is Continental's automated-parking program, chosen because Budapest holds the topic's largest headcount.

The talk's central move is a staged trick. At the system level, automated parking looks trivially simple on the Stacey Matrix — "everybody knows what parking is... that hasn't changed in the last 50 years," the hardware is settled, "we've been using C since 20 years" — which would imply pure waterfall. Then comes the reveal: rate the sub-problems, not the system. A live Slido vote places computer vision in the complex quadrant, path planning as complicated, and odometry as simple ("simple as a stone"), with the audience concurring. From that follows the thesis: "If you take the agile hammer and you put everybody in a scrum team with a scrum master, with the product owner and everything, this might not be the ideal way of doing it." One phase can legitimately run agile and waterfall teams side by side; teams are mapped onto the matrix the same way their sub-problems are, which makes architectural decomposition an organizational design tool, not just a technical one.

The talk walks a three-phase lifecycle. Pre-development builds a demo vehicle, shows it to OEMs (Continental's tire-era relationships open the doors), iterates, and wins the contract — Scrum fits, colocation matters, and a change control board is named an anti-pattern ("You have to trust your teams"). Productization meets automotive standards and hundreds of contracted requirements; only tools and processes that "really help us" survive, and synchronization runs through "empowered interfaces" — key people, not standardized rituals ("Maybe we don't need a stand up each and every morning"). Fine tuning is openly political: a base team in Europe ships to an application team colocated with the customer (Japan in the example), which tunes parameters, adapts the UI, and files bugs; the endgame is negotiation ("Aligning with your customer is always politics"), triage ("If you crash your car... you focus on the car crash"), a flight budget for flying base engineers to the application team, and relentless priority communication — "if your firefighters don't know what to do... this will come to a disaster."

The Q&A adds the practical furniture: complexity rating is "more or less done top down by the architects" and partly preference-driven ("there are some people who don't like agile"); DevOps is a hard prerequisite, since without "continuous everything" feedback-driven teams "will result back into this... waterfall workflow because they cannot do anything"; a waterfall team can depend on a Scrum team via timeboxing (deliverables at the boundaries, freedom inside, make-or-buy allowed); parking is 80–90% shared base software with per-OEM clone-and-own UI branches; know-how transfers by moving people between teams, not by documents; mid-project complexity jumps trigger quarterly reorganizations and quick reaction forces; and startups buy OEM access by recruiting ex-industry senior leaders onto their boards ("It's a small world").

The gist's own digest then catalogs what the talk leaves unanswered: no rubric for the complexity rating and no reconciliation when architects and teams disagree; no answer for a "simple" sub-problem turning complex mid-project; the safety-standards contradiction (documentation, traceability) declared but never reconciled — no ISO 26262 or ASPICE is ever named, and AI validation in safety-critical ADAS goes undiscussed; a bus factor of one in the "empowered interfaces" model; technical debt named but never measured; the promised concrete failures ("we made a lot of mistakes, failures, errors.
