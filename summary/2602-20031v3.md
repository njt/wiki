An open-weight Qwen2.5-Coder-32B model can detect when a steering vector was injected into its earlier context — and identify *which* of nine concepts was injected — even though its sampled outputs deny it. The paper extends Anthropic's Lindsey (2025) introspection result to a small, reproducible open model.

The experimental design is clean: a steering vector is applied while the KV cache for the first conversational turn is generated, then removed before the model is asked about it. Detection therefore requires reading cached representations of prior tokens, not reacting to ongoing perturbation. By default the model answers "no" (P("yes") shifts only 0.2%→0.3%), but logit lens analysis shows clear detection signals in middle layers (~50–60) that attenuate in the final layers — the introspective capacity is latent, hidden by late-layer suppression.

The effect is heavily prompt-dependent. Adding a "Pro-Introspection Document" (describing KV-cache information flow and citing Lindsey 2025) raises true-positive detection to 39.9% with only +0.6% false positives; vague or poetic framings work even better (up to 84% balanced accuracy), outperforming the "correct target" framing that explicitly describes activation injection. Concept identification reaches 1.66 bits of mutual information (52% of the nine-concept maximum), correlated with detection sensitivity (r = 0.68), ruling out generic-noise explanations. Norm-matched Gaussian noise injected instead of concept vectors produces shifts two orders of magnitude smaller. Limited replications on Llama-3.3-70B and Qwen2.5-72B show similar effects.

Implications: behavioral evaluation alone may systematically miss capabilities that exist "in hidden form"; safety training may be suppressing self-reports; and defenses that assume models don't notice activation-level interventions (like activation steering) cannot be assumed to hold. The authors are careful about scope — they claim "intervention reporting," not second-order metacognition, and engage seriously with concurrent skeptical work (Singh et al. 2026 arguing models can't distinguish activation interventions from input manipulations).
