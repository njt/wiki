A mixed-methods survey of 448 professional developers at Microsoft (1,193 responses from 8,000 sampled; 1,535 task-responses across five SDLC categories) measuring how much AI autonomy developers accept per task. Open-ended "where do you want AI / where do you refuse AI" answers were classified onto Endsley & Kiris's five-level automation scale (L1 no AI → L5 full automation) and modeled against four cognitive appraisals — value, identity, accountability, demand — plus experience and disposition measures, using mixed-effects ordinal and logistic regression with an LLM-council coding pipeline validated by human inter-rater checks.

Headline: the median accepted level is L3 — AI produces the artifact, the developer approves before it takes effect; 74% of responses sit at or below L3, 26% above it (L4 optional veto: 10%, L5 full automation: 16%). System-facing work (development, quality & risk, infra & ops) medians at L3: routine verification and toil is delegated eagerly while context-sensitive judgment (code review, pre-merge security) is kept. Design & planning and human-facing meta-work median at L2; mentoring, sensitive client communication, and learning are often held entirely at L1.

The paper's real contribution is decomposing "how much autonomy" into two transitions with different predictors. The action boundary (L2→L3, AI goes from suggesting to producing artifacts) is gated by accountability: felt answerability lowers the odds of letting AI act (OR 0.82). The decision-making boundary (L3→L4, approval becomes an optional veto) is gated by identity (OR 0.78) and pushed open by workload (OR 1.20). Among the four appraisals, only identity predicted the overall accepted level; AI experience and risk tolerance lift acceptance everywhere (OR 1.29 and 1.46 at the decision gate), while SE experience and technophilia predict nothing.

Section 6 recasts the results as a flight of cascading canal locks — accountability is the first gate, identity the second, AI fluency and risk tolerance the water — and derives ten anti-patterns for meaningful work design: deferred answerability, right-shifted quality, throughput stampede, shrinking fence, hollow orchestrator, expertise commoditization, thought homogenization, severed pipeline, cognitive debt accrual, and mentoring-by-bot. The prescription: define the protected core of a role by the judgment it requires, written into role definitions and promotion criteria rather than left to tool defaults; keep answerability at the commit; route toil to AI deliberately while protecting judgment-building work; and keep a share of entry-level work as practice rather than throughput.
