A New Yorker essay exploring how people make (or fail to make) life's biggest
choices. Rothman opens with Charles Darwin's famously mediocre pro-and-con list
about marriage and uses it to frame a broader question: why are our big
decisions often less calculated than our small ones?

The piece surveys three approaches. **Decision science**, represented by Steven
Johnson's book *Farsighted*, offers structured processes — scenario planning,
design charettes, full-spectrum analysis — to navigate bounded rationality and
conflicting objectives. Johnson applied these to his own family's cross-country
move, producing a PowerPoint deck and a contractual escape clause.

**Decision theory**, the philosophical tradition, treats decisions as
value-maximizing equations: identify your values, assign weights, and the right
choice pops out. But this breaks down for what the philosopher Edna
Ullmann-Margalit called "opting" — choices that reconfigure your values rather
than maximize existing ones. Choosing to become a parent, emigrate, or switch
careers changes who you are, making the pre-choice self an unreliable judge.

L. A. Paul sharpens this into the problem of **transformative experience**: you
can't know what parenthood is like before experiencing it, so there's no
strictly rational way to decide. Agnes Callard offers a counter in
**aspiration** — the gradual process of trying on new values through "bad"
placeholder reasons until you can grasp the real ones. Aspiration, she argues,
is how we author our own transformations rather than drifting into them.

Rothman weaves his own experience of becoming a father through the essay,
concluding that life in the "meanwhile" resists being reduced to stepping
stones — the meaningful instants follow one another without needing to be
chapters in a decision narrative.
