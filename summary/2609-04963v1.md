# Fractal basins trap latent reasoning

**Authors:** The fetched body omits an author list; attribution is inferred from the acknowledgments ("W.G." — NSF DMS 2436233, NSF CMMI 2440490), the code repo (github.com/GilpinLab/loopscape), and the UT Austin computing facilities named (TACC, Biomedical Research Computing Facility).
**arXiv:** 2609.04963v1, September 2026.

Reasoning models are widely observed to reason longer on harder tasks, but the general mechanism behind these slowdowns — "overthinking" — was unknown. This paper supplies one: reasoning models are dynamical systems whose basins of attraction become *fractal* as task difficulty rises, and whose slowdowns are the signature of **transient chaos** near saddle points.

The key move is a new probe. Treat the model and problem instance as an autonomous discrete-time dynamical system acting on the latent state, then vary the initial latent state along random 2-D slices and label each initialization by its convergence time. On hard tasks (Sudoku, mazes, Countdown, integer linear systems) these convergence-time maps are self-similar fractals, quantified by **basin entropy**; fractality tracks task difficulty across three distinct models (EqR, FPRM, Parcae) and architectures.

The mechanism: saddle points — weakly-unstable, nearly-correct attempted solutions (a Sudoku grid with repeated digits, a maze dead end) — scatter trajectories and delay convergence. Decoding slow trajectories shows they get trapped *near* almost-right answers before backtracking. The saddles are localized with the **fast Lyapunov indicator**, a metric borrowed from asteroid-orbit stability analysis.

The deepest result is a training experiment. A miniaturized looped transformer trained on integer linear systems starts with many stable incorrect fixed points; at a bifurcation (weights as control parameter) the incorrect solutions lose stability and become saddles, solvability and basin entropy jump together, and transient chaos appears — precisely when the model acquires the multi-step reasoning needed to escape incorrect solutions. Only the "core" variables (requiring Gaussian elimination, not direct substitution) produce positive Lyapunov exponents.

The conclusion: reasoning slowdowns are an *inevitable consequence of problem hardness*, not a training bug to be patched — and reasoning traces form a rich new class of dynamical system.
