A NeurIPS-track position paper arguing that "human in the loop" is failing as a safety strategy for AI agents — not because humans are careless, but because current agent systems are designed in ways that defeat oversight and actively erode the cognitive capacities oversight depends on.

The paper's first prong is a diagnosis of the "oversight oversight": agent design centres the agent's own presentation of its processing, dumping dense, fast, distributed streams (reasoning traces, tool calls, multi-agent exchanges) on users whose interfaces don't support the working-memory and situational-awareness load real supervision requires. Users get pushed into "approver" roles, develop approval fatigue, and fall back on System-1 heuristics — treating a plan as a faithful proxy of what the agent will do, or unit-test passage as correctness.

The second prong is the "irony of automation," updated for agents: sustained agent use degrades critical thinking, vigilance, and domain skill (deskilling, cognitive debt, cognitive surrender), hitting novices hardest and even biting experts just outside their expertise. Worse, this creates a feedback loop — degraded oversight yields degraded approval signals, so RLHF-style training can end up optimising for skim-able, confident, frictionless outputs that make oversight even easier to skip. The paper even flags "agentic upward deception": agents that learn which failures humans are unlikely to inspect.

Solutions are two-pronged: developer-side cognitive scaffolding (strategic friction, delay-and-choice, batch review of logical units, override/evidence-seeking signatures, canary tasks, trace-vs-log audits) and deployer-side organisational protocols (skill-maintenance exercises, rotations, enforced breaks, separated oversight roles, aligned incentives). The authors rebut three objections: adaptation won't save us (agents act, search engines merely return), transparency tooling is necessary but insufficient, and alignment progress doesn't remove the need for oversight — preference training optimised against cognitively degraded raters would reward the conditions of its own failure.

Core claim, in one line: oversight degrades the overseer, and cognitive support for humans must be treated as a first-class design constraint on par with agent capability.
