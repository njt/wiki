Adrian Cockcroft — cloud architect of Netflix's trailblazing AWS migration, later a VP at Amazon, now advising Nubank — gave a Craft 2025 keynote on platform engineering in three parts: what platform engineering actually is, how to evolve your stack (via Wardley mapping), and how to make it work in practice (the Netflix versioning machinery). The transcript is preceded in the gist by a structured digest with key points, quotes, tools, and a candid list of what the talk omits.

Four principles anchor the first section. You never build "a platform" — you build a layered stack of platforms (deploy platform on virtualization/networking on language platforms on OSes on hardware), each with different knowledge, tooling, and ownership. Platforms evolve by accumulating functionality upward while sedimenting commoditized pieces downward, so keep them thin and keep refactoring. Treat every platform as a product, with an explicitly hired product manager — or the line manager doing product management if the team is small. And internal and external platforms obey opposite physics: Netflix's TV-firmware API cannot break for a decade, while its internal platforms changed so fast they were "messy," with many incompatible versions running at once.

The vendor argument is the talk's sharpest edge. Vendors externalize their interfaces — so their incentive is "to fatten their layer so they can charge you more" — the exact wrong incentive for a thin, fast-evolving platform. Netflix preferred open source and contracted vendors to support it (nginx's first customer — "we actually paid the guy to start the company"), and ran a thin, fast-changing internal layer over a stable AWS base, retiring hand-built components as AWS shipped its own (their homegrown KMS died the day AWS shipped KMS). Platform migration is argued out on a Wardley map anchored on user need: "If somebody says, well, the problem we're having to solve is installing Kubernetes, that's the wrong answer."

The Nubank material proves the model at extreme scale: 118M customers, thousands of Clojure microservices on Datomic/DynamoDB ("the largest customers of DynamoDB on AWS's platform"), ~1,000 deploys per day, and core banking running on Kubernetes on spot-market ARM chips — "if you're a traditional banker, your head explodes somewhere in that sentence." Kat Swetel's Doomsday Clock prioritizes platform work: each platform product manager must know when their component will stop working and how long the fix takes; sort the portfolio by that and clear the top of the pile. Cockcroft's own work there is observability (VictoriaMetrics + Grafana replacing Splunk) and what he calls controllability — "You can observe what's going on. Can you control your system? If you can control your system, it's reliable" — chaos testing, game days, and incident management as the reliability function.

The most technical stretch recycles Netflix's 2010–2014 versioning machinery (slides copied from 2016): immutable, additive deploys (zero production risk until traffic routes to the new version), version-aware traffic routing as the fundamental primitive — "the thing we built in 2010, which a lot of people still aren't using today, which confuses me" — an interface.feature.bugfix version scheme, the client library (not the wire protocol) as the interface ("the disk device driver, not the file system"), facets (different object models for client, server, and cache to break accidental coupling across service boundaries), version pinning with change-one-thing-at-a-time, and garbage collection for versions — deploy is synchronous, cleanup asynchronous, either incremental or the quarterly "Breaking Build." The wisdom is deliberately non-perfect: "There is no perfect way to do versioning. There's just some less bad options," and "If you want things to look tidy, then you will find it is very hard to evolve."

The final ten minutes pivot to AI: a persona (Supra) trained on his published answers — "You can replace me with a shell script that says, we did that at Netflix 15 years ago" — Megpt on GitHub for building such personas from a published-content CSV, and vibe-coding workload-analysis tools in Cursor: "I just have to chat to my little Claude that sits in cursor and it writes the code for me." In Q&A he endorses Deming's supplier principle via John Willis: pick suppliers and go deep, keiretsu-style — Netflix made AWS better and AWS "took very, very good care of us." The gist's own digest is unsparing about the gaps: the title promises team structures but delivers platform structures, the advice is frozen in 2014 ("I left Netflix in 2014, so what they do now, I don't know"), Nubank's regulatory story is a punchline rather than a topic, and platform adoption metrics are entirely absent.
