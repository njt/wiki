Anthropic's research team interviewed 66 people across 58 enterprise customers about how businesses are actually adopting AI. The headline findings: value comes mostly from increased output rather than cost cuts; outright AI-driven layoffs are rare (two companies of 58) but reduced hiring is widespread (two-thirds of companies) and contractor cuts, especially offshore, are common; and the highest-value deployments require real organizational reorganization, not just tooling.

The report structures adoption into three strategies. **Top-down automation** — identifying a workflow and automating it — produces fragile "zombie" apps, resistance from the experts forced to build their own replacements ("I'm essentially building my own coffin"), and surveillance concerns (managers reviewing AI-generated per-employee throughput reports daily). **Bottom-up experimentation** empowers nontechnical domain experts to vibe-code internal apps, but stays trapped in pockets: single-task tools, maintenance burdens, "Claude grenades" flying across organizational politics. **Reorganization around AI** is the highest-value path: broadening roles to own whole workflows, redeploying employees toward bottleneck tasks (code review, taste, requirements, customer contact), and building shared infrastructure — especially company-wide data access — so nontechnical builders can ship safely.

The recurring mechanism is Paul David's electrified-mill analogy: individual contributors can't restructure the division of labor, so productivity gains stall where they can't reach. And because models improve too fast for fixed plans ("You wait three months, and something new you didn't think AI could do is now possible"), the durable strategy is scaffolding for role change rather than any particular workflow design.
