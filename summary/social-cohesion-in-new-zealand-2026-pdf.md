The Helen Clark Foundation's second annual social cohesion survey (2,882 New Zealand adults, Nov–Dec 2025, modelled on Australia's Scanlon Foundation monitor) finds cohesion drifting lower on every dimension year-on-year: trust in government fell to 39% (its lowest recorded), belief that elections are fair dropped 6 points to 51%, perceived opportunity fell 6 points, and neighbourhood "real say" fell 4 points. Yet strong pillars remain — 84% belonging, 81% pride, and 80% faith that democracy is a good way to govern.

The report's signature finding is a clustering analysis of 5,513 responses identifying "Three New Zealands": the Connected (30%, comfortable and settled but civically disengaged), the Ambivalent (41%, the policy-critical middle whose cohesion moves with economic conditions), and the Alienated (28%, disconnected from institutions but actively engaged in oppositional ways — protesting, posting online). Nearly half of Māori and Pasifika sit in the alienated group, as do majorities of both Green and NZ First voters — opposing tribes sharing the same structural position.

Five insights structure the analysis: young Kiwis are the most aspirational despite the worst lived experience; subjective financial stress is the dominant predictor of cohesion (self-described "poor" respondents score 14.4 points lower on the cohesion index); isolation shifts participation rather than reducing it (isolated people protest and post more, vote less — a "re-affiliation mechanism"); intergroup contact reduces prejudice, echoing Allport against Putnam; and social media use is near-universal (56% use it multiple times daily) with no clean causal link to outcomes, though X and Reddit heavy users stand out for low institutional trust. A UK companion study (Box 2) warns against well-funded but short-cycle, output-measuring cohesion programmes, and recommends funding continuity, evaluating relationships rather than events, sharing authority with communities, and aligning economic policy with cohesion goals.
