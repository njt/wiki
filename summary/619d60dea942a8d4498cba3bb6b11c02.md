Rich Hickey (author of Clojure) argues that the word "tool" is being misapplied to LLMs, and that the misapplication hides something important. A tool, in his definition, is something you *wield*: held in the hand, transmitting your force, under your continuous intention and direction, and building your expertise through use. Tools are predictable, reliable, deterministic and bounded.

LLMs fail every one of those tests. You don't wield an LLM — you make requests and it acts on its own. Hickey's verdict: "LLMs are automation" — like a fast-food drive-thru, which is not a tool for cooking but "automation for avoiding cooking." LLMs are not tools with which you write code; they are "automation with which you avoid writing code, to which you abdicate writing code."

The cost of abdication is what you stop learning. Using automation means divining and gaming the automaton's current behaviour — he likens prompt tweaking to "filling voodoo.md with just the right incantations" — and one software update can render it all obsolete. Delegating reading or writing means missing how to formulate arguments, notice things, build judgment about authors, and "learning how to think."

He closes by rejecting the soothing "they're just tools, you make of them what you will" framing: automation has intrinsic properties, and ignoring the tool/automation distinction is done "at your peril." The gist preserves a reader's dissenting reply (Aaron), who argues AI is a "functional foundry set" — tools that machine other tools — elevating the user organizationally into a director of tool-users.
