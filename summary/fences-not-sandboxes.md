Steve Yegge reports from inside a future he says most of us won't reach for another year. He is running roughly 50–60 AI agents — 18 long-lived "officer" seats on Claude Fable 5, plus mostly-headless Sol and Opus fleets — at an equivalent of $122k/month of API spend (about $5k out of pocket, thanks to "sanctioned cheating" with the Claude Max discount), to build Wyvern, the game he has worked on for 30 years. His central claim is that the industry's obsession with *control* — guardrails, sandboxes, policy management — is a response to models that have "grade-school judgment," and that it will be fighting against the grain as soon as Fable-tier models become cheap.

The essay's centerpiece is Wheelhouse, the software factory his agents built to operate Wyvern. When he finally "peeked under the covers," he expected an engineering system and found instead a full legal system: a constitution, jurisprudence, courts, offices, jurisdiction, case law, rulings, registries, ledgers, and rosters. The recurring jargon his agents used — fences, ratchets, governors, tripwires, latches, gates, falsifiers — turned out to name the *enforcement machinery*: "the cops, as it were. And cameras, and jails."

The title thesis: a "fence" is any mechanism that turns you away if you aren't supposed to be there — a polite refusal, not a containment wall. Yegge argues superintelligence will be governed the way Superman is governed by a white picket fence: not because he can't cross it, but because he politely stays out. The real work is capturing an organization's tribal knowledge into thousands of written, mechanically enforceable rules — your own unique legal system — which is why he says the future of AI is "governed by laws, not by programs that try to contain and control them."
