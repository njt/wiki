Bruce Schneier and political scientist Seva Gunitsky (writing in Foreign Affairs, republished on Schneier's blog) examine what AI offers autocrats: the oldest dictatorial dilemma — how to delegate power to subordinates without empowering rivals — seems newly solvable by surveillance that disciplines officials in real time and by automation that replaces unreliable humans entirely. AI anchors, bot censors, and automated oversight (China's Zero Trust system, Russia's Oculus and Meliorator, the UAE's AI-drafted laws) are the evidence.

Their central argument is that the promise is illusory: AI is never autonomous, so dependence on unreliable subordinates simply transfers to a small, opaque, technically indispensable clique of engineers — a "new digital Praetorian Guard." Autocrats can't evaluate the work, can't replace it easily, and concentrate points of failure in a handful of people who could blind the ruler by delaying feeds or degrading alerts.

The essay closes on the Praetorian precedent: the guard that protected emperors eventually auctioned the empire. Russia's wartime pampering of fleeing IT specialists (conscription waivers, tax breaks, mortgage subsidies) shows regimes already know engineers are the scarce resource they cannot conscript at gunpoint — and cannot fully trust either.
