# How to Scale Intent, Quality, and Artistry with AI (Katie Dill, Stripe)

Stripe's head of design Katie Dill, speaking at a Lenny's Podcast event, draws an analogy between the post-WWII building boom and today's AI building boom: when urgency and new tools outpace intentionality, styles get copied without thought and the result is "zombie buildings" — and, by extension, "zombie UI."

She names three watch points: LLMs give you the probable (the popular, the past), not the original or context-specific; AI makes things feel done prematurely (the "microwave burrito" — enamored by speed, overlooking serious flaws); and easy building makes work feel disposable, unowned, unmaintained.

Her four recommendations: (1) have a point of view — if you don't, AI gives you its generic one; define your brand and standards, and apply them regardless of how the work was made ("it doesn't matter to the users how it was made"). (2) Encode your standards into the machine — the design system's object is no longer the screen but the system itself; Stripe's first MCP over design docs produced three different results for the same prompt, so they rebuilt it as a CLI harness with full opinionated templates and flows, not just components. She cites Gutenberg's 290 characters as the historical model of a system opinionated enough to feel handmade while machine-made: the old system scaled consistency, the new one must scale *intent*. (3) Refuse to confuse done with good — the quality filter that used to run throughout development now must happen post-build, which makes the editor role crucial; experience the product as a user would, push things to full formation, and sweat the details AI doesn't (the "Pepsi bubbling" crit produced 17 bullet points of craft improvements). She tells the story of a designer iterating an AI-assisted opening animation 56 times — AI opened the possibility space, but human scrutiny closed the gap. (4) Unleash creativity and artistry — chats, charts, and CLIs are not the epitome of interaction; the best practices haven't been written yet. Improve inputs with brand-specific prompts and source material, stress outputs, use adversarial agents as critics, and protect room for the strange. "AI can help us raise the ceiling, not just the floor."

She closes with Ruskin on Gothic architecture — no two columns alike, each showing the hand of the maker — as the counter-image to the zombie building: users are impressed by anticipated needs and care in the details, not by the tooling used to build it.
