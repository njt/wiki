A second ytx-gist transcription of Joseph Pelrine's Craft 2025 talk "CRM — The Real Secret to High-Performing Teams." The gist carries a structured summary plus the full talk transcript; the transcript is byte-identical to the sibling gist already in the wiki ([[Crew Resource Management — Joseph Pelrine (Craft 2025)]], raw/7a244cff090307fefc55d6ad285bc8d0) — only the auto-generated digest differs. Pelrine is a psychologist, one of agile's pioneers (he ran the first Scrum Master training for SAP in Budapest twenty years ago and shared a Munich flat with Kent Beck during Europe's first XP project), and now works as a psychologist at a Swiss Alpine hospital where every employee, himself included, is a certified emergency responder.

The argument: technical skills make a high-performing individual; non-technical skills make a high-performing team. From "hardcore psychological research" he names three failure modes — human-factor errors, fixation errors, and communication errors — and demonstrates cognitive overload live with a Stroop test, quoting error-rate research of roughly one mistake every 30 minutes on routine unstressed tasks, every 5 minutes on complex unstressed ones, and every 30 seconds under stress. Overload produces cognitive tunneling, tunneling produces fixation errors ("keep doing more of the same" — he cites Google research that developers debug the wrong thing 50% of the time), and communication errors do the rest. The fix is Crew Resource Management, NASA-originated ground rules for action and interaction that let technical skill survive even "when the shit hits the fan," adopted one habit at a time with explicit sunset dates and reviewed in retrospectives.

The talk's central reframe: psychological safety is not a prerequisite for high performance but a result of it — ground rules build trust, and psychological safety emerges in the culture, which is "the first derivative of a change in behavior over time," a derivative property you cannot change directly (Lewin's B = f(P,E): change the environment, not the person). On this reading Extreme Programming already was CRM — a set of interaction ground rules, not a technical-skills spec — and his best XP team, under stress, turned the practices "up to 11 or 12" instead of letting them slide. Error philosophy is James Reason's: the person-centered view produces fear and punishment, while the systems approach treats "human error is a consequence and not a cause" and asks how to make it systematically more difficult for errors to become incidents (Swiss cheese model). The toolkit maps failure mode to countermeasure: fixation → the 10-for-10 principle and Lufthansa's FORDEC loop ("essentially doing scrum on medicine"); communication → closed-loop protocols (SBAR, challenge-response, kitchen order-repeat); human factors → cheap systemic barriers (the Swiss QR-coded prescription chain adds "about a minute" and closes the error path); all built on McGrath & Larson's four team functions.

What this digest adds over the sibling gist is a sharper, thirteen-item "holes in the cheese" catalogue: no software-team evidence and unsourced statistics (70% of production problems from human factors); adaptation of the 15 rules left as homework; the self-organization contradiction (he ridicules "pseudo-agile self-organizing teams" while having taught Scrum); the SAFe question dodged on stage; hiring raised in the opening and then abandoned; the psychological-safety literature dismissed rather than rebutted, plus the bootstrapping problem — CRM demands people speak up, which itself requires some baseline safety to start; failed barriers not metabolized (the reflective reindeer antlers "worked for a while" until drivers adapted); and nothing on blame cultures, remote teams, enforcement, or measurement. It also preserves quotes the first digest missed: "There is no yellow triangle in the middle," the Einstein-definition-of-insanity joke, the eBay alcohol budget, and the closing teacher anecdote about what a question is.
