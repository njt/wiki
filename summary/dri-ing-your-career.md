# DRI'ing Your Career

A ytx gist carrying a structured digest plus a full machine transcript of a conference talk on owning your career when you cannot rely on having a good manager. The moderator introduces the speaker as "Kate Houston," who "leads client engineering at DuckDuckGo," "is the author of Engineering Leader," and works as a coach — a bio that matches Cate Huston (the transcript garbles names throughout), so the source is treated here as Cate Huston. The gist's own digest is unusually honest: it ends with an eleven-item "What the Talk Leaves Open" section.

The frame is raccoons. Pampered Instagram raccoons (Melanie on a trike, Piper the painter, Tamo with his own crypto) stand in for tech workers in the fat years, when managers handed out feedback, support, and opportunities; Toronto's garbage-bin-defeating strays stand in for now — "we live in Toronto now." The reframe is deliberate use of the power you do have: optimize the arc of the career, not the current job — "expect more from your career and less from your current job" — and set proximate objectives that pass a four-part control test (challenges you but feels possible, teaches you something, you want it, within your control). "Get promoted" fails the test, because if somebody else has to give you the goal, "what is your work in that?"

The toolkit: opportunities are planted, not just received — side writing, CFPs, onboarding and documentation work, hard problems — then triaged as roses (take them), tulips (decide consciously; she already has enough), and dandelions ("that's for somebody else"); low-value "token women invitations" become "negotiation practice"; a written plan pairs a specific-but-broad what with a why, resources, supporters, and opportunities. The "raccoon squad" (manager, skip-level, peers, mentors and sponsors, coaches, work BFF) replaces the fantasy that one manager meets all your needs. Coachability = receptiveness × actionability, with a five-step plan (build self-awareness, get a broader perspective, shed defensiveness, admit screw-ups first, ask for advice instead of feedback — "people much prefer to give advice than feedback. Who knew?") and feedback triage: information to improve with, information about the giver, or the bin. Bad feedback ignores broader context, negates your input, or undermines rather than helps you succeed — the Netflix standard (direct feedback must help the person succeed) is the usable kernel.

On leaving: quit from power, not from confusion; run an annual quitting review (she credits it, plus organizational change, with her five-year tenure); watch five signals — you've stopped learning, you're building elaborate coping mechanisms, you're morally conflicted about hiring ("if you think other people deserve better, then it's possible that you do too"), your confidence is shrinking, your health is affected. And interview regularly: market data either fuels negotiation or reassures you that you have "a good deal under capitalism."

The digest's closing audit names what the talk skips: negotiation mechanics, no method for when you don't know what you want, how to build a squad from zero, the spare-time privilege baked into "planting flowers," systemic forces (layoffs, visas, and AI's reshaping of software work — never named in a careers talk for developers) versus individual agency, and the unresolved promotion/pay tension the Q&A exposes.
