Abraham Flexner's classic 1939 Harper's essay (published by the Institute for Advanced Study, which he founded) argues that curiosity-driven, apparently "useless" research is the true source of the most useful technology. Radio traces to Maxwell's and Hertz's disinterested mathematics and physics, not to Marconi's last technical detail; electricity to Faraday's indifference to utility; relativity to Gauss's unpublished non-Euclidean geometry; bacteriology to a seventeen-year-old Ehrlich "just fooling" with dyes.

Flexner's claim is not that everything useless turns useful — he explicitly refuses that justification. His point is deeper: the criterion of usefulness, applied at the moment of research, would have strangled every one of these discoveries. Institutions should therefore be organised around unhampered curiosity, tolerating waste as the necessary price. He closes with a portrait of the Institute for Advanced Study — no duties, only opportunities; no committees; a paradise for scholars — and a warning, written as fascism closed over Europe, that the real enemy of the human race is the man who tries to mold the human spirit.

The essay is also a founding document: it doubles as the manifesto and the founding story of the IAS itself, crediting Hitler for Einstein, Weyl, and von Neumann arriving in Princeton.
