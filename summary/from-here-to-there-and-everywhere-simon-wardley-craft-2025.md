Simon Wardley's Craft 2025 keynote (Budapest), transcribed from YouTube and preserved as a ytx gist holding a structured summary plus the full talk transcript. It opens with a confession: as CEO of Fotango (16 lines of business, ~10M users) he wrote strategy by copying other companies and "changing a few words" — "Our CEO was completely clueless... I know this for a fact because I was the CEO." A Charing Cross bookseller sold him two translations of Sun Tzu, whose five factors (purpose, landscape, heavens/climatic patterns, doctrine, leadership) overlaid onto Boyd's OODA loop gave him the first picture of what strategy actually is: observe, then orient, decide, act. The observation tool is a map — anchor (the user), position (a chain of needs), and consistency of movement (the evolution axis: genesis → custom-built → product → commodity). Mind maps, system diagrams, SWOT diagrams and customer journey maps are all graphs, not maps: no anchor, no position, no movement. A SWOT of Thermopylae cannot tell Themistocles where to defend the Hot Gates; position and movement can.

From maps come patterns. Climatic patterns (~30) are the economic rules of the game: everything evolves; past success breeds inertia (Blockbuster was first with a website, online ordering and streaming experiments — "Blockbuster out innovated everyone. That's not what killed them. Inertia did."); each industrialisation triggers co-evolution of practice (EC2 → DevOps, serverless → FinOps) and a Jevons-paradox explosion of higher-order systems; and competitors' efficiency leaves you no choice over evolution — "with cloud it was never a question of if, it only was a question of when," repeated verbatim for AI. Doctrine (~40) is universally useful: know your users then focus on user needs, in that order; know the details of the supply chain. Gameplay (~150) manipulates the market — AWS's Innovate-Leverage-Commoditise play is documented in 17 pages of his mapping in *Reaching Cloud Velocity*. Method must match evolution stage: XP/agile in-house on the uncharted left, lean in the middle, Six Sigma or outsourcing on the industrialised right — proven at HS2 (delivered under budget, praised by the Public Accounts Committee) and the NASA/Planet Labs Carbon Mapper satellite, and the reason most outsourcing contracts fail: they specify the un-specifiable. A 15-minute mapping session killed a multimillion-pound robotics project at an insurance company whose "bottleneck" existed only because non-standard racks required servers to be physically modified — "These people weren't daft, they were trapped by past narrative."

The AI application runs the same pattern. Conversational programming (one designer being the machine) was demonstrated at AWS re:Invent in 2018 by Alexander Simovich; in 2024 we call it Copilot, vibe coding, whatever. The practices are still unsettled, "a bit like DevOps before Andy and Patrick gave it the DevOps moniker," and the Jevons explosion is coming: your VP will vibe-code a financial reporting system and demand it goes to production on Monday. The load-bearing claim: architectural diagrams and ERDs are "wishes, intents and beliefs" — "The code is the architecture... The real decision maker and architect was always the coder." So vibe coding is not a tooling choice but an architectural one: you are handing decision-making to the AI, and "the most important architectural question today" is where you value humans in the decision-making process — map the system, outsource what you don't care about, vibe-code prototypes, do software engineering plus AI with code review where it matters, and revisit the boundaries as agentic AI takes on more of the checks and balances. "Architecture is an expression of values."

The talk's engineering thesis: coding is a craft; testing is engineering. Tests are contextual, composable micro tools — hypothesis-driven, systematically exploring the problem domain, generating their own information (nobody paints in the red/yellow/green), so "the best specification of a system is the test suite." Coding uses one monolithic "kitchen blender" everywhere, explores ad hoc, decides on gut feel, and does manual inspection — while over 50% of the development budget goes to reading code, "the single most costly thing we do in development, we never discuss." The fix is to learn from testing: stop using monoliths, build composable micro tools for the problem at hand — build the tool first, then solve the problem. The demo case is Tudor/feenk's legacy-translation story: 10–20 person-years of failed effort, a CIO betting on failure, and "we're finished" at the four-week meeting because "we spent the first three weeks building the tool to solve the problem and then we solved the problem."

The closing (voted for by the audience) is sovereignty: tools, medium and language control how you reason, so AI concentration creates "new theocracies" — the counter is openness including training data, diversity of sources, and critical thinking. Values get baked into training data (the trolley problem; the "gold member" route-priority scheme that embeds social inequality into transport until a flood leaves the poor stranded — "The market is basically a bunch of idiots"; the DVLA saw this coming in 2015). Nation-states mostly have no map rooms for their technological and economic landscapes; China does — "You don't get into a supply chain war with somebody who actually understands the supply chain." Three walk-away messages: observe your landscape; treat where humans matter as the key architectural decision; turn coding from a craft into an engineering subject.
