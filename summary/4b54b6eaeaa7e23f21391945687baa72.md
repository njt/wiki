A ytx gist transcription of Charity Majors' (co-founder and CTO of Honeycomb) Craft 2025 talk in Budapest, billed in the agenda as "Build Boring Engineering Orgs" but pivoted mid-preparation to "In Praise of Normal Engineers." The boring-technology thread only resurfaces in the Q&A; the talk is really about org design, hiring, and the systems that shape engineers.

Majors' core move is to concede the 10x-engineer claim and then deflate it: yes, some engineers are dramatically more productive, but "so what" — teams own software, everyone shares the same delivery pipeline, and "if it takes the slowest engineer at your company five hours to ship one line of code, it's going to take the fastest engineer five hours to ship a single line of code." Superstar hiring ("top 10% of global talent" — she names Netflix and Coinbase) lets leaders off the hook: "any jackass can build an org where the best engineers in the world get shit done." The real competitive advantage is socio-technical systems where normal engineers convert ordinary effort into business momentum; a great org is defined by its floor, not its ceiling.

The prescriptions are a platform-engineering checklist: keep the deploy interval short ("deploys are the heartbeat of your company"), make it easy to do the right thing, have every engineer deploy and own their own code (feature flags, no dev/ops split), invest in observability because poor sense-making is "the dark matter of software engineering," staff an internal tools team with internal SLOs, treat learning as the norm, and treat diversity as operational resilience — "a monoculture can move faster than any other team" until someone gets sick or you hire outside the culture. Mixed-level teams keep everyone a newbie at something.

The deeper argument is about minting rather than renting talent: "zero babies were born good at algorithms. Great engineers are forged, they're made, they're not born." Excellence is learnable, situational, and specific to your business; hiring only pre-minted talent "reinforces and replicates all of the prejudices and inequities of the world at large" ("talent may be evenly distributed across populations. Opportunity is not"). She applies sociology's Fundamental Attribution Error to companies: "your company is a system. What kind of engineers are minted by your system?" Honeycomb's own proof: engineers who could not command FAANG salaries on arrival could "waltz right out the door tomorrow and make at least half a million dollars a year" a few years later — and mostly stay because the work is fulfilling.

The gist's own digest is unusually sharp about what the talk omits: no migration path out of the doom spiral she describes so well, no plan for de-risking an org already wrapped around a wizard engineer, no pay philosophy to match her skewering of top-10% hiring rhetoric, no answer for scale beyond Honeycomb's ~250 people, and — glaringly for a 2025 productivity talk — no mention of AI at all.
