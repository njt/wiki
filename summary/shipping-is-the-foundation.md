Sean Goedecke argues that every other engineering skill — design docs, meetings, tech-leading, process work — rests on the foundation of being able to ship. He borrows the "aggro" framing from competitive games (Dota 2, Magic: the Gathering, Starcraft): a maximally aggressive default strategy constrains the whole strategy space, because no clever plan survives losing to a rush. In a tech company, shipping is aggro: any elaborate coordination activity should be compared against the default of just sitting down and making the change yourself.

A leader who can't ship produces predictable dysfunctions: spending fifteen minutes estimating a five-minute task, proposing designs that are un-shippable for reasons codebase familiarity would have revealed, inflating estimates, blowing trivial asks into grand projects, and frustrating the engineers who *can* ship — which breeds covert "fixes" and distrust. For senior and staff engineers the stakes compound: high volume of requests only works if easy asks are delivered immediately, and the whole point of a manager's informal ask is to be the fast, illegible end-run around resourcing process.

On AI, Goedecke is skeptical that models make everyone a shipper. Shipping was never just writing code: it is finding the shortest pragmatic path to a (non-trivially defined) finish line, solving the little problems along the way, and keeping the right managers informed. LLMs help but can't run that end-to-end — it needs too much context about systems, people, and motivations. He credits Xiaoyu He's "Aggro is the Foundation" about mathematical research, and notes software engineering is itself a kind of research.
