A ytx gist holding the transcript and AI-generated digest of Sander Hoogendoorn's Craft 2025 talk on how his 13-person e-commerce team works. He calls it "mostly successful" because a serious outage during the company's biggest quarterly sale — extended to three days — was still being repaired the morning he spoke. The seven-habit structure itself was generated by ChatGPT from summaries of his previous talks, then he reverse-engineered a talk to fit it.

The diagnosis is "technical death": his 20-year-old deal-site company ended up with an ERP eight versions behind, user accounts stored in the CMS ("CMS systems do not contain user accounts"), every data-syncing mechanism imaginable, and microservices "with all sorts of weird names" on every cloud — so the team spends all its time keeping software running and none innovating. The cause is organizational, not technical: "it's never the tech... it's always the organization" — managers, product managers, and marketing demanding the next feature while refusing time to refactor. Monoliths are "okay-ish" and legacy can be beautiful (the Pantheon has functioned for 2,000 years). The innovator's dilemma compounds it: a team frozen in its stack cannot reinvent itself when a newcomer with better tech and a better business model arrives.

The habits: (1) prioritize pragmatically — a mission you can actually build software toward, a 70/20/10 budget (70% escaping the legacy mess, 20% small features on production systems capped at two-to-five days, 10% keeping the lights on), and a cross-functional Tech Board that kills ideas with four questions (goals? feasible? small enough? now?) and reprioritizes nearly weekly; (2) kill complexity — Cynefin puts most real work in the complex/chaotic zones where estimates are fantasy, so the only way out of a live system is experiments allowed to fail, and "if you think your steps are small, make them smaller"; (3) own the work — the team, not the individual, owns software (quoting Charity Majors), and autonomy is created by removing rules (Dee Hock): no Scrum, sprints, retros, stories, estimates, product owner, or pull requests — everyone becomes a "product engineer" who talks to the business directly; (4) communicate — event storming, pairing, mobs, weekly lean coffee, with knowledge hoarding treated as disqualifying; (5) microteams — fluid groups self-form per board item, ship, and disband; (6) deliver continuously — 40–50 production releases a day, because "missing the bus is not a big deal if you know there'll be another one along in five minutes"; (7) have fun — but motivation comes from purpose, mastery, and autonomy (Pink's model), not outings. Change starts with you: "your old keys will not open new doors."

Caveats he supplies himself: he cut his prepared AI section entirely, leaving one line — "everything seems to look faster; that doesn't mean you go faster." The digest flags what the talk never addresses: how the C-suite was persuaded to fund the model, what quality gate replaces pull requests, how a no-retros team learns from the very outage he opened with, the "Someday/maybe" column that is officially revisited quarterly but never actually is, and that success is never defined beyond release frequency. His PowerPoint crashed mid-talk and he kept going without slides — an unplanned demonstration of his own thesis that failure at the worst moment is survivable if you keep moving.
