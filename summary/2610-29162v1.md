An arXiv psycholinguistics study testing whether frontier LLM output overuses linguistic constructions known to cause human comprehension difficulty — object relative clauses, long syntactic dependencies, stacked noun compounds, denominal verbs ("footnote it"), inanimate subjects with animate verbs, and low-frequency n-grams.

Experiment 1 builds a novel corpus of AI text across chat, coding, and essay settings from 14 models (Claude Sonnet 5/Opus 5/Opus 5.5/Fable 5, GPT-5.6 Luna/Terra/Sol, GLM 5, Kimi K2.5, Olmo and Llama variants), comparing against genre-matched human corpora (OpenSubtitles, StackExchange, PERSUADE 2.0 essays, Wikipedia). A round-trip translation paradigm (English→Japanese→English via NLLB-200) holds meaning fixed while replacing wording, so any feature that drops off after translation is one the model chose over an available alternative. Result: frontier models overuse rare n-grams, denominal verbs, inanimate subjects, and reduced object relatives relative to paraphrase; all nine frontier/near-frontier models exceed human baselines on inanimate subjects and low-frequency n-grams. Effects are smallest in single-prompt essays and largest in iterated chat and coding sessions. Base and instruct open models (Llama, Olmo base/instruct) track humans closely; the reasoning-trained Olmo Think patterns with the frontier models. Notably, Claude Opus 5.5 scores lower than Opus 5 on 19 of 21 difficulty measures.

Experiment 2 tests the causal hypothesis: post-training for math/code/reasoning under verifiable rewards pressures models toward information density, not communicative success. Fine-tuning Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with GRPO under a per-token length penalty makes the model categorically prefer the shorter member of syntactic alternations (dropping optional "that", dative shift), and the compression preference transfers to constructions never seen in training. The paper's framing: human language is shaped by lossy working memory and incremental processing; RLHF still rewards verbosity, but the move to RLVR removes humans from the loop and lets models converge on a dense "dialect" that is optimal for the model but out-of-distribution for human readers. Remedies include rewarding effective communication directly — newer models already show this is quantifiable.
