Larcombe and Bierwirth (2026) analyse two decades of NHANES blood-chemistry data from the U.S. population and find a steady, population-level rise in serum bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) that closely tracks the rise in atmospheric CO₂. Over the same period (1999–2020), serum calcium and phosphorus both declined.

The authors argue that these shifts are consistent with chronic CO₂ retention: the extra CO₂ inhaled from ambient air is not fully cleared by ventilation because breathing rate is regulated by blood pH, which the bicarbonate buffer system keeps stable. The body stores the surplus CO₂ instead, and the measured blood-chemistry trends are the signature of that accumulation.

Linear extrapolation of the trends puts venous HCO₃⁻ at the upper bound of the healthy range by ~2076, with calcium and phosphorus hitting their lower healthy limits later this century. The paper reviews evidence that even modestly elevated CO₂—levels already common indoors (1,000–3,000 ppm)—produces oxidative stress, cognitive decline, anxiety, tissue calcification, and kidney damage in animal models and human studies. Indoor exposure compounds the problem: Americans average ~87% of their time in enclosed spaces.

The central claim is that atmospheric CO₂ is not only a climate problem but a direct, near-term physiological threat. The authors call for urgent emissions reduction, noting that human physiology evolved under ~280 ppm CO₂ and may have little margin above current levels.
