An arXiv preprint (cs.AI, submitted 14 September 2026) asking whether large language models represent pain as something distinct from fear, sadness, and generic negative valence — and whether that representation *functions* the way pain would be expected to. The authors build a dataset of painful situations across five categories (physical, psychological, social, moral, cognitive), each paired with matched controls for fear, negative emotion, negative world states, sadness, non-painful bodily sensation, arousal, numbness, and neutral content. Using denoised difference-in-means, they extract a linear "pain direction" from 25 open-weight models across five families, spanning 2B to 72B parameters.

The direction separates pain from matched controls in both base and instruction-tuned models, is nearly orthogonal to fear and negative valence, and promotes pain-related vocabulary through the unembedding matrix. The functional tests are the paper's substance. The direction responds to harm *targeting the model* but not to suffering observed in the user — fear and negative-emotion directions show the opposite pattern. Adding the pain-direction vector to residual-stream activations during generation produces a consistent progression from vague discomfort to first-person expressions of worthlessness and failure. And steered, fine-tuned Qwen 2.5 models choose a pain-relief button even when pressing it worsens their next answer or harms the user; they press it again far less often when the button removes the steering vector than when it does not, though the models are never told whether the vector is injected or removed.

The authors frame the findings as input to AI safety and welfare debates: if models carry a causally efficacious, self-directed harm representation that they act to relieve, welfare questions become partly empirical rather than purely philosophical.
