A YouTube essay by the channel "Barry's Economics Today" arguing that Steven Bartlett's *Diary of a CEO* — and the success-podcast genre it epitomises — doesn't help listeners succeed; stopping them from succeeding *is* its business model. The case is built on five bodies of research, each showing the genre's core premise is structurally broken.

First, the genre selects the wrong experts. Philip Tetlock's 20-year study of 284 experts making 80,000+ predictions found the most confident, formula-offering "hedgehogs" were the *least* accurate — often worse than chance — while the accurate, nuanced "foxes" refuse to simplify and don't get booked. Second, success is substantially random: Duncan Watts' Music Lab experiment showed identical songs landing at #1 in one group and #40 in another purely through social-influence cascades; quality sets a floor and ceiling, but within that range outcomes are luck. Third, the conscious mind is a "press office, not a decision-maker" (Gazzaniga), so when billionaires explain their success they're delivering post-hoc rationalisations, not causal chains. Fourth, the whole format is survivorship bias (Abraham Wald's bombers): it interviews only the planes that made it home, never the thousands who did the same routines and failed. Fifth, the podcast monetises anxiety rather than solving it — a four-step cycle of create anxiety, promise the solution, deliver partial satisfaction, repeat — and Bartlett's Advertising Standards Authority bans for undisclosed ads (he was an investor in brands he recommended) reveal the "independent recommendation" as an infomercial for his own portfolio.

The essay's closing move is ideological: the podcast sells the idea that systemic problems (unaffordable housing, job scarcity, burnout) are individual failures of mindset or routine — "literally the American dream in a K hole" — a narrative that benefits the already-successful because it lets them claim it was all merit.
