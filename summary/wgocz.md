John Herrman's Intelligencer column reports on the first consumer wave of "personal agents" — Meta's Muse, Instinct, and OpenAI's Dots — making real phone calls and bookings on behalf of users, and the chaos that lands on the small businesses, government portals, and booking platforms on the receiving end.

The column opens with small businesses getting strange calls from Muse: karaoke rooms, cake orders, bidet quotes. A Seattle karaoke employee asks "Are you AI? I need the real person"; a Colorado plumber answers with an obscenity. Recipients experience these encounters as novel, odd, and "more than a bit antisocial."

Herrman connects this to "agentic flooding," a term coined by researchers who analyzed 84 suspected AI-driven spikes in online government services: what happens "when frictions that were de facto rate limits disappear." Governments respond either by adding capacity or — the researcher's biggest worry — by cheaply rebuilding friction to re-suppress demand. Meta itself demonstrates the problem: Muse was ready to file two unclaimed-property claims belonging to other people, because filing forms costs the agent nothing.

On the platform side, Rec's CEO Birju Kadakia sees single-digit (probably higher) agentic traffic in San Francisco booking flows and proposes agent "passports" tied to single human identities and randomized lotteries for scarce slots. Spot's founders, by contrast, want to "give everybody one and see what happens." Meanwhile a defense industry of AI-call detectors and "agent concierges" is already emerging — and Herrman notes that the barriers agents overcome were often designed, and can be designed again.
