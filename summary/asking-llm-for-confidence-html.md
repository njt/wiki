A short, sharp essay arguing that a confidence score generated by an LLM is not a measurement of anything. The author builds up what a trustworthy confidence score actually requires — it must reflect the evidence behind a prediction, and it must be calibrated against recorded outcomes on the same kind of question — then shows that an LLM's self-reported number has neither property.

The core observation is mechanistic: an LLM is autoregressive, so "0.85" is not read from a gauge inside the model, it is simply a plausible continuation of the text. Pretraining calibrated the model only on "what text comes next", never on whether its written confidence matches how often it is correct. The visible symptoms follow directly: the same question yields different numbers on retry, rewording moves the score ("Are you sure?" is not new information), and text already written ("This is clearly the right answer") determines the number that follows.

The constructive half of the post gives a five-step recipe for getting real confidence without asking the model: choose observable signals (did search find an article, do two sampled replies agree), start from the historical base rate, record every reply and its outcome, update the rule from grouped records, and keep checking calibration against the outcome table. The rule inherits determinism, evidence-dependence and calibration from its own data — and is only as good as the record, which someone must actually grade.
