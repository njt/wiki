A verbatim transcript plus a pre-written digest of a conference panel on probabilistic forecasting in software delivery. The method on trial is Monte Carlo simulation run over a team's own historical throughput data — no assumed parametric distribution, percentile-based delivery dates. Defending it: Daniel Vacanti (ProKanban.org, flow-metrics author) and Colleen Johnson (ProKanban.org CEO). Attacking it: Gaia Becheri (trained statistician, delivery/risk manager in insurance) and Nigel Turlow (lean consultant), with Dan North moderating — and repeatedly abandoning neutrality to argue his own "Monty Python Simulation" critique.

Four lines of attack emerge. Gaia's statistical strike: sampling your own data *is* assuming an empirical distribution, so "there is no distribution" is incoherent, and non-parametric statistics "only converge when you have enough data" — the field's "few weeks of data" claim doesn't survive. Nigel's lean dilemma: if the system is stable enough to model, ordinary lean averaging (cycle time, takt time, throughput) already works; if it isn't, the model is invalid — and the variation that forces forecasting comes from "management, red tape, bureaucracy," so interrogate why you need to forecast at all. North's misapplication charge: Monte Carlo is a population tool being used as a single-case oracle — "will this project finish by June?" is like asking about a single surgery outcome before the operation. And the communication problem, from Gaia's insurance world: percentiles don't survive the trip to the executive floor; forecasts become targets, and a falling confidence number gets answered with "why didn't you succeed," not "what changed?"

The defense is mostly operational rather than theoretical: reforecast daily ("every day that goes by is a new data point"), sample like-for-like periods, manage WIP rather than shuffle people, and frame "85% by June 15th" as "15% chance it's later" — an invitation to troubleshoot the system, not a promise. Vacanti's theoretical ground is Shewhart's definition of stability (data within accepted limits of variation) and Little's Law (stationary long-term averages). North's offered distinction — a system can be "super spiky in a predictable way," so predictable without stable — was dismissed rather than engaged, as was Gaia's conditional predictability (non-stationary but known dynamics, like seasonal holidays).

Vacanti himself delivered the verdict: "fundamentally where this panel has failed is before we can even have an argument, we have to agree what we're arguing about." Distribution, stability, predictability, and stationarity were all contested and none resolved. The digest candidly lists what went unanswered: the cold-start problem (what if the future doesn't look like the past — Vacanti's only reply was rhetorical questions about retirement and cancer), how much data is enough, scale and cross-team dependencies, the independence assumption, why 85%, and — strikingly — validation: an entire debate about assumptions with zero debate about evidence (no backtesting, no calibration). The commercial elephant (the defenders sell books, training, and certification) went unacknowledged.
