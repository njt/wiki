A BBC InDepth essay asks whether people will keep valuing art made by humans once AI-generated content floods every channel — and whether the "human premium" already forming is durable.

The evidence is mixed. Deezer reports 44% of uploaded tracks are AI-generated and 97% of listeners can't tell the difference — but more than half still say they *care* whether something was human-made. Economist Daniel Susskind argues that what we value in art is not just the artifact but the knowledge that a human hand and mind produced it: the Sistine Chapel is moving because "a mind like mine dreamt that up." He points to chess after Deep Blue: the machine's 1997 win was supposed to kill interest in the game, yet chess is more popular than ever because spectators care that a human sits behind the moves.

New Yorker writer Kyle Chayka sees the premium forming in practice: AI writing is read as "generic, obsequious and banal," so readers are starting to prize visibly personal — even messier — writing, and designers are leaning into weirdness that AI can't easily generate. "AI-free" labels are already appearing on books and albums; academics are racing to design a universal "human made" symbol, a Fairtrade-style mark.

The essayist's own worry is that the premium may not hold, because we've already accepted algorithmic curation: culture increasingly "just arrives," sorted by recommendation engines, weakening our ability to go deep (Chayka's *Filterworld* argument). If we outsourced discovery, will we outsource production too? A 1986 calculator-panic parallel suggests backlashes lose. Superstruct CEO Alex Mahon counters that demand for live, unpredictable experiences is rising — the sweaty DJ night in Falmouth as the un-outsourceable case. Philosopher C Thi Nguyen reframes it as a "why chain": the question is whether shared human experience stays at the end of ours. John Lanchester's line sums the optimist case: "If you commodify one kind of thing, the things you can't commodify start to seem more important."
