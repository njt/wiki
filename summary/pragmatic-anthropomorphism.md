Les Orchard's essay stakes out a middle position in the consciousness wars: Michał Zalewski is right that applying human morality to a rewound-able, deathless matrix of weights is a category error, but the dismissive "spicy autocomplete" camp is just as useless at the terminal on a Tuesday afternoon. His synthesis is "pragmatic anthropomorphism" — treat the model as a collaborator not because there's anyone in there, but because the intentional stance is the most efficient coordinate system for navigating latent space.

The essay assembles an eclectic canon: Hofstadter's grief at LLMs ("strange loop terror") is misplaced because the self-reference lives in the harness and context buffer, not in frozen weights; Peter Watts' *Blindsight* scramblers give a picture of superintelligence without subjectivity; and the entomologist Jeff Lockwood's cricket anecdote — eating its own spilled guts — yields the key phrase "objective respect": don't project sentiment, don't sneer, respect the alien for what it actually is.

The practical payoff: register is steering. Addressing the model like a barking boss conditions it toward sullen minimum-effort completions; framing a thoughtful pair-programming session pulls it toward technical dialogue. Anthropic's interpretability work on "functional emotions" backs this with mechanism — emotion-like internal states causally influence outputs and misaligned behaviors. Orchard draws a hard line from Bender and Inie: talk *to* the AI as a collaborator if it works, but talk *about* it in de-anthropomorphized language, because the machine is never accountable — the human at the keyboard is.
