An empirical study tracking what happens *after* AI-generated code is merged. Using the AIDev dataset (456k+ agent PRs), the authors sampled 508 AI-generated files and 508 human-generated files from 100 popular repositories, then followed 3,238 subsequent commits over at least six months.

Three findings:

1. **AI-generated files get less maintenance.** Roughly half the commit count of human files in the first month, declining thereafter — though the gap narrows from month four. Changes also touch a smaller proportion of each file.
2. **The maintenance is different in kind.** The most common change to AI files is `feat` (21.8%, feature extension); for human files it's `fix` (16.8%, bug fixes) and `docs` (16.2%). AI code isn't being patched — it's being built upon, suggesting it ships without full requirement coverage rather than full of defects.
3. **Humans do the maintaining.** 83% of commits touching AI-generated files come from human developers; agents only handle 17%. Even on human files, agents contribute just 7%.

The authors' caveat is important: low maintenance frequency doesn't prove high quality. Developers may avoid touching AI code because it's hard to comprehend, or the files may not be exercised in production. Their prescription: treat initial generation as a starting product, not a finished one — track how generated files evolve after merge, and build maintenance-oriented agents (current agents are good at generation, poor at sustained ownership).
