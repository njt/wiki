Howey reflects on the closing of two "lucky windows" for authors. The first — cheap digital publishing without gatekeepers — let him bypass agents and publishers when he started writing in 2009. The second — a stretch when publishing was easy but writing was still genuinely hard — he now estimates lasted barely ten years, roughly 2014 to 2024. With AI-generated prose now good enough to spark bidding wars and confound provenance, he argues both windows have shut for good.

The post was prompted by a news story: a debut novel about Nigerian cartels reportedly lost a $2.4 million advance after publishers raised AI concerns. Howey treats it as a Rorschach test — if the book was human-written, the accusations were devastatingly unfair; if it was AI-generated and still impressed professional editors, the era of human-exclusive literary prestige is already over.

He offers four predictions. Publishers will cave and sell lightly-edited AI-generated books. Most readers won't care about provenance any more than they cared about self-publishing stigma. A niche of machine-book enthusiasts will emerge, analogous to chess-engine fans. And every author will end up using AI tools — for research, editing, marketing, even drafting — himself included.

The heart of the essay is a distinction between writing for external rewards (money, attention, status) and writing for the love of thinking in words. The former is threatened by AI; the latter is untouched. Howey notes he now second-guesses his own prose, wondering if his signature em-dashes and comma splices read as bot-like — then reminds himself that the models sound like *him*, not the reverse. He closes by urging writers to do the thing for the love of it, and to let jealousy over who profits pass through rather than pool up.
