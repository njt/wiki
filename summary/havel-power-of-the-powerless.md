Václav Havel's 1978 essay analyzes how post-totalitarian systems in the Soviet bloc sustain themselves not through overt violence but through the willing participation of ordinary citizens in ideological rituals they don't believe in. Havel calls this "living within the lie."

His central example is the greengrocer who hangs a "Workers of the World, Unite!" sign in his shop window. The greengrocer doesn't believe the slogan; he displays it as a ritual of compliance that signals he will not disrupt the system. In doing so, he becomes part of the machinery that keeps it running — what Havel terms "auto-totality," where individuals are simultaneously instruments and victims.

The essay's key move is to argue that "living within the truth" — refusing to participate in these ideological rituals — is therefore a profoundly political act. From this Havel identifies a "hidden sphere" of authentic life: samizdat publishing, underground music, private education, and other parallel structures where people live by their own values. These pre-political spaces, not formal opposition programs, are where genuine resistance grows.

Havel calls for an "existential revolution" — a decentralized movement grounded in individual conscience, trust, and responsibility rather than institutional power. The aim is not to seize the state but to render it irrelevant through parallel structures of authentic life. The essay became a foundational text for Central European dissident movements; Polish Solidarity activist Zbigniew Bujak later described it as "the theoretical underpinnings" that sustained the Polish opposition.
