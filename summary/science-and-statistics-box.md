Box's 1976 R.A. Fisher Memorial Lecture argues that scientific progress comes from a motivated iteration between theory and practice — not from pure theory alone, nor from undirected data collection. He uses Fisher's decade at Rothamsted Experimental Station (1919–1927) to illustrate how a scientist who stays in close contact with real problems can drive rapid theoretical advance.

The paper lays out principles of good scientific method. Science is a feedback loop: tentative theory confronts practice, discrepancies induce modified theory, and the cycle repeats. This requires flexibility — the willingness to seek out and exploit error, especially one's own. It requires parsimony: since all models are wrong, the scientist should seek economical descriptions rather than elaborate ones. And it requires selective worrying: being alert to what is importantly wrong, not distracted by irrelevancies ("mice when there are tigers abroad").

On mathematics, Box draws a sharp line: pure mathematics deals in conditional statements ("given A, does B follow?") with no claim on real-world truth. Applied mathematics makes assumptions known to be false but useful nonetheless. A statistical technique cannot be known to work unless it is used; major advances come only through the theory-practice iteration.

The bulk of the paper traces Fisher's Rothamsted work through episodes: analyzing decades of Broadbalk wheat-yield records (leading to orthogonal polynomials, residual analysis, and distributed-lag models), a faulty potato-fertilizer experiment (producing the first complete ANOVA table and the principle of randomization), the tea-tasting lady (illustrating experimental design), and collaborations on cotton-yarn strength (extreme-value theory) and bacterial counts (nonlinear design). Each shows theory arising from, and returning to, practical problems. Fisher also introduced the sponsor/critic dual role in data analysis: the analyst first sponsors a model to select procedures, then switches to critic to apply diagnostic checks on the residuals.

Box coins two pathologies that arise when the feedback loop breaks. *Cookbookery* forces every problem into one or two routine techniques without thought to objectives or assumptions. *Mathematistry* develops theory for its own sake, never touching practice, often redefining the problem to fit a tractable mathematical form rather than solving it. Both sever the iteration that drives genuine advance.

The paper closes with a call to train statisticians as scientists — integrating teaching with project work where practical experience is gained — rather than producing theorem-provers or cookbook-users in isolation. Fisher, Box argues, was great precisely because he was simultaneously an applied statistician, a mathematical statistician, a data analyst, and a designer of investigations. "It is surely because he was all of these that he was much more than the sum of the parts."
