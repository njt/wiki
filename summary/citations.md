This is a Google Scholar citation record for the most-cited ontology how-to document ever written: Noy and McGuinness's Stanford technical report *Ontology Development 101* (KSL-01-05, SMI-2001-0880, March 2001). The record carries the opening of the paper's abstract and a citation count, not the full paper.

The excerpt defines an ontology in Gruber's classic formulation — "explicit formal specifications of the terms in the domain and relations among them" — and situates ontologies as a technology escaping AI labs onto "the desktops of domain experts." Written in 2001, it reads as a period piece of the Semantic Web moment: ontologies are already "common on the World-Wide Web" in the form of Yahoo!-style taxonomies and Amazon product categorizations; the W3C is standardizing RDF "to make [knowledge] understandable to electronic agents searching for information"; DARPA is extending RDF into DAML "aimed at facilitating agent interaction on the Web."

It also catalogs the era's flagship ontologies: standardized medical vocabularies (SNOMED, the UMLS semantic network) and the UNSPSC product-and-services ontology from the UN Development Program and Dun & Bradstreet. The through-line of the abstract is that ontologies exist so that machines — the paper says "agents" — can share and reason over structured knowledge. That is precisely the problem this wiki's agent-era sources keep rediscovering.
