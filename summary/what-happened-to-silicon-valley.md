Tim O'Reilly's Margaret Levi Lecture at Johns Hopkins is a 40-year retrospective on Silicon Valley's political economy and an argument about where AI fits in it. The Valley he signed up for in 1993 built the commercial web as a machine for pointing people at the best of what others had made — "harnessing collective intelligence" — where Google's organic search served users 94% of the time without monetization. That was value creation: companies won by solving real problems.

The turn came not from bad people but from the machine of shareholder capitalism — what Charlie Stross calls a "slow AI" whose objective function rewards shareholders only. O'Reilly's analytical core is economic rents: "rising tide rents" (Schumpeterian rewards for innovation, often seeded by government) are lawful, but companies then build moats to keep profits after innovation stops, converting them to "robber baron rents" — extraction, which Cory Doctorow named enshittification. He adds Jerry Goodman's "supermoney": financialized companies live in a betting economy where a dollar of Tesla profit was worth $600 of stock value, versus $1 in the operating economy, and the AI buildout is the largest such bet yet — data centers financed against future demand from startups that haven't found product-market fit.

His conclusion is not doomerism but mechanism design. AI "skipped its youth" — enshittified from the start by extractive training-data enclosure and capital demands that force value capture before value creation. Yet MCP shows the alternative emerging, just as Unix accidentally built "an architecture of participation." The question he poses — "What game ought we to be playing?" — gets the answer "the game of human flourishing," requiring a circulatory economy, open protocols, and measuring success by diffusion, not capability.
