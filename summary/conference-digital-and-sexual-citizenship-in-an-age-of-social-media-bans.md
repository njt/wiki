Conference notes from an ECU Ethical Digital Futures Group event on Australia's under-16 social media ban, held after the ban took effect. Tama Leaver's keynote frames the ban as a textbook moral panic and "regulatory theatre": built on Jonathan Haidt's *The Anxious Generation*, amplified by the ad-company-backed 36 Months campaign and News Corp (motivated by the News Media Bargaining Code collapse), and resting on evidence that researchers like Candice Odgers show is far muddier than politicians claimed. The exemption framework meant to push platforms toward age-appropriate design was traded away in last-minute ALP–Liberal negotiations; age verification relies on a stacked "waterfall" of estimation tools; 85% of young people reportedly still use the banned platforms; and no platform has been fined — while the government doubled potential fines and quietly widened the legal definition of "social media" to capture recommender systems and endless feeds.

Three further panels deepen the critique. Dobson, Coffey and Hawker's selfie research shows young people as sharp critics of their own image editing — distinguishing "structural edits" (body reshaping, "breaking the rules") from acceptable "touch ups" — and traces a technological shift from overlay AR filters to generative AI tools like TikTok's EffectHouse and Meta's in-housing of filter creation, which removes public oversight entirely. John Sutcliffe offers a rights-based critique of "digital paternalism", using the beach-safety analogy: we don't ban the beach, we educate. Leila Green notes the money spent on the ban might have gone to the kids actually most at risk (Indigenous, rural, queer — not the white middle-class families Haidt centred), since youth suicide numbers are falling, not rising. Alan McKee argues the ban's goals are deliberately vague and that shame-based discourse — mirrored from pornography research — stops young people from talking to adults when things go wrong.

The throughline: protection rhetoric displacing participation, consultation, and evidence; and young people treated as objects of policy rather than citizens of digital space.
