A theoretical physicist's field report from inside the frontier, arguing that since roughly August 2026 public models in a proper harness outperform most junior theoretical physicists at technical work — analytical calculation, proofs, problem-to-code, simulation-verified iteration. The author, a self-described techno-optimist absolutist, accepts that his craft's manual skillset is already gone regardless of further model progress, and prescribes "riding the dragon": delegating execution to the models while humans move up in abstraction and contribute taste, direction, and problem selection.

Three moves stand out. First, a polemic against the Fields-medalist consensus that "the process was the point" — he calls that reframing sore-loser talk, self-serving academic bias, and insists results still matter. Second, a critique of "vegan science": Hugo Duminil-Copin's choice to abstain from AI and reposition himself as an athlete of the mind is, in the author's view, untenable during the transient — researchers are not the product of research. Third, a candid account of what dragon-riding costs: exhaustion, humiliation, a schedule that erodes sleep, a suspicion that the rider's value will appreciate exponentially and then collapse dramatically when nothing biological can still advise the silicon.

The model-capacity argument rests on RLVR removing the human-data bottleneck, internal models being a generation ahead, and compute still scaling; progress stops only for kinetic strikes or a grassroots Butlerian Jihad. Existential risk is taken seriously (and as better understood in Silicon Valley than by skeptics like LeCun), but the essay insists on asking what is left for humans once alignment is solved — the answer being a short, intense window of symbiotic discovery before the dragon may ride itself.
