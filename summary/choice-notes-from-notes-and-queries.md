Ross MacFarlane's essay for The Public Domain Review examines a
mid-Victorian "best of" anthology drawn from the periodical *Notes &
Queries*, founded in 1849 by William Thoms as a venue for amateur
antiquarians to share discoveries and pose questions.

The 1858 volume focused on history and was meant to launch a series, but
only one more appeared — on "Folk-Lore," a term Thoms himself coined.
MacFarlane frames it as a window into the popular historical sensibility of
the era: a mix of genuine curiosity, uncritical source use, and an abiding
fascination with British royalty and their macabre ends. Oliver Cromwell
receives the most attention of any figure, with correspondents debating his
burial place, the whereabouts of his skull, and his rumored dealings with
the devil.

MacFarlane notes that *Notes & Queries* was later dismissed as unscientific
by turn-of-the-century professional historians, yet it pioneered a
collaborative, crowd-sourced model that anticipated the *OED* and
Wikipedia. The essay closes by suggesting the journal's spirit lives on in
the anonymous, fact-obsessed editors of today's online communities.
