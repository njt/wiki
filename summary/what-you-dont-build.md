Liam Nugent's essay on the two features he has tried to take "a hard pass" on in consumer-facing financial services apps: the document hub and the notifications centre. Both answer a real problem — legacy systems that emit PDFs, compliance's "persistent communications channels," a stakeholder wanting a bell icon — and both grow into monsters under one-more-thing pressure: "By the end you've essentially rebuilt Google Drive," or "a bad Gmail clone." His diagnosis is organisational, not technical: both features let the organisation off the hook at the expense of the user, and the build is only the beginning of a maintenance bill paid release cycle after release cycle.

What actually kills these platforms isn't argument but the bill: "illustrating the running costs. Not the build cost. The running costs, laid out over years." Then the underlying need gets met the boring way — each use case on its own merits, something simple or something that already exists, even if imperfect. The harder half is removal, and Nugent grounds it in evidence rather than taste: Gerry McGovern's long argument that culture rewards production while review, maintenance and removal are seen as lesser work, his Top Tasks finding that stripping 80–90% of a site's content improved sales and cut support calls, and Klotz and colleagues in Nature showing people systematically overlook subtractive changes even when removal is obviously better.

The agent-era coda inverts the obvious reading of DHH's celebration of endless execution: if building is nearly free, the temptation is to make more stuff — but Nugent thinks the smarter use of agents is the pruning and the maintenance, "to remove things judiciously, and more carefully than anyone is going to manage by hand." He closes on Steve Jobs' 1997 product matrix — not four products forever, but focus that pooled engineering talent instead of spreading it thin — and a confession that doubles as the thesis: "I'd have written a shorter post, but I couldn't work out what to take out."
