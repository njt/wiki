Steph Ango asks a single question — what creates an edge, an advantage, a moat in a competitive landscape? — and answers it with a taxonomy of eighty strategies, each described in a sentence and illustrated with examples drawn from both biology and business.

His framing sentence: successful things "optimize competency on a narrow set of strategies, often a unique and unusual combination of strategies." The unit of analysis is deliberately broad — "organisms, people, products, organizations, networks" — all of which "overcome competitors and accumulate energy by developing strengths along certain vectors."

The eighty strategies are grouped into thirteen categories. **Accumulation** (usership, completeness, aggregation, diversification, omnipresence, cloning) and **Price** (affordability, luxury, skimming, bundling) cover the classic business-school moats. **Time** (heritage, craftsmanship, organic, endurance) and **Uniqueness** (specialization, versatility, hybrid, divergence, authenticity, rarity, scarcity, secrecy, irreverence) cover edges that can't be bought quickly. **Offense** (violence, litigation, nettlesomeness, sabotage, parasitism, scavenging, espionage, swarming, highest bidder, chokepoint, puppeteering) and **Defense** (deterrence, reliability, predictability, unpredictability, decentralization, security, privacy, durability, neutrality, obscurity, antifragility) span the aggressive and protective poles. **Deception** (camouflage, mimicry, decoy, lure, infiltration), **Timing** (first-mover, second-mover, last-mover), and **Accreditation** (monopoly, prestige, curation) are narrower but sharp. **Collaboration** (union, alliance, emergence, centralization, standardization, symbiosis, herding, distributed ownership, transparency), **Speed and scale** (iteration, efficiency, agility, precision, blitzing, composability, modularity), **Ease** (intuitiveness, fun, simplicity, low-friction, charm), and **Transformation** (malleability, metamorphosis, copycat) round it out.

The piece is deliberately value-neutral and cross-domain: predators and patent trolls sit in the same category as Coca-Cola and coral reefs. The implicit argument is that a moat is any durable asymmetry along one of these vectors — and that the winning combinations are often strange hybrids that no single competitor can copy.
