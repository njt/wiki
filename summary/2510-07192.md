This arXiv paper (2510.07192, submitted 8 Oct 2025) makes the first demonstration that data-poisoning attacks on LLM pretraining need only a **near-constant number of poisoned documents** regardless of dataset or model size. The accepted assumption — that adversaries control some *percentage* of the training corpus, so bigger corpora mean bigger attacks — is wrong in the direction that matters: bigger corpora mean nothing, because the absolute attack budget does not grow.

The evidence is scale. The team ran the largest pretraining poisoning experiments to date, pretraining models from 600M to 13B parameters on chinchilla-optimal datasets from 6B to 260B tokens. **250 poisoned documents** compromised models at every size, even though the largest models trained on over 20× more clean data than the smallest. Smaller-scale ablations covered broader poisoned-to-clean ratios and non-random placement of poison samples. The same dynamics hold for fine-tuning-stage poisoning.

The implication is uncomfortable: injecting backdoors into large models may be *easier* than previously believed, since the required poison count does not scale with model size. The authors call for substantially more research on defences. For anyone reasoning about trust in models trained on web-scraped or agentic-collected data, the practical reading is that dataset size is no comfort, and per-document vetting of a 260B-token corpus is infeasible — the threat model shifts from "bulk adversary" to "small, well-placed adversary."
