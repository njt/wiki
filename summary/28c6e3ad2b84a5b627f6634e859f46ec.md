Adam Conover's video essay argues that the disappearance of the sitcom is structural, not cultural: it is the result of the streaming business model, not cancel culture or shifting taste. The demand is measurable — sitcoms are only 1.7% of new shows this decade, yet The Big Bang Theory (ended 2019) was still top-10 streamed in May 2026, and sitcoms drive 13% of Hulu's subscriber retention from just 7% of its catalog.

Three changes broke what Conover calls the "comedy factory." Short season orders (8–10 episodes) force dramatic arcs because there is no time to simply hang with characters — and time is the essential ingredient, since first seasons are awkward until writers learn their characters. Separating writing from production destroyed the apprenticeship that let the Cheers creators accumulate thousands of episodes of craft. Outsourcing shoots overseas severed the writer–audience feedback loop: the live studio audience was a real-time joke-testing instrument, and "comedy is not just a monologue. It's a conversation between the writer and the audience."

His thesis compresses to "the process is the product" — shows were funny because of how they were made. He closes on the social stakes: sitcoms model how very different people come to love each other, exactly what a lonely, polarized era needs. The précis notes the economics are asserted rather than quantified, and no counterargument is seriously engaged.
