A ytx gist transcription (structured summary + full transcript) of Joseph Pelrine's Craft 2025 talk "Crew Resource Management (CRM): The real secret to high-performing teams". Pelrine is a psychologist and one of the pioneers of agile methods — he ran the first Scrum Master training for SAP in Budapest twenty years ago and shared a flat with Kent Beck while doing Europe's first Extreme Programming project; he now works as a psychologist at a Swiss mountain hospital and advises executives. His thesis: high performance is an environment problem, not a talent problem. Stack up skilled individuals and "they act like a bunch of idiots" (his running example: the Swiss national football team). Like functional versus non-functional requirements, teams need technical *and* non-technical skills — and it is the non-technical ones that make the team perform. HR keeps hiring for technical skill because it cannot test for team fit, so Pelrine's answer is to stop trying to hire better people and instead build an environment in which ordinary people perform.

The environment fails in three ways documented by psychological research: human-factor errors (cognitive overload), fixation errors, and communication errors. He demoed overload live with a Stroop test — "95% of people make mistakes. The other 5% are liars" — and cited error-rate research of roughly one mistake every 30 minutes on routine unstressed tasks, every 5 minutes on complex unstressed ones, every 30 seconds under stress. Overload produces cognitive tunneling, tunneling produces fixation ("keep doing more of the same" — Google research says developers debug the wrong thing half the time), and communication errors do the rest. The fix is Crew Resource Management: NASA-originated ground rules for non-technical team interaction, proven in aviation and medicine, which Pelrine has applied in helicopter rescue, with Lufthansa, Formula 1 (Ferrari), and Swiss champion football. CRM is "a set of ground rules for action and interaction among the people in a team that give the team the ability to do their technical skills as well as possible — even when the shit hits the fan."

The provocative move is inverting the psychological-safety dogma: everyone recites psychological safety as the *prerequisite* for high performance; CRM treats it as the *result*. Via Lewin's B = f(P,E), you change the environment, not the person; ground rules build trust (an interpersonal construct); psychological safety then emerges in the culture — and culture is "a derivative property" you cannot change directly. On this reading Extreme Programming already *is* CRM: a set of interaction ground rules, not a technical-skills spec. His best XP team, under stress, turned the practices "up to 11 or 12" instead of letting them slip, because trust meant nobody had to check anybody. The error philosophy is James Reason's: the person-centered view of error ("bad people") produces fear and punishment, while the systems approach treats human error as a consequence, not a cause, and asks not "how do we get better" but "how can we make it systematically more difficult for these errors to become an incident?" Blaming is easier, which is why most organizations choose it. Countermeasures match failure mode: fixation → the 10-for-10 principle and Lufthansa's FOR-DEC decision loop ("essentially doing scrum on medicine"); communication → closed-loop protocols (SBAR, challenge-response, kitchen order-repeat); human factors → systemic barriers (his Swiss pharmacy's QR-code-and-second-person verification adds about a minute and closes the error path).

The gist's own digest is unusually strong and includes a "holes in the Swiss cheese" section that catalogs the talk's omissions: no method for hiring non-technical skills; unsourced statistics (the 70% human-factors claim, Google's 50% wrong-debugging figure); no software-adapted set of the 15 rules ("set up for medicine"); the psychological-safety inversion asserted rather than demonstrated; a punt on what to do when the leader isn't recognized; SAFe explicitly dodged; remote and distributed teams completely absent; no adoption cost or metrics (Lufthansa invests one to two weeks of simulation per crew per year); and no engagement with existing software-industry practice — Google's Project Aristotle, SRE blameless postmortems — which already occupies this ground. The promised story about his high-stress XP team ("I don't have the time to talk about it") never gets told.
