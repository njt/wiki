Virginia Postrel's history of Habitat, the first massively multiplayer virtual world, and the unlikely machine that made it possible. Habitat ran on the Commodore 64 — 64 kilobytes of memory, 100 kilobytes of disk, 300-baud modems — yet introduced almost every term and mechanic now taken for granted in online worlds: the word "avatar," in-game currency, paid cosmetics, player-to-player trade, and the first "dupe bug."

## The C64 and QuantumLink

The Commodore 64 (1982) sold for $595 against an Apple II at ~$1500, and its cheap chips — a stripped-down industrial controller CPU, a synthesizer sound chip, a game graphics chip — made it powerful *and* disposable enough to be a "game machine." Its 1986 refresh, the 64C, bundled QuantumLink (Q-Link), a telecom program reaching a dedicated network priced at $3/hour in 1986 versus CompuServe's $8 off-peak and $22.50 weekdays. Q-Link bought transmission capacity after business hours "for pennies on the dollar" and used then-exotic client-server architecture to serve more users at the same cost.

## Habitat's Firsts

Developed at Lucasfilm's games division, Habitat reached the public in 1989. It had no plot and no goal — "deliberately open ended and pluralistic," in creators Chip Morningstar and F. Randall Farmer's 1990 paper. It introduced avatars, 2,000 starting tokens with 100 more per daily login, player trade, avatar customization, ownable and decoratable houses, and vending machines (Vendroids and Pawn Machines). Players threw parties, published a newspaper ("The Rant"), and founded a church ("The Order of the Holy Walnut").

## Emergent Behavior and the First Exploit

Open-ended play produced unpredictable results: a treasure hunt that took weeks to build was solved in eight hours; players exploited a pricing error in the vending machines to quintuple Habitat's money supply overnight — the first "dupe bug," in Farmer's words. Spontaneous performing-arts groups synchronized over 300-baud latency using stopwatches and phone calls. Farmer and Morningstar learned to think like "rule makers" rather than traditional game designers.

## The Lesson

Habitat demonstrated, decades before social media, that in an online setting "people will do everything that can possibly be done — good, bad, evil, neutral, weird, crazy" (Alex Handy, Museum of Art and Digital Entertainment). Handy's coral-reef metaphor captures the core finding: "wonderfully delicious fish and super-poisonous fish" all come together; you don't get to choose. The piece ends with reader comments noting Farmer's later reputation-systems work (his book *Building Reputation Systems*) and the observation that consumers, not professional systems developers, led the move "from algorithms to social apps."
