A long Twitter synthesis by Nicolas Colin (Aug 30–31, 2026, with replies through Sep 4), prompted by Patrick Collison's "ritual quarterly complaint about German notaries." Colin's thesis: the notaries are a distraction — Germany's tech ecosystem is starved of capital for macroeconomic reasons.

The mechanism, traced in seven steps: German export surpluses plus Hartz-era wage repression plus the Schuldenbremse mean national savings pile up on corporate and government balance sheets, not households'. Citing Christensen, he argues that when corporations hold the keys to national savings they reinvest in manufacturing competitiveness, never in startups — the same pattern as Chinese SOEs, compensated for in China only by Big Tech conglomerates and "the CCP operating the entire nation like a venture capital fund." The US has the opposite setup — an "overflow mechanism" in which the trade deficit floods the country with foreign capital into safe assets, crowding domestic endowments and pension funds up the risk curve and into VC funds. The eurozone removes the exchange-rate rebalancing (a Deutsche Mark would have appreciated and shrunk the surpluses), locking the German pattern in place.

The reversal logic cuts both ways: Germany would need higher wages and unleashed consumption to attract capital, which its postwar identity makes unlikely; and by balance-of-payments accounting, the US cannot shrink its current-account deficit without mechanically shrinking the capital-account surplus that feeds its financial ecosystem — Colin reads Trump's world tour soliciting bilateral investment commitments as a clumsy attempt to replace the structural mechanism with ad hoc deals.

The replies sharpen the argument. David Rosskamp counters that the BoP diagnosis doesn't explain US VC's genesis — Stanford/DARPA clusters, option culture, Nasdaq, deep M&A, and decades of realized returns did — and Colin concedes the plumbing point while narrowing his claim to scale ("The plumbing exists, but nothing is flooding it"), proposing three falsifiable tests. Arvid Hallén raises Sweden (German-style surpluses, Europe's strongest VC cluster, household equity culture) as the anomaly the framework must eventually explain.
