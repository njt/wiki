James Shore's fourth essay in his series on assessing AI's impact on software development — and, as he says, the one that "isn't really about AI at all." It's about modeling and forecasting the *business value* of what software teams build, because ROI depends on what you deliver, not how fast you build it.

Core arguments:

- **Value, not cost.** There is no guaranteed relationship between software cost and value: "$20M of software that's worthless, $2M that transforms a business." Teams obsess over cost estimates and ignore value modeling because it's organizationally awkward, not technically hard.
- **The bottleneck moves to customers.** With AI making code cheap, Lee Eason's hypothesis is that customers' limited ability to *absorb* new features becomes the constraint. Expensive development used to force prioritization; now the most impactful work risks being shipped and simply ignored. Customer attention is the scarce resource.
- **The attribution problem.** Revenue is easy to see at the bottom line but hard to attribute to a specific feature (the sales promotion, the influencer, the partnerships push all contributed). Solution: model value with proxy metrics — Shore uses Dave McClure's Pirate Metrics (AARRR) — for faster feedback and to isolate other departments' effects.
- **Organizational process.** As VP of Engineering, Shore spent ~2 years standing up a "product bets" process: an NPV spreadsheet (new sales, upsell, retention, cost savings, expenditures, plus development costs) required for major initiatives, seeded by CEO/CFO sponsorship and peer relationships. Forecasting made people uncomfortable; CFO rigor and adversarial peer critique of each other's models fixed inflated estimates.
- **Useful, not true.** "All models are wrong, but some are useful." The model's value is surfacing hidden assumptions and strategy, enabling milestone-and-ceiling "Build-Measure-Learn" funding with continue/cut-losses decisions, providing accountability beyond delivery dates, and — the AI payoff — combining measured speed gains and consequence models to judge AI's actual productivity benefit.
- **Prioritization is strategy.** Software priorities determine what a company can do next quarter; ignoring value to chase cost estimates "unintentionally sabotages the company's strategy." This is why he calls it the most important essay in the series.

Caveats he names: models are gameable (watch for people hiding bad news), they're heavyweight (not for bug fixes), and they inform rather than replace strategic judgment.
