A ytx gist of Randy Shoup's Craft 2025 talk — ten years after his first Craft appearance — containing a structured summary (key points, pithy quotes, tools and practices, unanswered questions) plus the full transcript. Shoup was eBay's Chief Architect and then VP of Platform Engineering; Velocity was the engineering-transformation program he helped lead starting around 2020. His framing: "I could have called this talk... how we doubled engineering productivity at eBay but still didn't save the company."

The setup is a paradox. Early eBay was a genuine pioneer — database sharding, real-time search (2003), eventual consistency, distributed tracing, co-invention of feature flags, Kafka-style guaranteed messaging (2006) — and yet GMV has been flat since 2007 while US commerce grew 8x; eBay grew 1.5x. The diagnosis came from a four-stage value stream map (planning, development, delivery, post-release iteration) across ~4,500 applications and 4,000–5,000 engineers, with problems in every stage. Velocity deliberately attacked only the middle two stages, on the reasoning that "software delivery makes everything else possible by enabling faster change and reducing the cost of change." Using DORA metrics as the instrument, eBay moved from solid medium performer (deploys twice a month, 10-day lead time) to high performer (2–3 deploys/week, 2-day lead time) — the 35th to the 75th percentile. Deployment frequency and lead time improved 5x; change failure rate and recovery time improved 3x *without being targeted*, because continuous delivery capabilities improve speed and stability simultaneously. "Doubled engineering productivity" meant a same-size team delivering double the features and bug fixes.

The playbook is a textbook of transformation mechanics: think big, start small, learn fast; ~10% pilot domains chosen as a deliberate cross-section of the business ("no one could say, oh, that's going to work on the buying side, but not on the payment side"); parallel platform tracks; an embedding model of senior ICs sent "out into the provinces"; daily/weekly/monthly communication cadences; and the pivotal move — the impediment interview, where every team's blockers become the platform team's backlog: "You just gave my team our backlog." Platform as product, not platform as ivory tower: the old platform team would "produce software and hope that anybody would use it and then be grumbling when they didn't." Engineering wins included fully automated commit-to-deploy pipelines for 98–99% of applications, traffic mirroring as "black box testing at scale," contract/OpenAPI-driven interactions replacing the manual "partner sign-offs" ("the worst distributed coordination algorithm in the world"), blameless release halts, and cadence ratcheting of yearly site-wide upgrades down to monthly — "if it hurts, do it more often." Mobile went from monthly to weekly releases in eight months, after the release manager's "Randy, it can't be done."

Then the hard part. The wins were all in the delivery layer; the misses — daily deploys, small cheap experiments, rolling planning — were all in the layers owned by planning and culture. Three reasons: (1) strategy and planning — the Innovator's Dilemma, the "seller straitjacket" (fixing misspelled "iFones" destroyed sellers' arbitrage businesses), centralized annual waterfall planning with 50-team coordinated releases, and the feature factory, encapsulated by a 2004 VP celebrating "we delivered 5,000 train seats to the business this quarter... What she was saying and celebrating was we cost $60 million," and by Managed Payments: "That's $1.5 billion in personnel costs to learn how to take credit cards" — for slower seller payments than PayPal. (2) Technology dead ends — custom forks of OpenStack and Kubernetes, the proprietary MARCO framework, no public cloud. (3) Organizational culture, "the biggest factor" — Westrum's pathological type, a "culture of terror" in which one VP built a 700-person empire and "personally approved every single deployment... for an entire year." The evolutionary argument ties it together: "A flat business selects for, in an evolutionary way, selects for risk averse behavior" — fifteen years of flat GMV made caution rational at every level, so eBay's good baseline change-failure rate was a symptom of stagnation, not excellence. The thesis, extending Drucker: "culture eats strategy and product and technology and process and anything else you can think of for breakfast as well."

The gist's summary section closes with a sharp list of unanswered questions: the business case is never closed (the talk opens with flat GMV and ends with flat GMV, never connecting doubled productivity to a business outcome); the rising-tide confound (the whole industry was improving fast, and the 15 pilot teams "forced their way in" under CEO spotlight); no ROI for Velocity itself from a speaker who mocks train-seat accounting; "doubled productivity" defined but not measured; culture diagnosed with no treatment playbook; the terror VP anecdote left unexamined as a system ("Who did the culture actually protect?"); and — in a 2025 talk about doubling engineering productivity — no mention of AI at all.
