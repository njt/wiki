A six-month retrospective on running an autonomous software factory — issues in, code out, validation in the middle — that had merged roughly 1,700 pull requests by month four. The author's claim is that throughput was the least interesting outcome, and that what actually mattered was everything the loop did not ship: trust, calibrated actors, institutional memory, and intent.

Five lessons carry the piece. Throughput arrives before the instruments that would judge it exist, so early merge counts are "inventory, not velocity." Trust comes from structure, not prompting: fail-closed gates, adversarial review charges, and decision records enforced by mechanical conformance checks. Agents stop being scripts and become "seats" — named roles whose findings are themselves tracked and calibrated, because some reviewers agree with everything and that is a defect. Institutional memory, not context windows, is the unsolved half of the problem; the minimum viable version is decision records filed where the next run will look. And intent is the scarce input: when the author went quiet for two weeks, the factory kept running but optimized noise, revealing that the human seat is upstream — deciding what is worth wanting.

The lessons crystallize into a governance model the author calls PAAA: Purpose, Articles, Actors, Artifacts — a development organization in a box, with founding documents written one incident at a time.
