Zach Lloyd (CEO of Warp) argues the next paradigm after prompt-driven development is the "software factory": agents doing autonomous work across the whole software lifecycle, with all work public and measured by default. He concedes the metaphor is demoralising — "dark factories" imply engineers aren't needed, and public token-measured work feels like being a cog — but insists the shift is inevitable on productivity and cost grounds.

His answer is to redefine the engineer's job as two jobs: **product engineering** (prompting and steering agents, owning quality and taste — delegable work like crash fixes and dependency upgrades starts around 20–30% automated and climbs) and **factory engineering** (measuring the factory with DORA metrics and LLM-as-a-judge scorers, then improving skills, context, and model mix so the human-touch count keeps falling). Warp operationalises this by measuring "human touches per PR" and baking factory improvement into performance reviews.

The post is also marketing: Warp Factories is in early access with $10k of free usage for qualified companies, and Lloyd is describing his own product's worldview as an industry inevitability. Still, the two-jobs framing and the "public-by-default inner loop" observation are substantive contributions to how engineering orgs actually change.
